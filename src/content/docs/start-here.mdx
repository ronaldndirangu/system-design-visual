System design is the craft of choosing components (servers, caches, databases, queues) and wiring them together so a product
stays fast and correct as more people use it. This course teaches it by letting you **watch systems fail** and then fix them
with your own hands.

## Who it is for

- Developers who build features and want to understand what happens after their code ships.
- Students and career switchers preparing for system design interviews.
- Anyone who has heard "just add a cache" and wants to know when that advice is wrong.

You need basic programming knowledge. You do not need any cloud account, and nothing here asks you to sign up.

## How each lesson is built

Every lesson follows the same seven beats, so you always know where you are.

1. **Objectives.** A short list of what you can do by the end.
2. **The failure.** The system breaks in a specific way. You predict what happens before the answer appears.
3. **The idea.** A step by step diagram and an everyday analogy introduce the fix.
4. **The simulation.** A live model you can play, pause, step, and reset. Every result also appears as a table of numbers,
   so you can read it with a screen reader or with motion turned off.
5. **The trade-offs.** No fix is free. A table lists what you gain and what you pay.
6. **The quiz.** Each wrong answer comes with an explanation of why it is wrong.
7. **The challenge.** A short design problem that applies the idea somewhere new, with one possible answer to compare.

A lesson takes 20 to 25 minutes. Split it into two sittings if you like: read up to the simulation, then come back for the
quiz and challenge. A few topics are **quick cards** instead: five-minute reference pages, such as the design checklist,
that end with a short quiz.

## One running example

The first three modules grow a single product, a **URL shortener** like `sho.rt/abc123`, from a weekend project to a
service with 100 million users. Each lesson adds one component to the same diagram, so you can always see why it exists.

Later modules switch to examples that fit their problems better: a click analytics pipeline for queues and streams, a
shopping cart for consistency, and twelve case studies, each chosen for one constraint that dominates its design. The
AI systems module builds a customer support assistant.

## The simulations are honest models

The simulations use small, deterministic models: the same settings always produce the same result. They leave out many
real world details on purpose, but they never show you a behavior that real systems do not have. When a model simplifies
something important, the lesson says so.
