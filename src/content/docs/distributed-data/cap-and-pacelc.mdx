## The failure: a cart on both sides of a cut

An online store keeps each shopping cart in two datacenters, West and East, so every shopper gets a nearby answer and
the store survives the loss of a building. Sam shops on a phone near West and on a laptop near East. Every change crosses
the link between the datacenters, about 40 ms each way, so both copies of the cart stay the same.

Then a fiber gets cut between the two buildings. Both datacenters keep running, and both of Sam’s devices still reach
the nearest one. Only West and East can no longer talk. Sam taps **Add book** on the phone.

>

**Refuse, or accept and let the copies disagree.** West has no way to tell East about the book. If it accepts, the
laptop’s next read at East misses the book. If it refuses, the two copies stay identical, and the phone gets an error.
No rule avoids both, and no faster server or smarter code changes that while the link is down.

</Predict>

## The idea: the choice exists only during a partition

The CAP theorem names three properties of a replicated system:

- **Consistency:** every read sees the latest confirmed write, as if one copy existed. This is the linearizable promise
  from [consistency models](../consistency-models/).
- **Availability:** every request that reaches a working server gets a real answer instead of an error.
- **Partition tolerance:** the system keeps working when the network splits the servers into groups that cannot talk.

You often hear CAP as “pick any two of three”. **That reading is false.** Partitions are not optional: cables get cut,
switches fail, and a long pause looks the same as a dead link. A system spread over two buildings will meet a partition
whether it planned for one or not. What CAP says is narrower: **while a partition lasts**, each side must either refuse
some requests and stay consistent, or answer them all and risk serving data the other side has changed. When the
network works, CAP asks nothing of you.

<Analogy>

Two branches of a shop share one stock list and keep it in sync by phone. One day the phone line goes dead. A customer
at the north branch asks for the last lamp. The clerk can say “Sorry, I cannot check with the south branch, come back
later”, or sell the lamp and hope the south branch did not sell it too. When the line comes back, the clerks compare
notes. If both sold the lamp, someone gets an apology. Even on a normal day, the careful clerk who calls the other
branch before every sale serves customers more slowly than the clerk who trusts the local list.

</Analogy>

## PACELC: the cost the rest of the time

Partitions are rare. Most of the time the link works, and CAP says nothing about that time. PACELC fills the gap: **if
there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency.** A system that keeps one
agreed copy pays for it on every request, because a reader far from that copy waits for a round trip. A system that
answers from the nearest copy stays fast, and a reader sometimes sees a change late.

So a design gets two labels. The refuse lane in the simulation below is **PC/EC**: it refuses during the cut and waits
for East the rest of the time. The accept lane is **PA/EL**: it answers locally in both cases.

## Try it

so every difference comes from the rule the datacenters follow. In the **refuse** lane, East holds the only copy of the
cart, and West passes each phone tap to East. In the **accept** lane, each side keeps a copy, answers at once, and sends
each change across the link. All numbers come from this toy model with a fixed seed and are illustrative.

What to look for with **Link cut for 3 s** (the link is down from 1.5 s to 4.5 s):

- The refuse lane refuses 8 phone taps, each after a 300 ms wait, and shows 0 out-of-date reads. The laptop keeps
  working through East the whole time. Once the link returns, the phone’s add lamp at 4.71 s goes through in 85 ms.
- The accept lane answers all 28 taps in 5 ms, and 7 reads come back out of date. At 1.79 s the laptop sees a cart with
  only the scarf, without the book the phone added at 1.54 s. At 4.45 s the phone sees only the mug and the book,
  without the tea, pen, jam, and soap the laptop added.
- At 4.54 s both sides merge two versions, and **the scarf comes back**, though the phone removed it at 3.99 s. The
  laptop removes it again at 5.05 s, and both sides end with the same cart.

Then switch to **No cut**. Nothing is refused, yet the refuse lane’s average wait is 42 ms against 5 ms in the accept
lane, because every phone tap crosses to East and back. With **Far apart, no cut** the link takes 120 ms each way, and
the refuse lane’s average climbs to 116 ms while the accept lane stays at 5 ms. That gap is the else branch of PACELC.
The accept lane pays in the calm too: in both scenarios, the phone at 4.45 s reads a cart without the soap the laptop
added at 4.43 s.

## What real systems do

The widget is a toy, and real systems differ from it in a few important ways:

- **A consistent system keeps more than one copy.** Here East holds the only copy, so losing East loses the cart. Real
  PC systems replicate each write to a majority of servers and elect a leader, as in [Raft](../raft/). During a
  partition the majority side keeps working, and only the minority side refuses.
- **Merges need more than a union.** The accept lane keeps both versions and merges them with a union, so a removed
  item can come back. Amazon’s Dynamo paper reports exactly this for its shopping cart, and accepts it because a lost
  add costs more than a resurrected item. Better merges record removals: a tombstone marks an item as removed, and an
  observed-remove set, a kind of CRDT (conflict-free replicated data type), keeps the remove unless the other side added
  the item again after seeing it.
- **Many systems sit between the lanes.** [Quorums](../quorums/) let you choose how many copies each read and write
  must reach, which slides a store between PA/EL and PC/EC per request. [Read replicas](../../scaling/read-replicas/)
  are PA/EL for reads: they answer locally and lag behind the primary.
- **Availability in CAP is strict.** It means every working server answers every request. A system that answers 99.99%
  of requests can still choose consistency during a partition, so “CP” does not mean “often down”.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="During a partition, and the rest of the time">

| Choice                                 | What it buys                                                                         | What it costs                                                                                                   |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Refuse during a partition (PC)         | No out-of-date reads and no versions to merge; the copies never disagree.             | The cut-off side errors or waits: 8 of 28 taps refused in the run, each after 300 ms.                            |
| Accept during a partition (PA)         | Every tap answered in 5 ms on both sides, for as long as the cut lasts.               | Out-of-date reads (7 in the run) and conflicting versions; a weak merge brings a removed scarf back.             |
| Wait for the far copy when calm (EC)   | One agreed cart; a read never misses a confirmed change.                              | Every far request pays a round trip: 85 ms per phone tap on a 40 ms link, 245 ms on a 120 ms link.               |
| Answer locally when calm (EL)          | 5 ms answers at any distance.                                                         | Replication lag still causes stale reads, and edits within one link delay of each other still conflict.          |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

Your store runs in West and East. For each feature, choose PA or PC for a partition and EL or EC for the rest of the
time: the shopping cart, chat between shoppers and support, checkout (taking payment and selling the last unit in
stock), and the order history page.

<div slot="answer">

- **Shopping cart: PA/EL.** A refused add loses a sale. Accept adds on both sides, store the cart as an observed-remove
  set so removes stick, and settle any leftovers when the shopper checks out.
- **Chat: PA/EL, with causal order.** A message sent during a cut shows up on the other side when the link heals. Use
  causal consistency within a conversation so a reply never appears before its question, and show a “sending” mark
  until the far side confirms.
- **Checkout: PC/EC.** Selling the last unit twice or charging a card twice cannot be merged away. Send checkout to a
  majority-replicated store, show “try again” on the minority side during a cut, and pay the round trip on this one
  path.
- **Order history: PA/EL, plus read your writes.** A history page that lags a few seconds is fine, but a shopper who
  just ordered must see that order, so read it from the side that took it.
- **The pattern:** CAP and PACELC apply per operation. One product mixes both choices, and the strong ones stay on the
  few paths that need them.

</div>

</Challenge>
