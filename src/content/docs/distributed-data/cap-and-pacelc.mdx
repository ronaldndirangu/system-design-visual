## The failure: a cart on both sides of a cut

An online store keeps each shopping cart in two datacenters, West and East, for nearby answers and to survive losing a
building. Sam shops on a phone near West and a laptop near East. Every change crosses the link between them, about
40 ms each way, so both copies match.

Then a fiber between the buildings gets cut. Both datacenters keep running, and each device still reaches its nearest
one. Only West and East can no longer talk. Sam taps **Add book** on the phone.

## The idea: the choice exists only during a partition

The CAP theorem names three properties of a replicated system:

- **Consistency:** every read sees the latest confirmed write, as if one copy existed. This is the linearizable promise
  from [consistency models](../consistency-models/).
- **Availability:** every request to a working server gets a real answer instead of an error.
- **Partition tolerance:** the system keeps working when the network splits the servers into groups that cannot talk.

You often hear CAP as “pick any two of three”. **That reading is false.** Cables get cut and switches fail, so a
system spread over two buildings will meet a partition whether it planned for one or not. CAP says something narrower:
**while a partition lasts**, each side must either refuse some requests and stay consistent, or answer them all and
risk serving data the other side has changed. When the network works, CAP asks nothing of you.

more slowly.

Where it breaks: clerks can settle a double sale by talking later, while servers need a merge rule written in advance
and cannot tell a dead line from a slow one.

</Analogy>

## PACELC: the cost the rest of the time

Partitions are rare, and CAP says nothing about the rest of the time. PACELC fills the gap: **if there is a Partition,
choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency.** A system with one agreed copy makes every
far reader wait for a round trip. A system that answers from the nearest copy stays fast, and a
reader sometimes sees a change late.

So a design gets two labels. The refuse lane in the simulation below is **PC/EC**: it refuses during the cut and waits
for East the rest of the time. The accept lane is **PA/EL**: it answers locally in both cases.

the phone misses the soap the laptop added at 4.43 s.

## What real systems do

The widget is a toy. Here East holds the only copy, so losing East loses the cart. Real PC systems replicate each write
to a majority of servers and elect a leader, as in [Raft](../raft/). During a partition the majority side keeps
working, and only the minority side refuses. Real PA systems merge with rules that record removals, so a removed item
stays removed.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: merges that remember removals</summary>

The accept lane merges with a union, so a removed item can come back. Amazon’s Dynamo paper reports exactly this for its
shopping cart, and accepts it because a lost add costs more than a resurrected item. A tombstone marks an item as
removed. An observed-remove set, a kind of CRDT (conflict-free replicated data type), tags every add with a unique ID,
and a remove deletes only the add tags it has seen. An add made on the other side during the cut survives the merge,
every replica reaches the same answer, and a deliberate concurrent add wins.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: systems between the lanes, and strict availability</summary>

[Quorums](../quorums/) let you choose how many copies each read and write must reach, which slides a store between
PA/EL and PC/EC per request. [Read replicas](../../scaling/read-replicas/) are PA/EL for reads: they answer locally and
lag behind the primary.

Availability in CAP is strict: every working server answers every request. A system that answers 99.99% of requests
can still choose consistency during a partition, so “CP” does not mean “often down”.

</details>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="During a partition, and the rest of the time">

| Choice                               | What it buys                                        | What it costs                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Refuse during a partition (PC)       | No out-of-date reads and no versions to merge.      | The cut-off side errors or waits: 8 of 28 taps refused in the run, each after 300 ms.  |
| Accept during a partition (PA)       | Every tap answered in 5 ms on both sides.           | Out-of-date reads (7 in the run) and conflicting versions; a weak merge undoes a remove. |
| Wait for the far copy when calm (EC) | One agreed cart; no read misses a confirmed change. | Each far request pays a round trip: 85 ms on a 40 ms link, 245 ms on a 120 ms link.    |
| Answer locally when calm (EL)        | 5 ms answers at any distance.                       | Replication lag still causes stale reads, and edits within one link delay still conflict. |

</TradeoffTable>

Skip the second datacenter when one region meets your latency and survival needs, since one copy never faces this
choice. Skip the accept lane for anything a merge cannot undo, such as stock counts and payments.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating partition tolerance as optional.** Links fail whether you plan for it or not, so “pick any two” only
  hides the choice until the first cut.
- **Labeling a whole system CP or AP.** One product can accept cart adds and refuse checkout.
- **Merging with a plain union or last writer wins.** A union brings removed items back, and last writer wins drops
  the losing version’s changes.
- **Forgetting the cost when the network works.** One agreed copy charges every far request a round trip.
- **Assuming conflicts need a partition.** Edits within one link delay of each other conflict too.

<KeyTakeaways>

- During a partition, each side must refuse some requests or answer with possibly stale data.
- PACELC covers the rest of the time: one agreed copy costs a round trip, and a near copy can be stale.
- Accepting on both sides needs a merge rule that records removals.
- Choose per operation: accept where a merge can fix the result, refuse where it cannot.

</KeyTakeaways>

Your store runs in West and East. For each feature, choose PA or PC during a partition and EL or EC otherwise: the
shopping cart, support chat, checkout (payment and the last unit in stock), and the order history page.

<div slot="answer">

- **Shopping cart: PA/EL.** A refused add loses a sale. Accept adds on both sides and merge with a rule that records
  removals, such as an observed-remove set.
- **Chat: PA/EL, with causal order.** A message sent during a cut arrives when the link heals, and causal order keeps
  a reply after its question.
- **Checkout: PC/EC.** A double sale or double charge cannot be merged away. Use a majority-replicated store, show
  “try again” on the minority side, and pay the round trip on this one path.
- **Order history: PA/EL, plus read your writes.** A page that lags a few seconds is fine, but a shopper must see the
  order they just placed.
- **The pattern:** CAP and PACELC apply per operation, and strong choices stay on the few paths that need them.

</div>

</Challenge>
