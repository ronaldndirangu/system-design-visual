## The failure: a charge with nothing to ship

The online store has grown into separate services. Checking out a cart now touches three of them, and each owns its
own database: **Inventory** reserves the items, **Payments** charges the card, and **Shipping** books a courier.
Inside one database, a transaction makes a change all or nothing (the Transactions and constraints lesson covers how).
Nothing like that spans three databases.

You have two obvious options, and both hurt:

- **Lock everything.** Take a distributed lock, or run two-phase commit, so all three services commit together. Every
  service holds its locks until all of them agree. When Shipping stalls for two seconds, the stock rows and payment
  records stay locked, and every other checkout for the same items waits. Many outside services, such as card networks
  and courier companies, cannot join a two-phase commit at all.
- **Just call them in order.** Reserve, charge, book. That works until a step fails halfway.

## The idea: local steps, each with an undo

A **saga** splits one business action into a chain of local transactions. Each step commits in its own service right
away, so no service waits on another's locks. If a step fails, the saga runs a **compensation** for every step that
already finished, newest first. Releasing the stock undoes the reservation, and refunding the card undoes the charge.

A compensation is a new forward action, and it restores the business outcome without erasing history. The refund shows
up as a second line on the customer's statement. Some actions have no undo at all: a sent email stays sent. Put those
after the **pivot**, the last step that can fail. Here, booking the courier is the pivot: once it succeeds, the order
will complete.

## Try it

Someone has to decide which step runs next. **Orchestration** gives that job to one service, the orchestrator, which
sends a command to each service and records every saga's progress in a saga log. **Choreography** has no one in
charge: each service listens on an event bus, reacts to the previous service's event, and publishes its own.

Messages get lost, so both styles retry. Commit to a guess before you run the last scenario.

## Orchestration or choreography

With **orchestration**, the whole flow lives in one place. The orchestrator knows the order of steps and the order of
compensations, and its saga log answers "where is order 3?" with one lookup. In the toy, a happy checkout takes **600
ms** and the courier failure cancels order 3 after **1000 ms**. The price is one more service to run and keep
available, and an orchestrator can slowly turn into a central service that knows everyone's business.

With **choreography**, services stay loosely coupled. A new loyalty service can subscribe to the shipping event, and
no existing service changes. The price is that the flow lives in four codebases. In the toy, the extra bus hops make a
happy checkout take **800 ms** and the courier failure take **1200 ms**. A real broker adds only a few milliseconds per
hop, so the bigger cost is debugging: to trace one order, you collect events from every service. Many teams solve that
with a read model that listens to every event and keeps one status row per order, an idea the
[CQRS](../cqrs/) lesson builds on.

A common rule of thumb: choreography for short flows of two or three steps owned by different teams, and orchestration
once the flow has many steps, branches, or compensations you need to audit.

## A saga gives up isolation

A database transaction hides its work until it commits. A saga commits every step as it goes, so other readers see the
in-between states. In the courier failure, 1 item for order 3 was off sale for **800 ms** before Inventory released it.
When Payments declined order 2's card, 3 items were held for **400 ms**. A shopper who looked in those windows saw
less stock than the store really had to sell, and a shopper who read the ledger saw a charge that later disappeared
behind a refund. The [consistency models](../consistency-models/) lesson describes what readers can see in more
detail.

Designs that live with this:

- **Pending states.** Store a hold as "reserved", separate from "sold", and an order as "pending" until the saga ends.
  Readers who understand the flag can show "2 left, 1 on hold".
- **Undo by adding back.** Write compensations as increments, such as "add 1 to stock", instead of "set stock to 24".
  Two sagas that interleave then cannot overwrite each other.
- **Risky steps early, permanent steps last.** Put the steps most likely to fail early, so fewer compensations run.
  Put steps with no undo, such as emails, after the pivot.

## Retries need idempotent steps

A lost reply and a lost request look the same to the sender, so the orchestrator resends a command after a timeout,
and the bus redelivers an event that nobody acknowledged. Every step and every compensation can therefore run more
than once. Payments must key each charge by order id, see that order 2 is already charged, and return its first answer.
Refunds need the same guard, or a retried refund pays the customer back twice. The Idempotency lesson covers these
keys in depth.

Retries also cost time. With the lost reply, order 2 finishes in **1000 ms** under orchestration and **1200 ms** under
choreography, against 600 and 800 for the other orders. A compensation must never give up for good, either: keep
retrying a refund until it succeeds, and alert a person if it keeps failing.

One more trap: a service must save its change and publish its event together. If it crashes between the two, the saga
stalls forever. The usual fix writes the event to an outbox table in the same local transaction, and a relay publishes
it later. The Event-driven design lesson covers that pattern.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Coordinating a checkout across services">

| Choice                                | What it buys                                              | What it costs                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Distributed lock or two-phase commit  | All or nothing, and nobody sees in-between states         | Locks held across services, one stalled service blocks everyone, outside APIs cannot join |
| Saga with compensations               | Each service commits alone, with no cross-service locks   | No isolation, and a compensation to write and test for every step              |
| Orchestration                         | One place holds the flow and every saga's state           | One more service to run, and it can grow to know too much                       |
| Choreography                          | Loose coupling, and new listeners need no other changes   | The flow is spread across services, so tracing one order is hard                |
| Idempotent steps                      | Safe retries, with no double charges or double refunds    | A dedupe record per order or request, checked on every step                     |
| Pending states                        | Readers can tell held stock from sold stock               | Every reader must understand the pending flag                                   |

</TradeoffTable>
