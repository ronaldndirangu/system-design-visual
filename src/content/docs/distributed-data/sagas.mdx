## The failure: a charge with nothing to ship

Checkout touches three services, and each owns its database: **Inventory** reserves the items, **Payments** charges
the card, and **Shipping** books a courier. A transaction makes a change all or nothing inside one database (see
[Transactions and constraints](../../foundations/transactions/)), and none spans three.

Two-phase commit can make the change atomic, and it blocks: if its coordinator goes down after the services vote,
every service holds its locks and waits. Card networks and couriers cannot join it at all. So the team calls the
services in order: reserve, charge, book.

## The idea: local steps, each with an undo

A **saga** splits one business action into a chain of local transactions. Each step commits in its own service right
away, so no service waits on another's locks. If a step fails, the saga runs a **compensation** for every finished
step, newest first: refund the card, then release the stock. A compensation is a new forward action, so the refund
shows up as a second line on the customer's statement.

Some actions have no undo: a sent email stays sent. Put them after the **pivot**, the last step whose failure cancels
the order. Here, booking the courier is the pivot. After it, the saga can only go forward: it retries each later step
until it succeeds and never compensates it.

<Analogy>

You book a flight, then a hotel, then a rental car. The car company has nothing left, so you cancel the hotel and then
the flight. Nobody freezes all three companies while you decide. Where it breaks: a traveler can accept a cancellation
fee and move on, while a saga must retry each compensation until it fully succeeds.

</Analogy>

## Try it: one checkout, two ways to coordinate

Someone decides which step runs next. **Orchestration** gives that job to one service, the orchestrator, which sends
commands and records each saga's progress in a saga log. **Choreography** has no one in charge: each service reacts
to the previous service's event, as in [Event-driven design](../../async/event-driven-design/), and publishes its own.
Messages get lost, so both styles retry.

## Orchestration or choreography

With **orchestration**, the flow lives in one place. The orchestrator knows the order of steps and compensations, and
its saga log answers "where is order 3?" with one lookup. The price is one more service to run, and it can grow to know
everyone's business.

With **choreography**, services stay loosely coupled: a new loyalty service subscribes to the shipping event, and no
other service changes. The price is a flow spread across four codebases, so tracing one order means collecting events
from every service.

Rule of thumb: choreography for short flows of two or three steps, and orchestration once the flow has many steps,
branches, or compensations to audit.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: timing and tracing in the toy</summary>

In the toy, a happy checkout takes **600 ms** under orchestration and **800 ms** under choreography, and the courier
failure cancels order 3 after **1000 ms** and **1200 ms**. The extra bus hops cause the gap. A real broker adds only a
few milliseconds per hop, so debugging is the bigger cost. While a choreographed saga runs, the Orders service only
knows the order was placed. Many teams add a read model that listens to every event and keeps one status row per
order, an idea the [CQRS](../cqrs/) lesson builds on.

</details>

## Isolation and retries

A database transaction hides its work until it commits. A saga commits each step as it goes, so other readers see the
in-between states. In the courier failure, 1 item was off sale for **800 ms**. When Payments declined order 2's card,
3 items were held for **400 ms**. The [consistency models](../consistency-models/) lesson covers what readers can see.

Retries add a second hazard. A lost reply looks like a lost request, so commands get resent and events redelivered,
and any step or compensation can run twice. Payments must key each charge by order id and
return its first answer to a repeat, and refunds need the same guard. The
[Idempotency](../../reliability/idempotency/) lesson covers these keys. A compensation never gives up: keep retrying a
refund, and alert a person if it keeps failing.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: designs that live without isolation</summary>

- **Pending states.** Store a hold as "reserved", separate from "sold", and an order as "pending" until the saga ends.
  Readers who understand the flag can show "2 left, 1 on hold".
- **Undo by adding back.** Write compensations as increments, such as "add 1 to stock", instead of "set stock to 24".
  Two sagas that interleave then cannot overwrite each other.
- **Risky steps early, permanent steps last.** Put the steps most likely to fail early, so fewer compensations run.
  Put steps with no undo, such as emails, after the pivot.
- **Save and publish together.** If a service crashes between saving its change and publishing its event, the saga
  stalls forever. Write the event to an outbox table in the same local transaction, and let a relay publish it later.
- **Retries cost time.** With the lost reply, order 2 finishes in **1000 ms** under orchestration and **1200 ms** under
  choreography, against 600 and 800 for the other orders.

</details>

## Two-phase commit, and when to skip the saga

Two-phase commit makes one change all or nothing across several databases. A coordinator asks every participant to
prepare, then tells all of them to commit or abort. It **blocks** while the coordinator is down: a participant that
voted yes can neither commit nor abort alone, so it holds its locks and waits. A saga trades isolation for
availability: each step commits alone, and no service waits on another's locks.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: how two-phase commit runs and blocks</summary>

A coordinator runs two rounds:

1. **Prepare.** The coordinator sends each participant its part of the change. A participant that can commit writes
   the change and a yes vote to its own log, keeps its locks, and answers yes. A participant that cannot answers no.
2. **Commit.** If every vote is yes, the coordinator writes "commit" to its log and tells everyone. One no, or a vote
   that never arrives, makes it write "abort" instead. Each participant applies the decision and releases its locks.

A participant that voted yes is **in doubt** until the decision arrives. It promised to commit if told to, so it cannot
abort alone. It does not know what the others voted, so it cannot commit alone either. If the coordinator crashes
after the votes, every participant keeps its locks and waits until the coordinator recovers and reads its log. That
wait makes two-phase commit **blocking**. When Shipping stalls, the stock rows and payment records stay locked, and
every other checkout for the same items waits. Application servers run two-phase commit across databases and message
brokers through the XA standard, and operators sometimes have to resolve an in-doubt transaction by hand.

Distributed databases such as Google Spanner and CockroachDB still use two-phase commit, and they shrink the wait.
Each participant, and the record that holds the decision, is a replicated group that runs consensus (Paxos in Spanner,
Raft in CockroachDB; the [Raft](../raft/) lesson shows how). When a machine dies, a new leader takes over with the
votes and the decision already on its disk, so participants wait seconds for an election instead of waiting for a
repair. The price is extra round trips to replicate every vote and decision, and every table must live in that one
database.

A distributed lock gives no such promise either: it stops two callers from running at once, and it cannot undo a
charge that Payments already committed.

</details>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Coordinating a checkout across services">

| Choice                                     | What it buys                                            | What it costs                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Two-phase commit                           | All or nothing across services                          | Locks held across services, a down coordinator blocks everyone, outside APIs cannot join |
| One database with distributed transactions | All or nothing with isolation, and no compensations     | Every table in one database, and extra round trips per commit                        |
| Saga with compensations                    | Each service commits alone, with no cross-service locks | No isolation, and a compensation to write and test for every step                    |
| Orchestration                              | One place holds the flow and every saga's state         | One more service to run, and it can grow to know too much                            |
| Choreography                               | Loose coupling, and new listeners need no other changes | The flow is spread across services, so tracing one order is hard                     |

</TradeoffTable>

Skip the saga when all the data lives in one database, even a distributed one: its transactions give all or nothing
and isolation with no compensations. Reach for a saga when separate services each own a database, or when outside
parties such as card networks take part.

## Common mistakes

- **Compensating by deleting the row.** The customer still sees the charge. Write a refund as a new transaction.
- **Charging without an idempotency key.** A retried charge or refund runs twice. Key every step and compensation by
  order id.
- **Putting an irreversible step before the pivot.** If a later step fails, nobody can recall the email. Send it after
  the pivot.
- **Letting a compensation give up.** A refund that fails once and stops leaves the customer charged. Retry it, and
  alert a person if it keeps failing.

<KeyTakeaways>

- A saga splits one action across services into local transactions, and each step commits right away.
- When a step fails, the saga runs compensations for the finished steps, newest first. A compensation is a new forward
  action, such as a refund.
- After the pivot, the saga only goes forward and retries each later step until it succeeds.
- A saga trades isolation for availability. Readers see in-between states, and every step must be idempotent because
  retries repeat it.
- Orchestration keeps the flow in one saga log. Choreography decouples services and spreads the flow across them.

</KeyTakeaways>
