## The failure: two leaders fork the chat

The chat service keeps its history on five servers. A leader takes every new message and copies it to the others in
order, like [read replicas](../../scaling/read-replicas/). Then the leader crashes.

The naive fix lets any server that stops hearing from the leader promote itself. If two notice together, both do.
“noon?” lands on one and “6 pm?” on the other, and merging the histories discards messages users saw as sent. This is
**split brain**. A slow or cut-off leader makes it worse: it looks crashed, yet keeps accepting messages.

## The idea: terms, votes, and a majority

Raft is a consensus algorithm: servers agree on one ordered log, here the chat history, while some crash or get cut off.
It rests on five rules.

- **Terms.** Time splits into numbered terms, each with at most one leader. A server that sees a higher term adopts it,
  so a stale leader steps down as soon as it hears from anyone.
- **Randomized election timeouts.** Each 50 ms heartbeat restarts a follower’s random 150 to 300 ms timer. When it runs
  out, the follower becomes a **candidate** in the next term and asks for votes.
- **One vote per term, for an up-to-date log only.** A server votes once per term, never for a candidate whose log is
  behind its own. A candidate needs 3 of 5.
- **Append with a consistency check.** Each append names the entry before it. A follower that disagrees says no, and the
  leader backs up until the logs match.
- **Commit on a majority, for the current term.** Once a majority stores an entry from the leader’s own term, the leader
  commits it for good. Earlier-term entries commit along with it, as
  [Why a leader counts only its own term](#why-a-leader-counts-only-its-own-term) shows.

Any two groups of 3 out of 5 share a server, the overlap from [Quorums](../quorums/). So no term has two winners, and
every winner needs a vote from a server that holds every committed entry. That gives **leader completeness**: each new
leader holds the whole committed chat.

<Analogy>

A club keeps its minutes in every member’s notebook and numbers its meetings. When the chair goes missing, any member
can call meeting 2 and ask to chair it. Members back one candidate per meeting and refuse anyone with missing lines. A
former chair sees meeting 2 on the door and sits down. Where it breaks: a server cannot tell a dead leader from a slow
one, so its timeout only guesses.

</Analogy>

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: the five rules in full</summary>

**Terms.** Every message carries its sender’s term. A server that sees a higher term adopts it and becomes a follower at
once, and every server rejects messages from an older term. This gives **election safety**: each term has at most one
leader, because a candidate needs a majority and each server votes once per term.

**The up-to-date check.** A voter compares the candidate’s last entry with its own. It refuses an older term on the
last entry, or the same term with fewer entries. A server grants its vote to the first candidate that passes and
refuses every other candidate in that term.

**The consistency check.** Each append names the entry just before the new ones, by position and term. A follower whose
log disagrees at that point says no, and the leader retries one entry earlier. Once the logs match, the follower deletes
everything after the match that conflicts and copies the leader’s entries. This gives **log matching**: if two logs hold
an entry with the same position and term, they agree on every entry up to it.

**The commit point.** The leader passes its commit point to followers on its next append or heartbeat, and each
follower applies entries up to it. A leader never erases or rewrites its own log; only followers truncate.

**Disk.** Real servers write their term, vote, and log to disk before they reply, so a server that restarts cannot vote
twice in one term or forget an entry it acknowledged.

</details>

## Commit on a majority, even with the leader cut off

In Leader crash, Node 2 crashes at 1.00 s, and a user sends “noon?” at 1.10 s. Node 3 times out first, wins term 2 at
1.19 s, and commits “noon?” at 1.25 s, once 3 of 5 servers store it. The chat had no leader for 190 ms. Any future
leader needs 3 votes, so at least one voter stores “noon?” and refuses a log that lacks it.

A partition is harder, because the old leader keeps running. In Old leader cut off, Node 2 and Node 3 split from the
other three. Node 2 accepts “tacos?” and “6 pm?”, but with 2 of 5 copies neither commits or gets confirmed. Node 1 wins
term 2 on the majority side. When the network heals, Node 2 sees term 2, steps down, and erases both. Clients resend
after a timeout and deduplicate by message ID, as [Idempotency](../../reliability/idempotency/) covers.

The minority side refuses writes, the consistent choice from [CAP and PACELC](../cap-and-pacelc/). A cut-off leader
still believes it leads, so before a read a leader confirms its majority (ReadIndex) or relies on a lease that assumes
bounded clock drift. Either gives the linearizable reads from [Consistency models](../consistency-models/).

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: ReadIndex and leases</summary>

**ReadIndex.** The leader records its commit point, sends a heartbeat round, and answers the read once a majority
replies in its term. A deposed leader cannot collect that majority, so it never serves stale chat. The cost is one
round trip per read, or per batch of reads.

**Leases.** The leader skips the round trip for a while after a majority acknowledges a heartbeat. Followers that heard
from it recently refuse to vote for anyone else until their election timeout passes, so no new leader can appear before
the lease ends. This is only safe when clocks drift by a bounded amount, because a fast follower clock or a long leader
pause can let an old leader answer after a new one has taken writes.

**Follower reads.** A follower can serve a read by asking the leader for its commit point, then waiting until it has
applied entries up to it. This spreads read load while keeping reads linearizable.

</details>

## Why a leader counts only its own term

A leader counts copies only for entries from its own term. Earlier-term entries commit indirectly, along with the first
own-term entry after them. Old entry on a majority replays Figure 8 of the Raft paper to show why.

1. “hi” commits, and Node 1 wins term 2. Its entry “pizza?” reaches only Node 2 before Node 1 crashes.
2. Node 5 wins term 3, adds “tacos?” as entry 2, and crashes. Only Node 5 stores it.
3. Node 1 wins term 4 and copies “pizza?” to Node 3, so at 1.04 s 3 of 5 servers store it.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: what this simplified Raft leaves out</summary>

Real Raft, as used in etcd (the store behind Kubernetes), Consul, and CockroachDB, adds pieces this model skips.

**Snapshots.** Servers compact old log entries into a snapshot so logs stop growing forever.

**Membership changes.** Adding or removing a server safely takes a special two-step configuration change.

**Pre-vote.** A server returning from a partition can bump the term and disrupt a healthy leader. Pre-vote first asks
whether it could win, and randomized timeouts keep the remaining elections rare.

**The no-op entry.** The model skips it, so earlier-term entries wait for the next chat message to commit.

**Client retries and reads.** Clients resend after a timeout, and leaders confirm leadership before reads, as the
ReadIndex and lease notes above describe.

**Disk.** The model keeps term, vote, and log through a crash as if they were on disk.

</details>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Raft choices">

| Choice                           | What it buys                         | What it costs                                 |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| Five servers instead of three    | Survives two failures instead of one | Each commit waits for 3 copies instead of 2   |
| Shorter election timeout         | Faster failover after a real crash   | Needless elections when a server is only slow |
| Wider random timeout range       | Fewer split votes                    | A slower worst case before anyone stands      |
| Lease reads instead of ReadIndex | Reads skip a majority round trip     | Safety depends on bounded clock drift         |

</TradeoffTable>

Skip Raft when a primary with a manual failover is enough, or when a leaderless [quorum](../quorums/) store fits data
with no single order. For one agreed order, use etcd, Consul, or a Raft-based database instead of writing your own.

## Common mistakes

- **Committing an old-term entry because a majority stores it.** A newer candidate can still win and erase it.
- **Running an even cluster, or a majority in one zone.** Four servers survive one failure, and a zone holding 3 of 5
  takes the cluster down.
- **Setting the election timeout near the heartbeat interval.** Pauses and lost packets start needless elections, and
  each one pauses writes.
- **Reading from the leader without a leadership check.** A cut-off old leader still answers, with stale chat.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Randomized timeouts, terms, and one vote per term give each term at most one leader.
- A leader commits an entry from its own term once a majority stores it; earlier-term entries commit along with it.
- Majorities overlap, so every new leader already holds every committed entry.
- A cut-off minority stops accepting writes, and reads need ReadIndex or a lease to stay linearizable.

</KeyTakeaways>

- **Any one zone.** Losing a two-server zone leaves 3 of 5, a majority. Spread servers so no zone holds a majority.
- **Shorter timeouts trade safety margin for speed.** Failover takes about one timeout plus a vote round trip. Under

  far below the time between real failures.
