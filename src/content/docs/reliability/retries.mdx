## The failure: three seconds of slowness, an outage that stays

Every time someone opens a sho.rt link, the redirect server sends a click event to the analytics ingest service. The
ingest service has 8 workers and a queue that holds 150 attempts. The redirect server gives each attempt 500 ms, as the
[Timeouts and cancellation](../timeouts/) lesson recommends, and each click a 2 s deadline in total. Every click
carries a click ID, so the analytics store can drop copies, as in [Idempotency](../idempotency/).

Retries look like pure upside. 2% of attempts hit a transient error, and a sender that tries again turns those failures
into confirmed clicks. Then, one afternoon, a slow disk makes every attempt take twice as long for 3 seconds.

## Why retries made it worse

Start with capacity. Eight workers at about 40 ms per attempt finish about 200 attempts a second. During the slowdown
that drops to about 100, below the 150 clicks arriving, so the queue grows by about 50 a second. Once about 50
attempts are queued, a new attempt waits longer than its 500 ms timeout while the service is slow. A full queue is
worse: with 150 attempts ahead of it, a new attempt waits about 750 ms even at full speed.

From then on, almost every attempt in the retry lane times out. Each timeout becomes another attempt in the same queue,
and each of those times out too. The sender sends 3.31 attempts for every click, and attempts peak at 720 a second
against a service that can finish about 200. When the disk recovers at 6 s, fresh clicks plus retries still exceed 200
a second, so the queue never drains. The slowdown caused the overload, and the retries keep it going. Engineers call
this a **metastable failure**: the system stays broken in a state the original trigger no longer explains.

The wasted work is easy to see in the table. The workers finish 2,972 attempts whose senders had already given up on
them, and the analytics store drops 1,198 late copies of clicks it already recorded. Without the click ID, those
copies would have inflated the counts. The lane without retries suffers during the slowdown too, but its load stays at
150 a second, so its queue drains as soon as the disk recovers.

## Backoff: wait longer after each failure

The first fix is to slow down. **Exponential backoff** doubles the wait after each failed attempt, so a struggling
service gets more breathing room the longer it struggles. The lane above already uses it: after the first failure the
sender waits up to 200 ms, after the second up to 400 ms, after the third up to 800 ms, and it never retries past the
click's 2 s deadline. It also skips a retry the deadline cannot fit: a retry that lands after the caller stopped
waiting is pure waste.

Backoff matters most for short outages. Pick **Restart at 3 s** below: the ingest service restarts and refuses every
connection for half a second. One lane retries at once, the other backs off with jitter.

Retrying at once burns through all four attempts in about 40 ms, long before the service is back, and 76 clicks give up
for good. Its attempts peak at 403 a second. Backing off spreads the same attempts across the restart: the jitter
lane peaks at 333 a second, gets 180 attempts refused against 317, and loses only 3 clicks. Both
lanes are healthy 0.1 s after the service comes back, because nothing piled up in a queue.

## Jitter: do not retry in lockstep

Backoff alone has a trap. When many senders fail at the same moment, as they do when a service restarts, they all
compute the same wait and come back at the same moment.

**Most of them.** All forty retry in the same 50 ms step. Once the service is back it takes 3, and the other 37 back off

<RetryHerdWidget client:visible />

Synchronized retries waste capacity twice: each wave overwhelms the service, then the service sits idle until the next
wave. The fix is **full jitter**: pick the wait at random anywhere between zero and the backoff window. A sender that
would have waited exactly 400 ms waits anywhere up to 400 ms instead. The senders spread across the whole window, so
the service sees a steady trickle it can handle. Real networks add a few milliseconds of natural variation, far too
little to break up waves hundreds of milliseconds apart.

## Retry budgets: cap the total

Backoff and jitter decide **when** retries go. Neither limits **how many**. Under sustained overload, every attempt
fails, every failure earns another attempt, and the storm above happens anyway: that lane already used backoff with
full jitter.

A **retry budget** caps retries as a fraction of fresh traffic. Each fresh click adds 0.1 of a token, up to 10 tokens,
and each retry spends one. With 150 clicks a second, the sender can sustain about 15 retries a second, plus one burst of
up to 10 from tokens it banked earlier, however bad things get. When the budget runs dry, failed clicks give up at once
and add no load.

With the budget, the sender retries only 103 times, sends 3,121 attempts, confirms 2,489 clicks, and recovers 2.2 s
after the slowdown, while the budget denies 520 retries that would have fed the storm. On a normal day the budget
changes nothing: only about 3 clicks a second fail, well inside 15 retries a second, so in the toy model a lane with a
budget matches the plain jitter lane click for click. A budget lets retries do their job on a normal day and switches
them off when they would make things worse.

Two more rules keep retries honest:

- **Retry at one layer.** If the browser, the redirect server, and the ingest client each make up to 4 attempts, one
  click can become 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 attempts at the bottom. Pick the layer that owns the deadline and the idempotency
  key, and let the others fail fast.
- **Retry only what can succeed.** Timeouts, refused connections, and 503s can succeed later. A 400 Bad Request will
  fail the same way every time. When a server answers 503 with **Retry-After**, as in
  [Queues and backpressure](../queues-and-backpressure/), wait at least that long.

The lesson Rate limiting covers how the server side enforces a fair share, and the lesson [Circuit breakers](../circuit-breakers/) shows how a
sender stops calling a failing service entirely and probes it gently until it recovers.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Retrying a failed call">

| Choice                          | What it buys                                                        | What it costs                                                                                |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| No retries                      | Never adds load                                                     | Every transient error loses the request: 52 clicks on a normal day in the toy model          |
| Retry at once                   | The fastest answer when one attempt fails at random                 | Spends every attempt inside a short outage and adds load exactly when the service struggles  |
| Exponential backoff, no jitter  | Fewer attempts the longer an outage lasts                           | Senders that failed together retry together, in waves                                        |
| Backoff with full jitter        | Spreads retries across the window, so the service sees a trickle    | Some retries wait longer; total retries still grow without limit under sustained overload    |
| Retry budget                    | Caps retries at a share of fresh traffic, so retries cannot feed an overload | During a long incident, failed requests stop retrying; needs a counter per sender   |
| Retries at every layer          | Each layer hides its own transient errors                           | Attempts multiply: 4 attempts at each of 3 layers is 64 at the bottom                        |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

sho.rt's mobile app creates short links through an API gateway, which calls the link service, which writes to a
database. Each of the three makes up to 3 attempts per call, with no wait between them. Last week the database failed over to a replica,
which normally takes 10 s. During the failover the database saw about 27 times its usual traffic, and the failover took
4 minutes. Explain what happened, and design a retry policy that keeps the next failover to about 10 s.

<div slot="answer">

- **Retries multiplied across layers.** Three layers that each make up to 3 attempts turn one request into 3 × 3 × 3 =
  27 attempts at the database, all sent at once, exactly when it could handle the least.
- **Retry at one layer only.** Let the app retry, since it owns the deadline and can hold the idempotency key. The
  gateway and the link service fail fast and pass the error back.
- **Back off with full jitter** from about 200 ms, double the window after each failure, cap it at a few seconds, and
  never retry past the request's deadline.
- **Add a retry budget** of about 10% of fresh requests per app instance, so a long failover shrinks retries to a
  trickle instead of a storm. Honor Retry-After when the gateway sends one.
- **Make link creation idempotent.** The app sends one idempotency key per new link, so a retry after a lost response
  returns the existing link instead of creating a second one.

</div>

</Challenge>
