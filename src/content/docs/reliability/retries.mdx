## The failure: three seconds of slowness, an outage that stays

Every time someone opens a sho.rt link, the redirect server sends a click event to the analytics ingest service: 8
workers and a queue that holds 150 attempts. The redirect server gives each attempt 500 ms, as in
[Timeouts and cancellation](../timeouts/), and each click a 2 s deadline. A click ID lets the analytics store drop
copies, as in [Idempotency](../idempotency/). Retries rescue the 2% of attempts that hit a transient error. Then a
slow disk makes every attempt take twice as long for 3 seconds.

Two facts about this pipeline decide what happens next:

- **The queue holds more work than the sender will wait for.** 150 queued attempts take 750 ms to drain even at full
  speed, against a 500 ms timeout.
- **The service finishes work nobody waits for.** Workers process every attempt they pull from the queue, even after
  its sender gave up.

## Why retries made it worse

Eight workers finish about 200 attempts a second, and the slowdown halves that to about 100, below the 150 arriving.
The queue grows until each new attempt waits past its 500 ms timeout. Each timeout becomes a retry in the same queue,
and that retry times out too. The retry lane sends 3.31 attempts per click, peaking at 720 a second. At 6 s the disk
recovers, yet fresh clicks plus retries still exceed 200 a second, so the queue never drains. Engineers call this a
**metastable failure**: the overload outlives its trigger. The lane without retries keeps its load at 150 a second, so
its queue drains as soon as the disk recovers.

<StepDiagram client:visible title="How a slowdown becomes a retry storm" nodes={nodes} edges={edges} steps={steps} height={300} />

<Analogy>

A busy pizza place stops answering the phone for a few minutes. Every caller who hears ringing hangs up and redials at
once. When the staff can answer again, the line is jammed with redials from people who already called, and new
customers cannot get through either.

</Analogy>

The analogy breaks when a caller hangs up: that call ends, and nobody cooks the pizza. An abandoned attempt stays in
the queue, and a worker still processes it.

**Deadline propagation** closes that gap. The sender passes its remaining time along with each attempt, as
[Timeouts and cancellation](../timeouts/) does across hops, and a worker drops any attempt whose deadline has passed
before starting it.

- **Without the drop**, as in this toy model, workers spend capacity on dead attempts while live ones wait behind them
  and time out, so the storm keeps going.
- **With the drop**, an expired attempt costs one quick check. Live attempts finish sooner, fewer turn into retries,
  and the queue drains soon after the disk recovers.

Dropping expired work leaves retries uncapped, so pair it with a retry budget. The simulations never drop expired work.

## Spread retries out: backoff and jitter

Retries still pay off on a normal day. The goal is to send them when the service can take them.

**Exponential backoff** doubles the wait after each failure: up to 200 ms, then 400 ms, then 800 ms. The sender skips
any retry that cannot finish before the click's 2 s deadline.

Backoff alone sends waves. Senders that fail together, as in a restart, compute the same wait and return together.
**Full jitter** picks each wait at random between zero and the backoff window, so senders spread out and the service
sees a steady trickle.

## Cap the total: attempt limits and retry budgets

Backoff and jitter decide **when** retries go, never **how many**. The storm lane used both and still sent 3.31
attempts per click. Four controls each limit something different:

| Control           | What it limits                                     | In this lesson                                                    |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Attempt limit     | Tries per request or message                       | 4 per click, inside the 2 s deadline                              |
| Backoff           | The wait between tries, with jitter                | Up to 200 ms, then 400 ms, then 800 ms                            |
| Retry budget      | Retries as a share of all traffic from one client  | About 10% of fresh clicks                                         |
| Dead letter queue | Where a message goes after its attempt limit       | Unused here; see [event-driven design](../../async/event-driven-design/) |

An attempt limit and backoff act on one request at a time. Under sustained overload every attempt fails, so each request
still sends 4 times its share. Only a retry budget caps the whole client.

The budget works like a token bucket. Each fresh click earns 0.1 of a token, up to 10 tokens, and each retry spends
one. At 150 clicks a second that sustains about 15 retries a second, however bad things get. When the bucket is empty,
failed clicks give up at once and add no load.

With the budget, the sender retries only 103 times, confirms 2,489 clicks, and recovers 2.2 s after the slowdown. The
budget denies 520 retries that would have fed the storm. Compare **Time to recover once the service is healthy**: the
lane without a budget is still failing when the run ends. On a normal day only about 3 clicks a second fail, well
inside the budget, so both lanes match click for click.

Two more rules keep retries honest:

- **Retry at one layer.** If the browser, the redirect server, and the ingest client each make up to 4 attempts, one
  click can become 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 attempts at the bottom. The layer that owns the deadline and the idempotency key
  retries, and the others fail fast.
- **Retry only what can succeed.** Timeouts, refused connections, and 503s can succeed later; a 400 Bad Request fails
  the same way every time. When a server answers 503 with **Retry-After**, as in
  [Queues and backpressure](../queues-and-backpressure/), wait at least that long.

[Rate limiting](../rate-limiting/) enforces a fair share on the server, and
[Circuit breakers](../circuit-breakers/) stop calls to a failing service until it recovers.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Retrying a failed call">

| Choice                   | What it buys                                    | What it costs                                                 |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| No retries               | Never adds load                                 | Loses every transient error                                   |
| Retry at once            | The fastest recovery from a random failure      | Burns every attempt inside a short outage                     |
| Backoff, no jitter       | Fewer attempts the longer an outage lasts       | Senders that failed together retry together, in waves         |
| Backoff with full jitter | A steady trickle of retries instead of waves    | Total retries still grow under sustained overload             |
| Retry budget             | Retries can never feed an overload              | In a long incident, failed requests stop retrying             |
| Deadline propagation     | Capacity goes to attempts someone still waits for | Every hop must pass the deadline along and check it         |

</TradeoffTable>

## Common mistakes

- Retrying at every layer, so attempts multiply.
- Backing off without jitter, so senders that failed together return together.
- Treating an attempt limit as a load limit.
- Letting servers finish work whose caller gave up.
- Retrying a 400 Bad Request, or a purchase without an idempotency key.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Retries add load when a service struggles. A queue longer than the timeout, plus servers that finish abandoned work, turns a short slowdown into a lasting outage.
- Backoff spaces out one sender's tries; full jitter spreads out senders that failed together.
- An attempt limit acts on one request. A retry budget caps retries across the client and costs nothing on a normal day.
- Propagate deadlines, retry at one layer, and retry only transient failures on calls that are safe to repeat.

</KeyTakeaways>
