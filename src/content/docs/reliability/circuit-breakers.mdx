## The failure: analytics hangs, redirects go down

Every time someone opens a sho.rt link, a redirect server looks up the long URL and then calls the analytics ingest
service to record the click before it answers. Each redirect server has 16 workers and a queue that holds 64 waiting
redirects. Following [Timeouts and cancellation](../timeouts/), every ingest call gets a 500 ms timeout, and a click
whose call fails is written to a local buffer and uploaded later. Analytics is a side job: the visitor only wants the
long URL.

One afternoon at 3 s, the ingest service hangs. It accepts calls and never answers. It stays that way until 12 s.

## Why a timeout was not enough

A timeout bounds how long one call waits. It does nothing about how many calls wait. On a normal day a redirect takes
40 to 60 ms (10 ms to look up the URL plus a 30 to 50 ms call), so 16 workers can finish about 320 redirects a second,
more than twice the 150 that arrive. Once ingest hangs, every redirect spends 510 ms on the lookup and the call that
times out, and 16 workers finish only about 31 a second.

The other 119 a second wait in the queue. The queue of 64 is full by 3.6 s, and from then on new redirects get a 503
until ingest heals. The ones that get in wait about 2 s in the queue plus 500 ms for their own call, so the p95 time to
answer a redirect climbs to 2,530 ms. A service the redirect never needed took the redirect down with it. This is a
**cascading failure**: trouble in one service spreads to every service that waits on it.

What the redirect server needs is a way to stop calling ingest at all while it is broken. That is a **circuit
breaker**: a small state machine wrapped around calls to one dependency. The name comes from the switch in a fuse box,
which cuts a circuit that draws too much current before the wiring overheats.

## The three states

The breaker in the simulation follows three rules:

- **Closed.** Calls go through, and the breaker records how each one ends. A timeout, a refused connection, and an
  error all count as failures. If at least half of the calls that finished in the last second failed, and at least 20
  calls finished, the breaker **opens**.
- **Open.** Calls fail fast without touching ingest. The redirect server writes the click to its buffer in 10 ms and
  answers. After a 2 s **cooldown**, the breaker turns **half-open**.
- **Half-open.** The breaker lets 3 calls through as **probes** and keeps skipping ingest for everyone else. If all 3
  probes succeed, it **closes**. If any probe fails, it opens again for another 2 s.

In the hang, the breaker opened at 4.1 s, more than a second after ingest stopped answering. Two things set that
delay. A hung call only counts as a failure when its 500 ms timeout fires, so the first failures land at about 3.5 s.
At that moment the 1 s window still holds the healthy calls that finished just before 3 s: about 16 failures out of
70 calls, well short of half. While every worker waits, no new call finishes, so the healthy calls slowly age out of
the window. The next timeouts land at about 4 s, and the breaker trips when 18 of the last 20 calls have failed. Until
then the queue filled, and those 70 redirects got the only 503s the breaker lane sent. A shorter call timeout would
surface failures sooner, which is one more reason to keep timeouts tight.

The **minimum call count** keeps the breaker calm on a normal day. With 2% random errors, about 3 of the 150 calls in a
second fail, nowhere near half. Pick **Normal day** above: in the toy model the breaker never opens, and both lanes
produce identical numbers, 3,013 redirects answered. Without a minimum, a breaker that judges one call would open on
the first random error.

## The fallback: answer without the dependency

A breaker helps only when the server has something useful to do while it is open. For sho.rt, the click can wait:
the redirect server writes it to a local buffer and uploads it once ingest is back. Look at the table after the hang.
The breaker lane records 1,391 clicks directly and buffers 1,553, so it keeps all 2,944 clicks behind the redirects it
answered. The lane without a breaker also buffers a click when its call fails, but each of its 1,016 turned away
redirects loses both the redirect and the click. The toy model counts a buffered click as kept; it does not simulate
the upload.

Some dependencies have no good fallback. If the links database itself fails, the redirect server cannot invent the
long URL. A breaker still helps there: failing fast with a 503 and a Retry-After header frees the workers at once and
tells callers when to come back, as in [Queues and backpressure](../queues-and-backpressure/). A caller that retries,
as in [Retries with backoff and jitter](../retries/), should treat an open breaker as a reason to stop retrying for
now.

## When the dependency fails fast

A hang is the worst case, because every call waits for its timeout. Pick a crash instead: ingest goes down at 3 s and
refuses every connection within 10 ms.

Here the breaker changes nothing for visitors. A refused call frees its worker in 10 ms, so no worker gets stuck: both
lanes answer 3,014 redirects, turn none away, and answer 95% of them within 60 ms. The breaker opens about half a
second after the crash and sends ingest 1,514 calls against 3,018, about half. For a service that is trying to restart, half the
connection attempts can be the difference between coming up and falling over again. The cost shows up at the end:
when ingest returns at 12 s, the breaker lane takes 1.8 s to record clicks again against 0.1 s, because it waits out a
cooldown before it tries. Against a slow dependency a breaker protects the caller; against a fast failure it mainly
protects the dependency.

## Probe gently

Half-open is the risky moment. The dependency may still be broken, or it may have just restarted with cold caches and
empty connection pools. A breaker with no probe limit lets every redirect through until the round is decided.

The gentle breaker caps the damage of a wrong guess at 3 stuck workers for 500 ms. The flood lane also times out 80
calls against 41 and turns away 72 redirects against 70, and it reconnects only 0.1 s sooner once ingest heals: the
cooldown decides when a breaker tries again, and the probe count only decides how hard. This toy ingest heals at 12 s
whatever its load, so the simulation cannot show the bigger danger. A real service that just restarted can fall over
again under a flood of probes, which sends the breaker back to open and starts the cycle over. A few probes answer the
question "is it healthy?" without asking it to survive everyone at once. Many systems also ramp traffic back up
gradually after the breaker closes.

Two related tools pair well with a breaker. A **bulkhead** caps how many workers may wait on one dependency, say 4 of
16, so even before a breaker opens, a hang can never take every worker. The lesson Rate limiting covers how a server
caps what each client may send, and the lesson Observability covers how to alert on a breaker that opens, since an open
breaker hides a broken dependency from visitors and so from anyone watching only the redirect error rate.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Protecting a caller from a failing dependency">

| Choice                               | What it buys                                                              | What it costs                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Timeouts only                        | Each call has a bounded wait; no extra state                              | A hung dependency still holds every worker: 1,016 redirects turned away in the toy model           |
| Breaker with a fallback              | The main job keeps working, and the dependency gets far fewer calls       | Needs somewhere to put the work, such as a buffer, plus code to replay it later                    |
| Breaker that fails fast with a 503   | Frees workers at once and gives callers a clear signal                    | Requests that need the dependency still fail, just quickly                                         |
| Low threshold or short window        | Opens sooner when the dependency breaks                                   | Opens on noise unless a minimum call count holds it back                                           |
| Long cooldown                        | Fewer probes and less load on a struggling dependency                     | Slower to notice recovery: up to one full cooldown after the dependency heals                      |
| No probe limit when half-open        | Nothing, apart from reconnecting a little sooner                          | Every caller becomes a probe: 61 against 12, and a p95 of 460 ms against 60 ms in the toy model     |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

Before redirecting, sho.rt's redirect servers ask a URL safety service whether the long URL is known malware. It
usually answers in 20 ms, but twice this month it hung for ten minutes, and redirects failed across the whole site.
Product says a known bad link must never be redirected without a warning. Design the protection: timeout, breaker
settings, and what a redirect does while the breaker is open.

<div slot="answer">

- **Give the safety check a tight timeout**, about 100 ms, so each hung call holds a worker briefly and the breaker
  sees failures quickly.
- **Wrap it in a circuit breaker per redirect server**: open when at least half of 20 or more calls in a few seconds
  fail, cool down for a few seconds, then send a handful of probes and close only if they all succeed.
- **Cap the workers that may wait on it** with a bulkhead, so a hang that the breaker has not yet caught still leaves
  most workers free.
- **Fall back on cached verdicts.** Most clicks go to popular links, so cache each link's last verdict. While the
  breaker is open, redirect links with a recent clean verdict, and show a warning page for links with no verdict or a
  bad one. That keeps the product rule without a site-wide outage.
- **Do not retry while the breaker is open**, and alert when it opens: visitors will barely notice, so the team must
  hear about it some other way.

</div>

</Challenge>
