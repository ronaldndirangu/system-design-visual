## The failure: a deploy breaks a page it never touched

of 5 connections, and a request that waits over 1 s for one gives up with 503. At 20 s, deploy v42 ships a checkout
query without its index: 30 ms becomes 500 ms. Browse never runs that query.

## Three signals, three questions

<StepDiagram client:visible title="One request, three kinds of telemetry" nodes={nodes} edges={edges} steps={steps} height={360} />

- **Metrics** are numbers aggregated over time: counters, latency histograms, gauges. They answer "is something wrong, when, and where?" For a fixed set of label values, their storage stays roughly
  constant whether traffic is 10 or 10,000 requests per second. Each update still costs a little CPU, and the cost
  grows with label cardinality. They cannot show a single request.
- **Traces** follow one request through every service as a tree of **spans**. They answer "where did this request
  spend its time?" They cost a lot per request, so most systems keep a sample.
- **Logs** are timestamped records of events, such as "slow query 552 ms: orders by customer_id, no index". They
  answer "what exactly happened?" Structure them as key and value pairs to filter on fields.

The three meet through the **correlation ID**, usually the trace ID. The gateway creates it, every service forwards it
in the W3C `traceparent` header, and every log line and span carries it.

<Analogy>

Metrics are a hospital's ward board: how many patients, how many in pain, average wait. Traces are one patient's chart,
every test in order with its duration. Logs are the nurses' notes. The wristband ID is the correlation ID: without it,
the notes could belong to anyone.

</Analogy>

The analogy breaks on cost: a hospital charts every patient, and a busy service traces only a sample.

## Two ways to read a dashboard: RED and USE

- **RED** for every service: **Rate**, **Errors**, and **Duration** (latency percentiles, never just the average). RED
  shows what callers feel.
- **USE** for every resource, such as pools, CPUs, disks, and queues: **Utilization**, **Saturation** (work waiting),
  and **Errors**. USE shows why.

In the dashboard above, RED shows the symptom: browse latency climbs and 503s appear. USE shows the cause: the pool sits
at 5 of 5 busy with a growing queue. Saturation warns first, because work queues before it fails. The
[SLOs lesson](../../thinking/slos/) explains why dashboards track p99 and how an error budget turns them into decisions.

Keep label values few: one value per user multiplies time series, the cardinality problem the
[metrics case study](../../case-studies/metrics/) covers.

A dashboard helps only when someone looks at it. Alerts decide when a person looks, and the
[SLOs lesson](../../thinking/slos/) gives the rule: alert on how fast the error budget burns.

- **Page** when the burn would empty the budget within a day or two, and require two windows to agree so a short blip
  wakes nobody. A common starting point from the SRE workbook: page when both the last hour and the last 5 minutes burn
  faster than 14.4x, which spends 2% of a 30 day budget in one hour.
- **Warn** with a ticket on a slow burn, such as just above 1x over 3 days. Someone fixes it during working hours.

Try it on the click pipeline from [circuit breakers](../circuit-breakers/). Sort each signal into page or warn:

1. Redirect errors burn the budget at 20x over the last hour and the last 5 minutes.
2. The breaker on the click uploader has stayed open for 10 minutes; redirects still succeed.
3. The click buffer is full and dropping clicks.
4. Consumer lag on the click queue reaches 20 hours, and the queue keeps messages for 24 hours.
5. Redirect errors burn at 1.5x over the last 3 days.

open breaker is doing its job while the buffer holds the clicks, and a slow burn can wait for working hours. Promote 2

## Finding the slow span

A trace waterfall draws each span as a bar on one timeline, indented under its parent. A parent's bar covers its
children, so the longest bar is rarely the culprit. Look for the most **self time**: the span's duration minus the time
covered by the union of its children's intervals. Parallel children overlap, and the union counts that overlap once. A
100 ms parent with two 80 ms children running side by side has 20 ms of self time; subtracting each child would claim
minus 60 ms. When a span fans out, follow its **critical path**, the chain of spans that sets the end-to-end latency.
Speeding up any other branch leaves the request just as slow.

The [timeouts](../timeouts/), [retries](../retries/), and [circuit breakers](../circuit-breakers/) lessons cover what
the shop does next. Observability tells it which one it needs.

- **Propagate context everywhere.** Pass the trace ID through HTTP headers, message queue metadata, and background

- **Link metrics to traces.** Many metrics stores attach an **exemplar**, a trace ID, to a histogram bucket, so you can
  jump from the spike on a chart straight to a slow request inside it.

## Sampling: which traces to keep

Storing every span of every request often costs more than running the service, so tracing systems **sample** within a
budget, such as 10% of requests. Both policies below spend the same budget; they differ in which traces fill it.

- **Head sampling** decides when the request starts, usually by hashing the trace ID. Every service reaches the same
  decision without talking to the others, and nothing waits in memory. It also decides before anyone knows whether the
  request will fail.
- **Tail sampling** buffers every span until the trace finishes, then decides. Here each one second window may store
  10% of the requests that finished in it, filled with failures first, then requests over 1 s, then the rest. All
  spans of a trace must reach the same collector.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: where tail sampling still loses traces</summary>

Why does tail sampling miss 14 failures? Each one second window stores about 4 traces at this budget, and failures
arrive in bursts: in the worst second, 14 requests fail and the window keeps 6 of them. Slow requests fare worse, 62
of 201, because they rank behind failures. A window cannot save its slots for a burst it cannot foresee, so the quiet
seconds before the deploy spend theirs on ordinary traces. The table counts these losses as failed or slow requests
dropped: 153 for tail sampling and 227 for head sampling.

On a **quiet day** nothing fails or runs slow, so both samplers spend the same budget on ordinary traces: head sampling
stores 262 and tail sampling 234. Tail sampling still pays for its buffer, up to 952 spans in memory. With a **tight
budget** of 1%, head sampling stores 31 traces and none of the 50 failures. Tail sampling stores 23, its full budget,
and 4 of the failures, because each window now earns well under one slot. Real collectors fix this with a separate rule
for errors, such as a small error budget on top of the baseline or a cap per error type, and they report how many
traces overflowed.

Tail sampling only sees what reaches the collector, so every service must export every span. A head sampler in front
of it would drop most failures before the tail sampler could judge them. Teams that need to cut export volume can head
sample at the edge and accept that some failures go missing.

</details>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Which signal, and how much of it?">

| Choice          | What it buys                                             | What it costs                                                    |
| --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Metrics         | Cheap totals, trends, and alerts                         | No single request; each label value adds a series                |
| Traces          | Where one request spent its time                         | Costly per request, so sampled; needs context propagation        |
| Structured logs | Exact events and values, easy to filter                  | Volume grows with traffic; useless across services without an ID |
| RED dashboards  | What callers feel, per service                           | Shows symptoms; the cause may sit elsewhere                      |
| USE dashboards  | Which resource is full or queueing                       | You must know which resources matter                             |
| Head sampling   | Simple, consistent, no buffering                         | Keeps rare failures at the base rate                             |
| Tail sampling   | Spends the same budget on failures and slow traces first | Buffers every span on one collector; failure bursts can overflow |

</TradeoffTable>

## Common mistakes

- **Alerting on averages.** A mean hides the slow tail. Chart p99 and page on SLO burn rate.
- **Putting user IDs in metric labels.** Each value adds a time series. Keep per-request detail in traces and logs.
- **Dropping the trace ID at one hop.** A queue or background job that skips `traceparent` splits every trace in two.
- **Blaming the longest bar.** A parent's bar covers its children. Compare self time and follow the critical path.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Metrics say when and where, traces say where one request spent its time, and logs say exactly what happened. The
  correlation ID joins them.
- RED on services shows the symptom; USE on resources shows the full resource behind it.
- The culprit span has the most self time: its duration minus the union of its children's intervals.
- Head and tail sampling spend the same budget; tail sampling decides after the trace ends, so it can keep the failed
  and slow traces.

</KeyTakeaways>
