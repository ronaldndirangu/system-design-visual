## The failure: a deploy breaks a page it never touched

A small shop serves 40 requests per second: three quarters are browse pages, one quarter are checkouts. Both routes
share one database connection pool with 5 connections. A request that waits more than 1 s for a connection gives up with
503. At 20 s, deploy v42 ships a new checkout query that forgot its index. The query used to take about 30 ms; now it
takes about 500 ms. Browse pages never run that query.

    'Browse slows down and some browse requests fail with 503',
    'Browse speeds up, because checkouts now leave the cache alone',
  ]}
  answer={1}
>

Browse breaks too. Slow checkout queries hold pool connections about 16 times longer, so the pool fills and every
request that needs the database queues behind them, browse included. In this toy model with seed 42, checkout p99
passes 500 ms at 20.7 s, the pool saturates at 21.2 s, browse p99 passes 1 s at 24.4 s, and the first 503 lands at
24.5 s. Over the minute, 50 of 2,347 requests fail.

</Predict>

This is a **toy model**: Poisson arrivals, fixed span lengths drawn from small ranges, and a first come, first served
pool. The numbers are illustrative. Every scenario replays the identical stream of requests, so every difference comes
from the deploy or the pool size.

Nobody wrote an alert for "browse breaks when checkout gets slow". That is the point of **observability**: the ability
to answer questions about your system that you did not plan for, from the telemetry it already emits. Monitoring
checks the failures you predicted. Observability helps with the ones you did not.

## Three signals, three questions

<StepDiagram client:visible title="One request, three kinds of telemetry" nodes={nodes} edges={edges} steps={steps} height={360} />

- **Metrics** are numbers aggregated over time: a request counter, a latency histogram, a gauge of busy connections.
  They answer "is something wrong, when did it start, and where?" They cost the same at 10 or 10,000 requests per
  second, so you can keep them for months. They cannot show you any single request.
- **Traces** follow one request through every service as a tree of **spans**. They answer "where did this request
  spend its time?" They are expensive per request, so most systems keep only a sample.
- **Logs** are timestamped records of discrete events, such as "slow query 552 ms: orders by customer_id, no index".
  They answer "what exactly happened, and with which values?" Write them as structured key and value pairs so you can
  filter on fields instead of parsing text.

The three meet through one field: the **correlation ID**, usually the trace ID. The gateway creates it, every service
forwards it (the W3C `traceparent` header is the common format), and every log line and span carries it. With it, one
search returns everything every service recorded about one request.

<Analogy>

Metrics are a hospital's ward board: how many patients, how many in pain, average wait. Traces are one patient's chart,
showing every test in order with how long each took. Logs are the nurses' notes. The patient ID wristband is the
correlation ID: without it, the notes and the chart could belong to anyone.

</Analogy>

## Two ways to read a dashboard: RED and USE

Two short checklists keep dashboards focused.

- **RED** for every service that handles requests: **Rate** (requests per second), **Errors** (failed requests per
  second), and **Duration** (latency percentiles, never just the average). RED shows what callers feel.
- **USE** for every resource: **Utilization** (how busy it is), **Saturation** (how much work waits for it), and
  **Errors**. Pools, CPUs, disks, and queues are resources. USE shows why callers feel it.

In the dashboard above, RED shows the symptom: browse latency climbs and 503s appear. USE points at the cause: the pool
sits at 5 of 5 busy with a growing queue. Saturation is the earliest warning, because work queues before it fails. The
[SLOs lesson](../../thinking/slos/) explains why dashboards track p99 rather than averages and how an error budget turns
these numbers into decisions.

Add labels such as route and status to metrics, and keep their values few. A label with one value per user or per
request multiplies the number of time series until the metrics store struggles; the
[metrics case study](../../case-studies/metrics/) covers that cardinality problem. Put per-request detail in traces and
logs instead.

## Finding the slow span

A trace waterfall draws each span as a bar on one timeline, indented under its parent. A parent's bar covers its
children, so the longest bar is rarely the culprit. Look for the span with the most **self time**: its duration minus
the time its children account for.

It waited. In this run, the slow browse trace spends 727 ms in db.pool.acquire and 20 ms in its own query. The
trace proves where the time went; it cannot say who held the connections. The logs written during that same second can:
they show other requests' "slow query, no index (v42)" warnings.

</Predict>

Try each trace. Before the deploy, a checkout spends most of its time in payments.charge, which is normal. Right after
the deploy, the culprit is db.query orders, the unindexed query. Later, browse requests blame db.pool.acquire, and the
failed browse waits the full 1 s before it gives up. With the filter off, the log panel mixes lines from every request
running at that moment; with it on, you see only this request's story.

Two habits make this work in a real system:

- **Propagate context everywhere.** Pass the trace ID through HTTP headers, message queue metadata, and background
  jobs. One service that drops it splits every trace in two.
- **Link metrics to traces.** Many metrics stores attach an **exemplar**, a trace ID, to a histogram bucket, so you can
  jump from the spike on a chart straight to a slow request inside it.

The timeouts, retries, and circuit breakers lessons show what the shop should do next: stop waiting a full second for a
connection, avoid retrying into a saturated pool, and cut off a dependency that keeps failing. Observability tells you
which of those you need.

## Sampling: which traces to keep

Storing every span of every request costs a lot, often more than running the service. So tracing systems **sample**.

- **Head sampling** decides when the request starts, usually with a hash of the trace ID, for example "keep 10%". Every
  service makes the same decision without talking to each other, and nothing waits in memory. It also decides before
  anyone knows whether the request will fail.
- **Tail sampling** buffers every span until the trace finishes, then decides: keep every error, every request over
  1 s, and a 10% baseline of the rest. It catches the interesting traces. The collector must hold every span in
  memory until it decides, and all spans of one trace must reach the same collector.

<Predict
  client:visible
  question="Head sampling keeps a random 10% of traces. During the incident, 50 requests fail. About how many of those failures will have a trace you can open?"
  options={[
    'All 50, because errors matter most',
    'About 5, one in ten',
    'None, because head sampling skips errors',
  ]}
  answer={1}
>

About one in ten: the coin flip happens before the request fails, so failures get the same 10% as everything else. In
this run, head sampling keeps 262 traces and only 3 of the 50 failures. Tail sampling keeps 489 traces, including all
50 failures, and holds up to 772 spans in memory while it waits to decide.

</Predict>

<SamplingDuelWidget
  client:visible
  initial="incident"
  title="Head sampling against tail sampling"
  instructions="Press Play and watch the failed requests chart. Then pick Quiet day: with no failures and no slow requests, both samplers keep the same traces. Finally try Tight budget."

On a **quiet day** the two tie at 262 traces, because no request fails or runs slow; tail sampling still pays for its
buffer. With a **tight budget** of 1%, head sampling keeps 31 traces and none of the 50 failures, while tail sampling
still keeps all 50. Tail sampling only sees what reaches the collector, so every service must export every span. A
head sampler in front of it would drop most failures before the tail sampler could judge them. Teams that need to cut
export volume can head sample at the edge and accept that some failures go missing.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Which signal, and how much of it?">

| Choice                  | What it buys                                           | What it costs                                                     |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Metrics                 | Cheap totals and trends, months of history, alerts      | No single request, and each new label multiplies series           |
| Traces                  | Where one request spent its time, across services      | Costly per request, so sampled; needs context propagation         |
| Structured logs         | Exact events and values, easy to filter                | Volume grows with traffic; useless across services without an ID |
| RED dashboards          | What callers feel, per service                         | Shows symptoms; the cause may sit in a shared resource            |
| USE dashboards          | Which resource is full or queueing                     | One per resource, and you must know which resources matter        |
| Head sampling           | Simple, consistent, no buffering                       | Keeps rare failures at the same low rate as everything else       |
| Tail sampling           | Keeps every error and slow trace                       | Every span exported, memory to buffer, routing to one collector    |

</TradeoffTable>

## Mini design challenge

<Challenge>

The shop adds a recommendations service that browse calls on every page view. Support says about 1 browse page in 500
now takes over 3 s, and nobody can reproduce it. Traffic is 400 requests per second across six services, and the
budget allows storing about 5% of traces. Sketch the metrics and dashboards you add, how you find one of those slow
requests, the sampling policy, and what every service must do for this to work.

<div slot="answer">

1. **RED per service and route.** Request counters and latency histograms labeled by service, route, and status (no
   user IDs as labels). Dashboards show p50, p99, and p99.9: a 1 in 500 problem lives above p99, so p99 alone can miss
   it.
2. **USE per resource.** Pool utilization and waiters, CPU, and the recommendations service's own queue depth. If its
   queue grows before the slow pages appear, that is the lead.
3. **Exemplars.** Attach trace IDs to the latency histogram so a click on the p99.9 spike opens one of the slow traces.
4. **Tail sampling at the collector.** Keep every error, every request over 1 s, and a 2% baseline. Route spans by
   trace ID so each trace lands on one collector. Errors and slow requests normally make up well under 3% of traffic,
   so the total stays near 5%. An outage can break that assumption, so add a per category rate cap: always keep
   requests over 3 s, and sample the 1 s to 3 s band when its volume spikes. The cap gives up some moderately slow
   traces to protect the budget.
5. **Propagation everywhere.** Every service forwards `traceparent`, starts a span per outbound call, and writes
   structured logs with the trace ID. In the slow trace, compare self times to find the span that owns the 3 s, then
   search the logs for that trace ID.

</div>

</Challenge>
