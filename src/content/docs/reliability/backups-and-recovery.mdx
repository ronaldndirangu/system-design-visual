## The failure: a migration deletes the orders

The shop from [observability](../observability/) keeps its orders in one 1 TB database with two
[read replicas](../../scaling/read-replicas/). At midnight, a job copies the whole database to a bucket.

At 15:40, a migration meant for a test table runs against production and deletes every order row. Checkout keeps
working, but the history page shows nothing older than 15:40.

Replicas, RAID, and extra zones protect against hardware failure. Bad writes from migrations, buggy deploys, scripts
run against the wrong database, and attackers need a copy from before the write: a **backup**.

## Backups are copies in time

Three designs cover almost every database.

- **Full backup.** A complete copy on a schedule. A restore loads one file, but every copy costs 1 TB.
- **Incremental backup.** Only what changed since the previous backup, about 5 GB an hour here. A restore loads the
  last full backup, then applies each incremental in order. One missing or corrupt link breaks everything after it.
- **Snapshot plus log replay.** The database writes each change to its write-ahead log first, as in
  [storage engines](../../foundations/storage-engines/). Archive that log every minute and keep a snapshot of the data
  files. A restore loads the snapshot, then replays the log up to any moment you choose. This is
  **point-in-time recovery**, or PITR.

Replay skips the waiting on clients and locks, so it runs faster than the original writes; this lesson assumes ten
times faster. The further the target lies from the snapshot, the longer replay runs.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: logical and physical backups</summary>

A **logical** backup, such as `pg_dump` or `mysqldump`, writes SQL or rows. It restores into a different version or
even a different engine, and you can pull one table out of it. Loading it rebuilds every index, so it restores slowly.
A **physical** backup copies the data files and log, as `pg_basebackup` or a disk snapshot does. It restores fast, but
only into the same engine version. Large systems keep physical backups for recovery and take logical dumps for
portability and single-table restores.

Copying data files while the database runs gives a torn copy, with some pages from before a write and some from after.
Use the database's own backup tool, or a snapshot taken together with the log that makes it consistent.

</details>

<Analogy>

A replica is a second pen tied to the first: whatever you write, including a line through a page, it writes too. A
snapshot is a photocopy of the notebook from last midnight. The archived log is a journal of every stroke since. To
recover a page someone scribbled over at 15:40, you take the photocopy and redraw every stroke from the journal up to
15:39.

</Analogy>

## RPO and RTO are budgets

- **Recovery point objective (RPO):** how much recent data you may lose, measured in time.
- **Recovery time objective (RTO):** how long until the service works again. Here, that means until the deleted orders
  are back in the primary.

Like an [SLO](../../thinking/slos/), each is a promise the business agrees to. Orders here get an RPO of 1 hour and an
RTO of 4 hours. Click analytics could accept a day for both, because raw logs can rebuild it.

Backup cadence sets the RPO: with full backups at midnight, a delete at 15:40 loses 15 h 40 min. The RTO is bytes to
load divided by load speed, plus replay time. At 500 GB per hour, 1 TB takes 2 hours to load. The widget starts the
clock when the restore starts; a real incident adds the time to notice and decide.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: snapshots that cost less than a full copy</summary>

The widget charges a full terabyte per snapshot. Cloud disk snapshots store only the blocks that changed since the
previous snapshot, so a 6 hour cadence costs far less in practice. Some also restore lazily: the new disk is usable in
minutes and fetches blocks on first read, which shortens the RTO but makes the first hours slow. Measure both in a
drill before you count on them.

</details>

## A backup you never restored is a hope

The corrupt scenario is the common one. In 2017, an engineer at GitLab.com deleted the primary database's data
directory by mistake. The team found its regular dumps had been failing silently, and the alert emails never arrived.
They restored from a staging copy about six hours old.

A restore drill proves a backup on a schedule:

1. Restore the latest backup into a fresh database, using the runbook and the on-call people.
2. Time each stage (fetch, load, replay) and compare the total with the RTO.
3. Compare row counts and checksums of key tables with the source at the same log position.
4. Alert when a drill fails or runs past the RTO.

Many teams automate a small drill daily and run a full one by hand each quarter.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: where to keep the copies</summary>

The classic **3-2-1 rule** says three copies of the data, on two kinds of storage, with one copy off site. Backups
usually live in [object storage](../../scaling/object-storage/), which is cheap per byte and spans zones. Put at least
one copy in another region and another account, with object lock so nobody can delete it early. An attacker or a buggy
cleanup script holding the database's credentials should be unable to erase its backups. Encrypt them too: a backup
holds every customer record.

</details>

## After the restore: put the right rows back

If the team notices at 16:10, the shop has taken 30 minutes of new orders since the delete. A restore has two shapes.

- **Rewind.** Restore the database itself to 15:39. Every order placed after the delete disappears, so this suits
  only a total loss, such as dead disks.
- **Restore aside and copy back.** Restore into a side copy, as in the diagram, and copy only the damaged rows into
  the live primary. New orders survive.

Write the copy script with inserts that skip rows already present, as in [idempotency](../idempotency/), because it
will fail halfway at least once.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: more copy-back traps, and the delayed replica</summary>

- **Colliding keys.** If the database reuses IDs, a restored order can clash with a new one. IDs that never repeat, as
  in [unique IDs](../../foundations/unique-ids/), avoid that.
- **Related rows.** If the delete cascaded into order lines and payments, restore those tables in the same pass, in
  foreign key order.
- **Everything downstream.** Caches, search indexes, and analytics saw the delete too. Rebuild them from the restored
  rows.

Some teams keep a **delayed replica** that applies changes an hour late. Stop it within the hour, roll it forward to
15:39, and it becomes a side copy in minutes.

</details>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Ways to recover from a bad write">

| Choice                           | What it buys                                       | What it costs                                                  |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Daily full backups               | Simple restore, one file to load                   | Up to a day of lost writes                                     |
| Full plus hourly incrementals    | Loss of under an hour, small extra storage         | A long chain to apply, and one bad link strands the rest       |
| Snapshot plus archived log       | Restore to any moment, about a minute of loss      | Replay time grows with the gap since the last snapshot         |
| More frequent snapshots          | Shorter replay, better worst case RTO              | More storage and more load on the database while copying       |
| Delayed replica                  | A side copy ready in minutes for recent mistakes   | A whole extra database, useless once the delay window passes   |
| Off site, locked copies          | Survives a lost region, attackers, and bad scripts | Slower restore from far away, and more to pay for              |
| Scheduled restore drills         | Proof that backups load, and a measured RTO        | Machine time and engineer time on every drill                  |

</TradeoffTable>

## Common mistakes

- Counting replicas, RAID, or extra zones as backups. They copy the bad write too.
- Trusting a backup job's exit code without restoring its output.
- Keeping backups under the same account and credentials as the database.
- Rewinding the live primary, which throws away every write since the mistake.
- Setting one RPO and RTO for every dataset.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Replicas copy mistakes within a second. Only a copy from before the mistake can undo it.
- The RPO bounds how much recent data you lose, and the RTO bounds how long until the service works again. Set both per dataset.
- A snapshot plus archived log restores to any moment. Snapshot often enough that replay fits inside the RTO.
- Restore into a side copy and copy back only the damaged rows, so writes made after the mistake survive.
- A backup counts only after a timed restore succeeds. Drill on a schedule and alert on failures.

</KeyTakeaways>

- **Loading alone takes 20 hours.** 10,000 GB ÷ 500 GB per hour, before any replay. Snapshot cadence cannot fix bytes

- **Restore less data at a time.** Split orders into about 32 shards of roughly 310 GB, as in

- **Drill per shard.** Daily, restore one random shard, time it, compare checksums, and alert past 45 minutes.
  Quarterly, restore all shards at once to prove storage and network carry the combined load.

</div>
