## The failure: the answer above the question

A group of five plans dinner. Ana asks "Thai or pizza?" and Ben answers "Thai!" a moment later. On Chen's phone,
"Thai!" appears first, and the question lands below it. Two minutes later Chen rides through a tunnel. When the signal
returns, the chat looks complete, yet the message with the restaurant address never arrives. Nothing on screen says
anything is missing.

Both bugs come from the same place. The server pushes messages down a pipe it does not fully control: several workers
deliver in parallel, and a phone's connection can die without telling anyone.

**25 of 96, about 1 in 4**, land below a message sent after them. Each worker delivers in order, so a stall delays the

## The idea: the server numbers, the phone sorts, the cursor repairs

This case study has one dominant constraint: **every device shows every message of a conversation, in the same order,
even though connections come and go.** The volume is large, yet each message is small and cheap. Order and completeness
are the hard part.

The chat service gives each message a **sequence number** within its conversation, so the order exists in one place. The
phone shows messages in sequence order and holds back any that arrive early, in a **reorder buffer**. Heartbeats detect
sockets that died silently. When a phone reconnects, it sends a **cursor**, the sequence number of the last message it
showed, and the server replays everything after it from the **message log**.

<Analogy>

A serialized novel mailed one chapter at a time. Each envelope carries a chapter number, so if chapter 7 arrives before
chapter 6, you set it aside until 6 shows up. Back from a holiday, you do not trust the mailbox. You tell the publisher
"the last chapter I read was 12", and they send 13 onward from their archive, including any the post office lost.

</Analogy>

## Walk the design checklist

The seven steps from the [design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/), applied to a chat service.

<Steps>

1. **Requirements.** One-to-one and group chats of up to a few hundred members. A message reaches an online reader within
   a second. Every device shows a conversation in the same order. A phone or laptop that was offline catches up with
   everything it missed. Calls, media, search, and end-to-end encryption stay out of scope.

2. **Estimates (illustrative).** Fifty million daily users who send 40 messages each make **2 billion messages a day**,
   about **23,148 per second** on average and **69,444 per second** at a 3× peak. Each message reaches 5 readers on
   average, so the push side handles about **347,222 deliveries per second** at peak. If 40% of users are online at peak,
   that is **20 million open sockets**, and at 100,000 sockets per gateway, **200 gateway servers**. A heartbeat every
   30 s on each socket adds **666,667 heartbeats per second**. At 200 bytes a message, the log grows by about **400 GB a
   day**. (See [requirements and estimates](../../thinking/requirements-and-estimates/) for the method.)

3. **API.** The phone opens a long-lived socket (`wss://chat.example/ws`). It sends
   `send {conversation, clientMessageId, text}` and gets back `ack {sequence}`. The server sends
   `message {conversation, sequence, sender, text}`, and both sides trade `ping` and `pong`. Catch-up uses plain HTTP:
   `GET /conversations/{id}/messages?after=29&limit=100`. Read receipts post `POST /conversations/{id}/read {upTo}`.

4. **Data model.** A `messages` table keyed by `(conversation_id, sequence)`, with the sender, the client message ID
   (unique per sender, to drop resends), the text, and the time. A `members` table holds each member of a conversation
   with the highest sequence they have read. Each device keeps its own cursor per conversation. Shard the log by
   conversation ID (see [sharding](../../scaling/sharding/)), so one conversation's counter lives on one shard.

5. **High-level design.** Gateways hold sockets and know nothing about chats. The chat service numbers messages, writes
   them to the log, and publishes them. Push workers read the topic and route each message to the gateways that hold its
   readers' sockets, using a presence map of who is connected where. A load balancer spreads new sockets across gateways
   by connection count (see [load balancing](../../scaling/load-balancing/)). The Real-time transport lesson compares
   sockets, server-sent events, and long polling, and the Logs and pub/sub lesson covers the topic in the middle.

6. **Deep dive: order and gaps.** This is where the design breaks first, and both simulations below live here. Parallel
   push workers reorder messages, and a socket that died silently swallows them. Sequence numbers fix the first. Cursors
   fix the second.

7. **Trade-offs.** One push path or many, the heartbeat interval, and server-side queues or a replayable log. The table at
   the end of this page lays them out.

</Steps>

## Try it: show a busy group in order

Both lanes receive the same 96 messages over the same four push workers, and the same messages meet the same stalls.
Only the phone differs. The run covers 30 seconds and plays in real time at 1×.

<ChatWidget client:visible duel="ordering" />

What to look for:

- **Busy group.** At 0.9 s message 4 arrives before message 2. The arrival-order phone shows it at once, and **25 of 96**
  messages end up below a message sent after them. The buffered phone shows **0** out of order. It holds **34**
  messages back, **0.7 s** on average and **1.7 s** at most, and its average delay rises from **0.6 s** to **0.8 s**.
- **More stalls.** At 35% stalled, the arrival-order phone shows **46** out of order. The buffer still shows none, and
  holds back more.
- **One worker per chat.** Route each conversation through a single worker, and nothing can overtake. Both lanes show
  **0** out of order with the same **0.8 s** average delay. The single path pays in head-of-line blocking: one stall
  delays every message behind it. Order costs about the same delay either way, so most chat systems pin each
  conversation to one partition and keep a small reorder buffer on the phone for the races that remain.

## Heartbeats and silent disconnects

A phone that enters a tunnel does not hang up. Its radio just stops, so the server's socket stays open and accepts writes
that go nowhere. The gateway learns about it only when something it expects stops arriving. That is the job of the
heartbeat: if no ping arrives within a timeout, the gateway closes the socket and marks the phone offline.

A short timeout finds dead sockets sooner, at a price. At the illustrative scale above, cutting the heartbeat interval
from 30 s to 10 s triples the load to **2 million heartbeats per second** and wakes every phone's radio three times as
often. Any timeout leaves a window in which the server pushes into a socket that is already dead.

## Try it: catch up after a tunnel

Both lanes receive the same 96 messages and lose signal at the same moment. The server queues messages for a phone it
knows is offline, and pushes them when it reconnects. Only the catch-up lane also sends a cursor when it reconnects.

<ChatWidget client:visible duel="sync" />

What to look for:

- **Tunnel.** The phone loses signal at 6 s. The server notices at 9.1 s, after **12 messages** went into the dead
  socket, and queues the rest. At 14.1 s the push lane receives **25 queued messages** and never sees the 12. It shows
  **84 of 96**, and the phone itself has no way to notice; the dashed note above its screen is the simulation pointing
  at the gap. The catch-up phone says the last message it showed is **29**, and the server replays the **37 messages**
  after it, including the 12. It shows all **96**.
- **The average delay.** The push lane averages **1.1 s** and the catch-up lane **1.7 s**. The push lane looks faster
  only because the 12 slowest messages never reach it.
- **Quick heartbeat.** A 1 s timeout cuts the loss to **3 messages**. The window shrinks and stays open, and the cursor
  still recovers every one.
- **Stays online.** No tunnel, no queue, no replay. The lanes match.

A cursor needs a log to read from. Keep each conversation's messages, numbered, for as long as a device might stay
offline, and a phone or laptop of any age can resync with one question. A device asleep longer than the log keeps
messages needs a full resync. When a gateway restarts, its 100,000 phones reconnect at once and all ask for catch-up, so
phones wait a random moment before reconnecting. The Retries with backoff and jitter lesson covers that spread.

## Chat versus notifications

Notifications is the other half of this pair. Both deliver short messages to people's devices, and they guard different
things.

| Question             | Chat                                                  | Notifications                                           |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Who owns delivery?   | Your gateways, over sockets you hold open             | External providers (push services, SMS, email)          |
| What must hold?      | Order within a conversation, and nothing missing      | Arrival before a deadline, within the user's choices    |
| What goes wrong?     | Messages overtake each other or vanish in dead sockets | A provider throttles or fails, and the code expires    |
| The guard            | Sequence numbers and a cursor into a replayable log   | Retries, failover between providers, and deduplication  |
| Late is…             | Fine, if the order is right                           | Often worthless: a login code that arrives late is junk |

Chat systems use notifications too: when the app is closed, the server asks the phone's push service to wake it, and the
phone then opens its socket and catches up with its cursor.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Designing a chat service">

| Choice                               | What it buys                                              | What it costs                                                   |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| One push path per conversation       | Order with no work on the phone                           | One stall delays every message behind it                        |
| Parallel paths and a reorder buffer  | No head-of-line blocking between messages                 | The phone holds messages, and must ask for gaps after a wait    |
| Short heartbeat timeout              | Dead sockets found within seconds                         | Battery, radio wakeups, and heartbeat load on every gateway     |
| Long heartbeat timeout               | Cheap, quiet connections                                  | A longer window of pushes into dead sockets                     |
| Queue on the server per device       | Simple push on reconnect                                  | Storage per device, and anything pushed into a dead socket is gone |
| Per-conversation log and cursors     | Any device resyncs, and lost pushes come back             | Each device tracks cursors, and mass reconnects spike reads     |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

Mia uses chat on her phone and her laptop. She reads a long conversation on the phone during a week away, while the
laptop sleeps. Design the sync so that when the laptop wakes, it shows every message in order, its unread badges match
the phone, and the server keeps no per-device queues.

<div slot="answer">

**Store conversations once, and let each device carry its own cursors.**

- **One log per conversation:** messages live in `messages`, keyed by `(conversation_id, sequence)`, with no copy per
  device. Keep them as long as a device may stay away, say 30 days, and fall back to a full resync after that.
- **A cursor per device per conversation:** the laptop remembers the last sequence it showed in each conversation. On
  wake it asks for everything after each cursor, newest conversations first, 100 messages a page, and shows them in
  sequence order.
- **Read state as a number:** `members.read_up_to` stores the highest sequence Mia has read. The phone raises it as she
  reads, and the laptop fetches it on wake, so unread counts are the difference between the latest sequence and
  `read_up_to`. A higher value always wins, so two devices can never move it backwards.
- **Wake the laptop gently:** while the laptop sleeps, a notification at most nudges it. Once awake it opens a socket and
  syncs, with a random delay so a fleet of laptops waking at 9:00 does not reconnect at the same instant.

</div>

</Challenge>
