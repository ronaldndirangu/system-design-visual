## The failure: one order, two charges

A shopper presses Pay on a $40 order. The payment service asks the bank to charge the card and waits. Two seconds pass
with no reply, so the service gives up on that request and sends a new one. The bank approves the second request at
once, and the order ships. A week later the shopper's statement shows two charges of $40.

The first request had reached the bank and charged the card. Its reply was slow. A timeout tells the service that no
answer arrived in time, and nothing about what the bank did.

## The idea: name every payment, then check the books

This case study has one dominant constraint: **move money exactly once**, even when the network loses requests and
replies. Load is modest. Correctness is the whole job.

Retries can carry one charge to the bank more than once, and after its last timed-out attempt the service may still not
know whether any landed. The fix makes repeats harmless and settles the unknowns later. An **idempotency key** names the purchase, and
every retry carries the same name. The bank stores the key with its result, so a repeat gets the first answer and moves
no money. A **double-entry ledger** records every payment as entries that must balance. **Reconciliation** compares that
ledger with the bank's own records and settles anything the retries could not, once the bank's evidence is final.

## Walk the design checklist

The seven steps from the [design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/), applied to a payment service.

<Steps>

1. **Requirements.** Charge a card for an order, refund it, and show a payment history. The hard bar: every payment moves
   money exactly once, and every charge at the bank matches an entry in the ledger. A payment that takes 3 seconds is
   fine. A payment charged twice becomes a support ticket and a chargeback. Fraud scoring, currency conversion, and
   payouts to sellers stay out of scope.

2. **Estimates (illustrative).** Ten million payments a day average about **116 per second**, and a 5× peak on a sale
   day is about **579 per second**. That load is modest. Each payment writes 2 ledger entries, so **20 million entries a
   day**, and at 200 bytes each that is about **4 GB a day**, kept for years for audits. Keeping each idempotency key for
   a day at 100 bytes takes about **1 GB**. (See [requirements and estimates](../../thinking/requirements-and-estimates/)
   for the method.)

3. **API.** `POST /payments` takes an order, an amount in cents, and a card token, plus an `Idempotency-Key` header. It
   returns the payment with a status of `succeeded`, `failed`, or `pending`. The same key with the same body returns the
   same response. The same key with a different body returns `422`, because it signals a bug in the caller.
   `POST /payments/{id}/refunds` takes a key too.

4. **Data model.** A `payments` table holds the key (unique), the amount, the status, and the bank's reference. A
   `ledger_entries` table is append-only: each row names a payment, an account, a direction (debit or credit), and an
   amount in cents. Balances are sums of entries. An `idempotency_keys` table stores each key with a hash of the request
   and the response to replay. A `refunds` table holds each refund with its own key, amount, and status.

5. **High-level design.** The checkout page calls the payment service, which writes to the ledger database and calls the
   bank. Bank webhooks and status lookups by key move a pending payment to its final state. A separate reconciler runs on
   a schedule, reads the bank's settlement file, and compares it with the ledger.

6. **Deep dive: the retry.** This is where the design breaks first, and the simulations below live here. After a timeout
   there are three possible worlds: the request was lost, the bank declined, or the bank charged and the reply was lost.
   Only the bank knows which. A retry with the same key asks the bank to answer from its key store, so all three worlds
   end with one charge at most. The [Idempotency](../../reliability/idempotency/) lesson covers key stores in depth, and [Retries with backoff and jitter](../../reliability/retries/)
   covers spacing the attempts. When every attempt times out, the payment stays unknown until the bank reports a final
   state.

7. **Trade-offs.** Timeout length, how long to keep keys, how often to reconcile, and what to show the shopper while a
   payment is pending. The table at the end of this page lays them out.

</Steps>

## The ledger

A payment service keeps its own record of every cent, separate from the bank's. Two rules make that record trustworthy.

- **Double entry.** Every payment writes at least two entries whose debits equal their credits, so the whole ledger sums
  to zero. A missing or extra entry breaks the sum, and a check can find it.
- **Append only.** No entry is ever edited or deleted. A refund writes a new pair of entries in the opposite direction,
  so the history shows both what happened and how it was corrected.

| Entry | Payment | Account                                    | Debit | Credit |
| ----- | ------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----- | ------ |
| 1     | 71      | Card network receivable (money on its way) | $40   |        |
| 2     | 71      | Merchant payable (money owed to the shop)  |       | $40    |
| 3     | 71      | Merchant payable (refund of $15)           | $15   |        |
| 4     | 71      | Card network receivable (refund of $15)    |       | $15    |

Write both entries of a pair in one database transaction (the [Transactions and constraints](../../foundations/transactions/) lesson covers this), and
store amounts as integer cents so rounding never creates or destroys money.

## Reconcile with the bank

Keys stop double charges. They cannot help when every attempt times out: after 3 tries the service still does not know
whether the bank charged the card. Both lanes below retry with a key, so neither one charges twice. Only one of them
reconciles every 10 s. It reads the bank's settlement file, then looks up by key each unknown payment the file does not
show yet. A lookup marks a payment failed only once the bank's answer is final.

<PaymentsWidget client:visible duel="reconcile" />

What to look for:

- **Bank outage.** From 8 s to 16 s the bank answers slowly and drops requests. **6 payments** give up after 3
  attempts. Trusting the ledger, they stay unknown, and the bank holds **$195** that the ledger never recorded. At 20 s
  the reconciling lane finds **4 charges** in the bank's file and completes those payments. A status lookup confirms
  that the bank never charged **1** payment, so the service marks it failed. The last payment stays unknown, because the
  bank still reports it as processing and the file has no line for it yet. At 30 s the file shows that charge too, and
  the gap closes to **$0**.
- **Calm day.** No outage, no unknown payments, and the lanes match. Reconciliation finds nothing, and that silence has
  value: it proves the books balance.

The toy folds two jobs into one pass every 10 s. Real systems run them apart, on different clocks:

- **Status updates settle each outcome within minutes.** The bank pushes a webhook when a payment reaches a final state.
  The service checks the webhook's signature, drops repeats by event ID, and applies each update once. For a payment
  still pending after a few minutes, a status checker asks the bank by idempotency key. A "still processing" answer
  leaves the payment pending, and only a final answer marks it paid or failed.
- **Settlement reconciliation checks the money.** Once a day the reconciler matches the bank's settlement file against
  the ledger, line by line. Files arrive late and sometimes partial, so a missing line opens an investigation and never
  fails a payment on its own.

A payment that spans several services (reserve stock, charge the card, book shipping) also needs a plan for undoing the
earlier steps when a later one fails. The [Sagas](../../distributed-data/sagas/) lesson covers that, and [Delivery semantics and the outbox](../../async/delivery-semantics/) covers sending
the "payment succeeded" event exactly when the ledger commits.

## Refunds without double counting

A refund moves money back to the shopper, and it fails the same way a charge does. A support agent clicks Refund $40 on
a $100 payment. The request times out, so the agent clicks again. An hour later a second agent refunds $70 on the same
payment. Each click should refund once, and the shopper should never get back more than $100.

One counter on the payment looks like enough:
`UPDATE payments SET refunded = refunded + 4000 WHERE id = 64 AND refunded + 4000 <= amount`. It stops the $70 refund,
and it cannot tell a repeat from a new refund. The agent's second click runs the same statement, so the counter records
$80 while the bank sent back $40. A later, legitimate $30 refund then gets refused. The counter also moves before the
bank answers, so a declined refund still counts against the payment.

Give each refund its own row and key instead, and reserve its amount in the transaction that creates the row.

- **One key per intended refund.** The refund form creates the key when it opens, so a second click on the same form
  sends the same key. A new refund opens a new form with a new key.
- **Claim and reserve together.** One transaction inserts the refund and reserves its amount on the payment:

  ```sql
  BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO refunds (payment_id, refund_key, amount, status)
  VALUES (64, 'rf_8c2e', 4000, 'pending')
  ON CONFLICT (payment_id, refund_key) DO NOTHING
  RETURNING id;
  UPDATE payments SET refund_reserved = refund_reserved + 4000
  WHERE id = 64 AND refund_reserved + 4000 <= amount;
  COMMIT;
  ```

  If the insert returns no row, the click is a repeat: the service skips the update and returns the refund it already
  stored. If the update changes zero rows, the refund would pass the payment's amount: the service rolls back, which
  removes the new refund row too, and returns `422`. The $70 refund ends there, since $40 reserved plus $70 is more than
  $100. Two refunds racing on one payment queue on the payment row, and the second checks the condition against the
  first one's total. This is the conditional write that stops [ticketing](../ticketing/) from selling a seat twice.
- **Three states.** A refund starts `pending`. After the commit, the service asks the bank to refund $40 and passes the
  refund's id as the bank's idempotency key, so every retry of this refund reaches the same refund at the bank.
- **Settle once, with the entries.** When the bank confirms, one transaction runs
  `UPDATE refunds SET status = 'succeeded' WHERE id = 9 AND status = 'pending'` and writes the two reversing entries,
  like entries 3 and 4 in the ledger table above. A repeated webhook or a second status lookup changes zero rows, so the
  service skips the entries, and a unique constraint on the refund and the account rejects them if a bug tries anyway.
- **Release on a final failure.** A final decline marks the refund `failed` and subtracts its 4000 cents from
  `refund_reserved` in the same transaction, so that money can be refunded later. A timeout is no answer: the refund
  stays pending, keeps its reservation, and settles through a webhook, a status lookup by key, or the settlement file,
  the same path a payment takes.

Reserving before the bank call errs on the safe side. While a refund is pending, a new refund sees less room than the
payment may end up having, and never more.

## Failure walkthrough: the service crashes after the bank charges

At 14:00:00 Leo pays $40 for order 8812, and his checkout page sends key `pay_5f1a`. The payment service sends the
charge to the bank and dies before the reply arrives. Four parts of the design keep the money and the books in step.

1. **The intent is on disk before the bank call.** The service commits payment 72 as pending, which claims `pay_5f1a`
   with a 30 s lease. Then it asks the bank to charge $40 with the same key. The bank charges the card at 14:00:01, and
   the service process crashes before the reply reaches it. The bank holds $40, and the ledger has a pending payment
   with no entries.
2. **The retry waits out the claim, then takes it over.** Leo's page retries at 14:00:05 and gets `409`, because the
   dead process still holds the lease. At 14:00:30 the lease lapses, and the next retry takes over the claim with a new
   fencing token (the [Idempotency](../../reliability/idempotency/) lesson covers expired claims). It asks the bank
   again with `pay_5f1a`. The bank answers from its key store with the charge from 14:00:01 and moves no money.
3. **One transaction settles the payment.** The two ledger entries and
   `UPDATE payments SET status = 'succeeded' WHERE id = 72 AND status = 'pending'` commit together. A late webhook for
   the same charge changes zero rows, so the service writes no second pair. If the first process had only paused and
   wakes up with the bank's reply, its update also changes zero rows.
4. **Without a retry, the checker settles it.** Had Leo closed the tab, nobody would retry. At 14:05 the status checker
   finds payment 72 still pending, asks the bank by key, hears "succeeded", and runs the same transaction as step 3.
   The next morning the settlement file lists the $40 charge, and the reconciler ticks it off.

Each guard covers one gap. Without the pending row, nothing tells the checker to ask. With a fresh key on the retry, Leo
pays twice. With the entries outside the status update's transaction, a second confirmation books $40 twice.

## Payments versus ticketing

[Flash-sale ticketing](../ticketing/) is the other half of this pair. Both protect something scarce, and they fail in
opposite ways.

| Question          | Payments                                              | Flash-sale ticketing                           |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| What is scarce?   | Money in one account, touched by few writers          | 10,000 seats, the same rows for everyone       |
| Who collides?     | One request with its own retry                        | Many strangers on the same seat                |
| What goes wrong?  | One payment runs twice after a timeout and retry      | Two buyers claim one seat at the same instant  |
| The guard         | An idempotency key and a ledger that must balance     | A conditional write on the seat row            |
| Undoing a mistake | Moved money needs a refund, a new entry in the ledger | A hold expires and the seat goes back on sale  |

Both fixes end with one authority deciding: the seat row for a ticket, the bank's key store for a charge. Every other
attempt learns the first answer.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Designing a payment service">

| Choice                           | What it buys                                           | What it costs                                              |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Long timeout                     | Fewer false timeouts and fewer retries                 | Shoppers wait longer when the bank is stuck                |
| Short timeout                    | Fast feedback for the shopper                          | More retries, and without a key, more double charges       |
| Idempotency key on every write   | Safe retries from the page, the service, and the bank  | A key store to run, and keys expire (24 h is common)       |
| Double-entry, append-only ledger | Every cent traceable, and mistakes fixed in the open   | More rows, and balances need sums or snapshots             |
| Webhooks plus status lookups     | Unknown payments settle within minutes                 | Signatures, deduplication, and lookups to run              |
| Nightly settlement file          | A simple batch job that checks the money               | A day of lag, and a missing line proves nothing            |
| Show "pending" to the shopper    | Honest while the outcome is unknown                    | A follow-up email or screen once the payment settles       |

</TradeoffTable>

<KeyTakeaways>

- A timeout means the outcome is unknown. Retry with the same key, and keep the payment pending until the bank gives a
  final answer.
- The idempotency key names the purchase, and the bank honors it, so every retry gets the first attempt's answer and
  moves no more money.
- Write the pending intent before calling the bank, then settle it from evidence: webhooks, status lookups by key, and
  the settlement file.
- The ledger is double entry and append only. A refund is its own keyed record that reserves its amount before any
  money moves, and it settles with its reversing entries in one transaction.

</KeyTakeaways>

**Closing rubric: grow one dimension tenfold.** Keep the estimates from the design checklist (a 5× peak, 2 ledger
entries of 200 bytes per payment, and keys kept for a day at 100 bytes). Suppose the ledger database sustains 10,000
writes per second, and each payment costs about 5 row writes: the key, the pending payment, its status update, and 2
entries. Raise payments from 10 million to 100 million a day.

1. What are the new peak payment rate, the ledger write rate, and the daily storage?
2. What do you change, and what does the change cost?
3. For 60 s at the new peak, every bank call times out. What happens to those payments, and how long does cleanup take?

<div slot="answer">

**The ledger database runs out of writes first.**

- **The new numbers.** 100 million payments a day average about 1,157 per second, and the 5× peak is about 5,787. At 5
  row writes each, the peak needs about 28,935 writes per second, 2.9 times what the database sustains. Today's peak
  is about 2,895. Entries grow to 200 million a day, about 40 GB a day and 14.6 TB a year. A day of keys takes about
  10 GB.
- **The change.** [Shard](../../scaling/sharding/) the ledger database by idempotency key across 4 databases, about
  7,234 writes per second each. A payment's key, row, refunds, and entries live on one shard, so each settlement stays
  one local transaction, and the payment id encodes its shard so refunds and lookups find it. The cost: a merchant's
  balance now sums entries from 4 shards, and adding a fifth shard later means moving rows.
- **The crash.** In 60 s at 5,787 per second, about 347,222 payments time out. Blind retries, 3 attempts each, would
  send about 17,361 calls per second into a struggling bank. A [retry budget](../../reliability/retries/) of 10%
  allows about 579 retries per second, and the rest stay pending, so shoppers see "pending" and nobody pays twice.
  Once the bank recovers, webhooks settle most of them, and a status checker at 1,000 lookups per second clears the
  whole backlog in about 6 minutes.
