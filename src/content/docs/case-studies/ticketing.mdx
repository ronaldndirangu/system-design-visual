## The failure: two buyers, one seat

A band sells 10,000 seats at 10:00 sharp. A million fans have the page open. Ana's request reads the seat map and sees
A10 free. A millisecond later Ben's request reads the same map, and A10 is still free, because Ana's write has not landed
yet. Both servers write their buyer into A10, both send a confirmation email, and on the night two people walk up to the
same seat holding valid tickets.

Nothing crashed. Each server did exactly what its code said. The bug lives in the gap between reading "free" and writing
"mine", and a million people make that gap crowded.

## The idea: let the database settle every race

This case study has one dominant constraint: **never sell one seat twice**, while a million people press Buy in the same
minute. Everything else (the waiting room, holds, the cached seat map) exists to make that one rule cheap to keep.

The fix moves the check into the write. A **conditional write** says "put Ana in A10, but only if A10 has nobody". The
database checks and writes in one step, so when Ben's claim arrives it changes zero rows, and his server tells him to
pick again.

<Analogy>

Two people spot the last free chair in a busy cafe. Read, then write is looking across the room, deciding the chair is
free, walking over, and sitting down on whoever got there first. A conditional write is putting your bag on the chair only
if no bag is on it yet, in one motion. A hold that expires is the cafe clearing any bag left alone for ten minutes.

</Analogy>

## Walk the design checklist

The seven steps from the [design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/), applied to a flash sale.

<Steps>

1. **Requirements.** Fans browse a seat map, hold a seat, pay within a time limit, and receive a ticket. The hard bar is
   correctness: no seat is ever sold twice. The site also stays up through the rush and serves fans roughly in arrival
   order. Resale and dynamic pricing stay out of scope.

2. **Estimates (illustrative).** One million people for 10,000 seats is 100 buyers per seat. If most arrive in the first
   minute and each makes about 6 requests (the seat map, a hold or two, checkout), the peak is about **100,000 requests
   per second**, and about **16,667 hold attempts per second** land on just 10,000 rows. At 100 bytes per seat, the
   whole seat table is about 1,000 KB, small enough to fit in memory many times over. Size is easy. Contention is the
   problem. (See [requirements and estimates](../../thinking/requirements-and-estimates/) for the method.)

3. **API.** `GET /events/{id}/seats` returns the seat map and can be a second stale. `POST /holds` takes an event and a
   seat and returns a hold with its expiry time, or `409 Conflict` when someone else got there first.
   `POST /orders` takes a hold and a payment token, with an `Idempotency-Key` header so a retried checkout cannot buy
   twice (the [Idempotency](../../reliability/idempotency/) lesson covers keys in depth). It claims the hold before it
   charges the card, and answers `409 Conflict` when the hold has already lapsed.

4. **Data model.** A `seats` table keyed by (event, seat) holds the status (`available`, `held`, `paying` or `sold`),
   the holder, and the deadline for the current hold. An `orders` table records each paid ticket, with a unique
   constraint on (event, seat) as a final guard.

5. **High-level design.** Fans pass through a waiting room, then a load balancer, to stateless app servers. The servers
   read the seat map from a cache and send every claim to the seat database.

6. **Deep dive: the claim.** This is where the design breaks first, and the simulations below live here. Three tools
   make the database reject a second claim. A conditional write:
   `UPDATE seats SET status = 'held', holder = ?, expires = now() + 8 min WHERE event = ? AND seat = ? AND (status =
   'available' OR (status IN ('held', 'paying') AND expires < now())))`, then check how many rows changed. A sold seat
   matches neither branch, so no claim can take it back. A row lock: `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE` inside a transaction, then
   the write. A unique constraint that refuses a second order row for the same seat. The [Transactions and
   constraints](../../foundations/transactions/) lesson covers all three, and [Holds that expire](#holds-that-expire)
   adds the checkout steps.

7. **Trade-offs.** Hold length, a waiting room or open doors, and pick your seat or best available. The table at the
   end of this page lays them out.

</Steps>

## Try it: claim a seat

Both lanes face the same 600 buyers, who arrive at the same moments and reach for the same seats in the same order. Only
the write differs. Times are compressed: the whole rush takes seconds here and minutes in real life.

<TicketingWidget client:visible duel="oversell" />

What to look for:

- **Doors open.** Read, then write issues **243 tickets for 100 seats**, and **70 seats** go to two or more buyers. The
  conditional write issues exactly 100 and rejects **715 writes**, each one followed by a fresh read and another try.
- **Correctness costs load.** Those retries push the conditional lane to a peak of **1,702 database queries per
  second**, against 411 for read, then write. The race turns from a correctness bug into a capacity problem, and
  capacity problems have known fixes.
- **Add a waiting room.** Letting in 20 buyers per second drops the conditional lane's peak to **52 queries per second**
  and its rejected writes to 14. Read, then write still promises **10 seats** twice. A waiting room makes collisions
  rarer. Only the conditional write rules them out.
- **Quiet test day.** With one buyer every 0.3 s nobody's reads overlap, and both lanes sell the same 52 tickets. This is
  why the bug passes every test and appears on launch day.

## Holds that expire

A buyer who claims a seat gets a **hold**: the seat is theirs while they type a card number. Some buyers never finish.
They close the tab, their card fails, or they change their mind. If a hold lasts until the buyer pays, an abandoned seat
stays locked for the rest of the sale while people in line are told it is sold out.

So every hold gets a deadline, and the deadline binds every buyer, including one whose payment is still at the bank. A
hold is a lease, and [Time, leases, and fencing](../../distributed-data/time-leases-fencing/) explains why the check
belongs in the write. A seat moves through a small state machine, and each move is one conditional write on its row:

1. **Held.** The claim from step 6 sets `status = 'held'`, the holder, and a deadline.
2. **Paying.** When the buyer presses Pay, the server claims the hold before it charges anything:
   `UPDATE seats SET status = 'paying', expires = now() + 10 min WHERE event = ? AND seat = ? AND holder = ? AND
   status = 'held' AND expires > now()`. One row changed means this checkout owns the seat, with a fresh deadline that
   covers a slow bank. Zero rows means the hold already lapsed, so the buyer picks again before any money moves.
3. **Sold.** The bank authorizes the card. One transaction then inserts the order row and marks the seat sold, only if
   the claim still stands (`WHERE holder = ? AND status = 'paying' AND expires > now()`). The server captures the money
   after that commit.
4. **Expired.** A hold or claim past its deadline goes back on sale, and the next claim from step 6 can take it. When
   the bank approves after that, the conditional write changes zero rows, and the server voids the authorization (or
   refunds a captured charge). The buyer gets an apology instead of a ticket for someone else's seat.

Both lanes below use conditional writes, so neither one sells a seat twice. They face the same 400 buyers, who press Pay
or walk away at the same moments. Only the hold rules differ.

<TicketingWidget client:visible duel="holds" />

What to look for:

- **Some buyers walk away.** With holds that never expire, **73 tickets** sell and **27 seats** end the run held for
  buyers who already left, while **300 buyers** hear "sold out". Expiring holds after 8 s releases **40 holds**, sells
  **97 tickets**, and turns away 260. The 3 seats still stuck at the end belong to buyers who left less than 8 s before
  the run ended.
- **Everyone pays.** Both lanes sell all 100 seats and the curves match. Every buyer presses Pay within 6 s and the bank
  approves at once, so no 8 s hold runs out.
- **Slow bank approvals.** Now the bank takes 1 to 5 s per payment. When holds simply expire after 8 s, **61 holds**
  lapse while their payments are still at the bank. Those buyers lose their seats, **59 approvals** arrive too late and
  are voided, and only **90 tickets** sell by the end of the run. When Pay claims the hold and extends it to 10 s, every
  approval lands inside its deadline: **100 tickets** and no voids.

Real holds last 5 to 10 minutes, and the claim's deadline should cover the payment provider's slowest normal answer. The
claim still needs a deadline: a checkout that hangs forever strands its seat exactly like a hold that never expires.
[Payments](../payments/) covers what to do when the bank never answers at all.

## The waiting room

The waiting room is a queue in front of the whole site. It admits fans at the rate the servers can handle, in the order
they arrived, and shows each one their place in line. It does three jobs at once:

- **Protects the database.** Load stays flat at the admission rate, however many people arrive. The [Queues and
  backpressure](../../reliability/queues-and-backpressure/) lesson covers this pattern.
- **Keeps it fair.** Fans are served in arrival order, so the fastest retry loop no longer wins. Pair it with the
  techniques from the [Rate limiting](../../reliability/rate-limiting/) lesson to slow down bots.
- **Shrinks the race.** Fewer buyers read the map at the same moment, so fewer claims collide.

It cannot guarantee one buyer per seat. Two admitted fans can still read the same free seat, so the conditional write
stays.

## Ticketing versus payments

[Payments](../payments/) is the other half of this pair. Both protect something scarce, and they fail in opposite ways.

| Question          | Flash-sale ticketing                           | Payments                                              |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| What is scarce?   | 10,000 seats, the same rows for everyone       | Money in one account, touched by few writers          |
| What goes wrong?  | Two buyers claim one seat at the same instant  | One payment runs twice after a timeout and retry      |
| The guard         | A conditional write on the seat row            | An idempotency key and a ledger that must balance     |
| Undoing a mistake | A hold expires and the seat goes back on sale  | Moved money needs a refund, a new entry in the ledger |

A seat hold can lapse cheaply because no money has been captured yet: at worst the server voids an authorization. Once a
payment is captured, nothing expires: the only way back is a second transaction that moves the money home.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Designing a flash sale">

| Choice                             | What it buys                                       | What it costs                                                 |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Conditional write per seat         | No double sales, one round trip per claim          | Losers retry, so collisions turn into extra load              |
| Row lock (`SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`) | Same guarantee, easy to extend to several rows     | Locks stay held for the whole transaction and queue up others |
| Unique constraint on (event, seat) | The database enforces it even if the code forgets  | Fails late, after the buyer thinks they won                   |
| Short holds                        | Abandoned seats return quickly                     | Slow payers lose their seat mid-checkout                      |
| Long holds                         | Payers have time                                   | Abandoned seats sit idle while buyers hear "sold out"         |
| Claim the hold when Pay is pressed | A checkout keeps its seat while the bank works     | One more conditional write, and a void for any late approval  |
| Waiting room                       | Flat load, arrival order, fewer collisions         | Everyone waits, and it is one more system to run              |
| Assign the best available seat     | Spreads claims across seats, so fewer collisions   | Buyers lose the choice of exactly where to sit                |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

A festival sells 50,000 standing tickets with no seat numbers to 2 million fans. There is no seat map, just a count of
tickets left. Where does contention move, and how do you avoid selling ticket 50,001?

<div slot="answer">

**The counter becomes the one hot row.** Every purchase decrements the same `tickets_left` value, so every claim in the
country waits on one row.

- **Keep the conditional write:** `UPDATE inventory SET left = left - 1 WHERE event = ? AND left > 0`, and treat zero
  rows changed as sold out. That alone rules out ticket 50,001.
- **Split the hot row:** store the 50,000 tickets as 50 bucket rows of 1,000 each, pick a bucket at random, and move to
  another when one empties. Claims now spread across 50 rows, the same idea as [sharding](../../scaling/sharding/) a
  hot key.
- **Keep the waiting room and holds:** admit fans at the rate the buckets can serve, and expire holds so abandoned
  checkouts return tickets to a bucket.

</div>

</Challenge>
