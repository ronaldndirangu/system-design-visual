## The failure: a train, three tunnels, one fixed file

A commuter starts a long video on the train. The app serves it as one fixed 1080p encode at 5 Mbps. In the open, the phone
gets about 5 to 8 Mbps, enough to stay ahead. Then the train enters a tunnel and the connection falls to about 1 Mbps.
Three tunnels lie along the first 90 s of the ride, at 20 to 30 s, 46 to 58 s, and 72 to 82 s.

The player keeps a **buffer**: video it has downloaded but not yet shown. While the network outruns the bitrate, the
buffer grows. When the network falls behind, playback drains the buffer, and when the buffer hits zero the picture
freezes behind a spinner. That freeze is a **stall**, and the time spent frozen is **rebuffering**. Viewers hate it more
than a blurry picture.

## The constraint: bytes

Most designs in this course count requests. Video counts bytes, and the bandwidth to move them decides every major
choice:

- **Upload.** A large file over a consumer connection often fails partway. The upload has to pick up where it stopped.
- **Storage.** Every minute of video exists as one source file plus several encodes. Storage grows every day.
- **Playback.** The player must keep its buffer ahead of the playhead on a network it cannot control: a carrier, a
  café router, a train.
- **Egress.** Every byte sent to a viewer costs money. At scale, the delivery bill can dwarf the cost of storing the
  video.

The rest of this lesson walks the [design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/).

## Requirements

**Functional**

- Creators upload videos of up to a few gigabytes from a phone or a browser.
- An upload survives a dropped connection or an app restart without starting over.
- Each upload becomes a ladder of resolutions and bitrates, and the video goes live when it is ready.
- Viewers press play and see a picture quickly, on any network from fiber to a moving train.
- The player adjusts quality to the network without the viewer touching a setting.

**Non-functional** (illustrative targets)

- First frame within about 2 s on a decent connection.
- Stalls rare enough that viewers spend well under 1% of watch time rebuffering.
- A completed upload is never lost.
- A typical upload is ready to watch within minutes.
- Delivery cost per view stays flat or falls as views grow.

## Estimates

These numbers are illustrative. Suppose creators upload 50,000 videos a day, 8 minutes each, recorded at 10 Mbps. The
ladder has five rungs, from 240p at 0.4 Mbps to 1080p at 5 Mbps. At peak, 1 million people watch at once, at an average
of 3 Mbps.

| Quantity                                            | Illustrative value                           |
| --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Source file per upload (8 min × 60 s × 10 Mbps ÷ 8) | 600 MB                                       |
| Uploads per day (50,000 × 600 MB)                   | 30 TB, about 2.78 Gbps averaged over the day |
| Ladder bitrates summed (0.4 + 0.8 + 1.4 + 2.8 + 5)  | 10.4 Mbps                                    |
| Ladder storage per minute of video (10.4 × 60 ÷ 8)  | 78 MB                                        |
| Minutes uploaded per day (50,000 × 8)               | 400,000                                      |
| Ladder storage per day (400,000 × 78 MB)            | 31.2 TB                                      |
| New storage per day (sources plus ladder)           | 61.2 TB                                      |
| New storage per year (61.2 TB × 365)                | about 22.3 PB                                |
| Peak egress (1 million viewers × 3 Mbps)            | 3,000 Gbps                                   |
| Peak egress ÷ average upload rate                   | about 1,080×                                 |

Two things stand out. First, the ladder weighs about as much as the sources: 31.2 TB of encodes on top of 30 TB of
uploads every day, and none of it goes away on its own. Cheap storage per gigabyte matters more here than fast storage.
Second, the traffic is lopsided. Peak egress is about 1,080 times the average upload rate. Uploads are a storage and
processing problem; playback is a bandwidth problem, and bandwidth is where the money goes.

## API

```text
POST /videos  {title, sizeBytes}
     returns { videoId, uploadId, partSize, parts }
POST /videos/{videoId}/parts/{n}/url
     returns { url, expiresAt }
PUT  {url}  (one part's bytes, sent straight to object storage)
     returns { etag }
GET  /videos/{videoId}/parts
     returns [ { n, etag } ] for every part that arrived
POST /videos/{videoId}/complete  {parts: [ { n, etag } ]}
     returns 202 Accepted
GET  /videos/{videoId}
     returns { status, manifestUrl }

GET  {cdn}/videos/{videoId}/master.m3u8
     returns the master manifest: one playlist per rung
GET  {cdn}/videos/{videoId}/720p/seg_00012.ts
     returns 4 s of video at 720p
```

The status moves from uploading to processing to ready, or to failed. The API server never touches video bytes. It hands
out pre-signed URLs, reports which parts arrived, and queues the transcode once the client calls complete. The complete
call carries the part list so the server can check every part before processing starts, and a client that calls it
twice after a timeout must not start two transcodes. The [Idempotency](../../reliability/idempotency/) lesson covers that guarantee, and the [APIs and
contracts](../../foundations/apis/) lesson covers how to evolve endpoints like these.

## Data model

| Table        | Key                | Holds                                                        |
| ------------ | ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| `videos`     | `video_id`         | Owner, title, status, duration, source key, manifest key     |
| `uploads`    | `upload_id`        | Video id, part size, parts received with their ETags, expiry |
| `renditions` | `(video_id, rung)` | Resolution, bitrate, playlist key, segment count, status     |

These rows are small, and they hold facts about the video. The video itself lives in object storage under keys like
these:

```text
sources/{videoId}/original.mp4
videos/{videoId}/master.m3u8
videos/{videoId}/720p/playlist.m3u8
videos/{videoId}/720p/seg_00012.ts
```

**Object storage** is a different kind of store from the databases in earlier lessons:

- **Buckets and keys.** A bucket is a named container, and a key is a path-like string inside it. You `PUT` an object
  under a key and `GET` it back by that key. The slashes are only part of the name; there are no folders, rows, or
  indexes.
- **Whole, immutable objects.** You write an object in one go and replace it whole. You cannot edit a few bytes in the
  middle. Reads can ask for a byte range, which players use for some formats.
- **Cheap and durable per gigabyte.** The service either copies each object to several disks or splits it with erasure
  coding into fragments plus parity, spread across machines, so a dead disk or a dead machine loses nothing. A terabyte
  here costs far less than a terabyte on database disks.
- **Slow per request, with no queries.** Each request carries far more latency than a lookup in a well-indexed database,
  and you cannot ask "which videos belong to this creator?" That question goes to the metadata database.
- **New content, new key.** Because objects never change, a new encode gets new keys. A cache can keep a segment for as
  long as it likes and never serve a stale copy.

The [Data modeling and storage choice](../../foundations/data-modeling/) lesson compares these stores in depth. The rule of thumb for this design: put the
bytes in object storage and the facts about the bytes in a database.

## High-level design

The design has two paths that meet at object storage. The write path takes bytes in: the creator's app uploads parts
straight to object storage, and a queue of transcode jobs turns each upload into a ladder of segments. The read path
sends bytes out: a viewer's player fetches a manifest and then segments, mostly from CDN edges near the viewer. The API
server and the metadata database sit to the side and handle small requests about the video, never the video itself.

## Deep dive: adaptive bitrate playback

### Segments and the ladder

The transcoder cuts each video into short **segments**, 4 s each in this lesson's model, and encodes every segment at
every rung of a **bitrate ladder**:

| Rung  | Bitrate (illustrative) | Bits per 4 s segment |
| ----- | ---------------------- | -------------------- |
| 240p  | 0.4 Mbps               | 1.6 Mb               |
| 360p  | 0.8 Mbps               | 3.2 Mb               |
| 480p  | 1.4 Mbps               | 5.6 Mb               |
| 720p  | 2.8 Mbps               | 11.2 Mb              |
| 1080p | 5 Mbps                 | 20 Mb                |

A **manifest** lists the rungs and the URL of every segment at every rung. HLS calls it a playlist (`.m3u8`), and DASH
calls it an MPD. The player fetches the manifest first, then fetches segments one at a time over plain HTTP, choosing a
rung for each. Plain HTTP matters: every segment is an ordinary file that any CDN can cache.

### Why segment boundaries line up

Most frames in compressed video store only what changed since earlier frames. A **keyframe** stores a full picture that
needs nothing before it. The encoder places a keyframe at the start of every segment, at the same timestamps in every
rung, so segment 12 at 360p covers exactly the same 4 s as segment 12 at 1080p and opens with a full picture. That
alignment lets the player switch rungs between any two segments: it can play segment 11 at 1080p and segment 12 at
360p, and the picture just gets softer. Without aligned keyframes, a switch would need frames from a rung the player
never downloaded.

### The buffer race

Playback drains the buffer at one second of video per second. Downloads refill it at a rate set by the network and the
rung. Take the tunnel's 1 Mbps:

- A 1080p segment is 4 s × 5 Mbps = 20 Mb. It takes 20 s to arrive, so the buffer gains 0.2 s of video per second of
  wall time while playback spends 1 s. The buffer shrinks every second.
- A 360p segment is 4 s × 0.8 Mbps = 3.2 Mb. It takes 3.2 s to arrive, so the buffer gains 1.25 s per second. The
  buffer grows, slowly, even in the tunnel.

When the buffer reaches zero, playback stalls until enough video arrives to resume. In the toy model, playback starts or
resumes once 4 s (one segment) is buffered, and the player stops fetching once 20 s is buffered.

### A simple adaptive rule

The adaptive player in this lesson follows a deliberately simple rule:

```text
for each segment:
  if nothing is measured yet, or less than 4 s is buffered:
      fetch the lowest rung (240p)
  else:
      estimate = average throughput of the last 3 downloads
      fetch the highest rung at or below 0.8 × estimate

while a segment downloads:
  speed = this download's average speed so far
  if it will not finish before the buffer runs dry,
  and the highest lower rung within 0.8 × speed would arrive sooner:
      drop it and fetch the same segment at that rung
```

The 0.8 is a safety margin. It leaves room for the network to dip a little without draining the buffer. Throughput is
measured per download, as the segment's size divided by the time it took, so a slow download counts only once it
finishes. That makes the estimate lag: the rule notices a tunnel late, and it notices open air late too. The buffer is
what carries the player through the lag, and the drop rule is its emergency brake.

Real players are more refined. Buffer-based algorithms such as BOLA choose rungs mainly from how full the buffer is,
and hybrid algorithms blend measured throughput with buffer level and look ahead. Real players also avoid switching too
often, because a picture that keeps changing sharpness annoys viewers. The toy rule is enough to show the core idea.

## Deep dive: uploads that survive a dropped connection

A creator uploads a 2 GB video from a phone on a shaky connection. With one long `PUT`, a drop throws away everything
sent so far, and the upload starts again from byte zero. On a connection that drops every few minutes, a big file may
never finish at all.

### Multipart upload

Multipart upload splits the file into numbered parts and sends each part as its own request:

1. **Initiate.** The client asks the API server to start an upload. The server opens a multipart upload in object
   storage and returns the upload id with a part plan.
2. **Upload parts.** For each part, the client gets a pre-signed URL and sends the part straight to object storage.
   Several parts travel at once, which also uses a fast connection better than one stream. Each successful part
   returns an ETag, a fingerprint of its bytes.
3. **Complete.** The client sends the list of part numbers and ETags. Object storage joins the parts into one object,
   and the API server queues the transcode.

When the connection drops, only the parts in flight fail. The client asks which parts arrived and retries only the
missing ones. If the app restarts, it resumes from the same list. Retries should wait a little longer each time and add
random jitter, so that thousands of phones coming back online after an outage do not all retry in the same instant. The
[Retries with backoff and jitter](../../reliability/retries/) lesson covers that pattern. Object storage also needs a cleanup rule that deletes parts
of uploads nobody completed, or abandoned parts pile up and cost money forever.

### Pre-signed URLs

A **pre-signed URL** is a storage URL with a signature attached. The API server creates it with its own credentials. It
allows one specific action, such as "upload part 7 of this upload", and expires after a short time. The client uses it
directly, so the video bytes travel from the phone to object storage without passing through the API servers. Those
servers stay small and cheap: they answer tiny JSON requests while object storage, built for bulk bytes, takes the heavy
traffic.

What to look for:

- **One drop costs a single request half the file.** With 3 drops, one request sends 5,000 MB to deliver 2,000 MB:
  3,000 MB wasted across 4 attempts. With 5 drops it sends 7,000 MB.
- **Multipart loses only the parts in flight.** With 3 drops and 100 MB parts, it sends 2,600 MB: 12 retried parts and
  600 MB wasted, across 34 requests.
- **Smaller parts waste less and cost more requests.** At 25 MB parts the waste falls to 150 MB, and the upload takes
  94 requests. Every request has overhead, and storage services usually charge per request.
- **Huge parts lose the benefit.** At 500 MB, 4 parts cover the whole file, so all of them are in flight at once and
  each drop wastes as much as it does for one request.
- **With no drops, both send exactly 2,000 MB.** Multipart only adds requests: 22 against 1.

## Deep dive: transcoding into a ladder

When the client calls complete, the API server marks the video processing and puts a job on the transcode queue.
Encoding is slow and CPU heavy, so it runs on its own fleet of workers, far from the request path. A creator waits
minutes for the video to go live; nobody waits on an HTTP request for it.

### Split, encode, and stitch

- **Split.** A worker probes the source for its length and resolution and plans the work as many small, independent
  tasks. With 4 s segments, an 8 minute upload has 8 × 60 ÷ 4 = 120 segments, and five rungs make 600 segment encodes.
  Real pipelines group segments into longer chunks per task, but the shape is the same.
- **Encode in parallel.** Many workers each take a task, read their slice of the source with a ranged read, encode it
  at one rung with a keyframe forced at every segment boundary, and write the segments to object storage.
- **Stitch.** When every task for a rung finishes, a worker writes that rung's playlist. When every rung is done, it
  writes the master manifest and marks the video ready in the metadata database.

Parallelism is the point. One long encode becomes hundreds of short ones, so a long upload finishes in about the time
of its slowest chunk plus the overhead of splitting and stitching.

### Retries without duplicates

Workers crash and tasks time out, so the queue redelivers tasks. A redelivered task must be safe to run twice, and
deterministic keys make it safe: the task for segment 12 at 720p always writes `videos/{videoId}/720p/seg_00012.ts`.
Running it twice writes the same object twice, and the second write replaces the first with identical bytes. The step
that marks the video ready checks the current status first, so a duplicate "all done" message changes nothing. The
[Idempotency](../../reliability/idempotency/) lesson covers this pattern in depth.

When uploads spike, the queue grows and the workers keep their pace. The [Queues and backpressure](../../reliability/queues-and-backpressure/) lesson covers how to
watch queue depth, add workers, and decide what to delay when the queue keeps growing. Here a sensible priority is to
encode the lower rungs before the top ones, so a new video can go live early at modest quality.

### Encode the top rungs lazily

Most uploads get few views. Encoding every upload at 1080p spends compute and storage on video almost nobody watches at
1080p. Some platforms encode the low rungs up front and encode the top rungs only once a video starts drawing views. The
cost is a short window when a fresh hit plays at lower quality.

## Deep dive: paying for egress

Every byte that leaves the cloud toward a viewer is **egress**, billed per gigabyte. In this design playback dwarfs all
other traffic, so the egress bill is the number to watch.

Without a CDN, every segment comes from object storage at the origin, and every byte pays origin egress prices. With a
CDN, viewers fetch segments from edge servers near them. The edge keeps popular segments in its cache. On a miss, it
pulls the segment from the origin once and serves it to everyone nearby who asks next. Video segments suit a CDN
perfectly: they are immutable, identical for every viewer, and requested again and again for popular videos. The [CDNs](../../scaling/cdn/)
lesson covers edge placement and routing, and [Caching](../../scaling/caching/) covers hit ratios and eviction.

### Try it

The calculator uses round illustrative prices: $0.05 per GB from the origin and $0.02 per GB from the CDN. Real prices
vary by provider, region, and volume, and large platforms negotiate their own.

What to look for:

- **The defaults: 10M views × 6 minutes at 720p.** At 2.8 Mbps that delivers 1.26 PB a month. With a 90% hit ratio, the
  origin serves only 126 TB. The bill falls from $63,000 without a CDN to $31,500 with one: 1,260,000 GB × $0.02 =
  $25,200 for CDN delivery, plus 126,000 GB × $0.05 = $6,300 to fill the edge caches.
- **The ladder choice is a bill choice.** Switch the average bitrate to 1080p, and the same views deliver 2.25 PB. The
  bill scales with the average delivered bitrate, so a higher top rung raises it and a more efficient encoder lowers it.
- **The hit ratio decides whether the CDN pays.** Drag it down. At 40% the CDN breaks even. At 20% it costs more than
  serving from the origin alone, because you pay the CDN for every byte and the origin for every miss.
- **At 0%, the origin sends every byte the viewers get,** and the CDN only adds its own charge on top.

### The long tail

Views on a video platform follow a long tail. A small set of popular videos draws most of the views, while millions of
old or niche videos get a few views each. Popular segments stay hot in every edge. A rarely watched video misses at the
edge almost every time, so its bytes come from the origin. The hit ratio is a property of the catalog and the audience,
and platforms push it up in three ways. They keep long cache lifetimes on immutable segments. They pre-warm edges with
a video they expect to spread. And they add a regional cache tier between the edges and the origin, so a miss at many
edges becomes a single origin fetch.

The player controls the other half of the bill. The top rung, the encoder's efficiency (newer codecs reach similar
quality in fewer bits), and the safety margin in the adaptive rule all set the average delivered bitrate.

## Trade-offs

Every choice here trades bytes against something else: storage, compute, quality, or complexity.

<TradeoffTable title="Choices for storing and delivering video">

| Design                                | What it buys                                       | What it costs                                             |
| ------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| One fixed 1080p file                  | One encode, top quality on fast networks           | Stalls whenever bandwidth dips below 5 Mbps               |
| Adaptive bitrate ladder (this lesson) | Few stalls on shaky networks, fast startup         | Five encodes per video, about twice the encoded bytes     |
| Lazy top rungs                        | Less compute and storage for rarely watched videos | New hits play at lower quality until the top rungs finish |
| Origin only                           | No CDN contract to manage                          | Origin prices on every byte; distant viewers wait longer  |
| Origin plus CDN                       | Cheaper bytes near viewers at high hit ratios      | Costs more than origin only when the hit ratio is low     |
| One upload request                    | Simplest client and server                         | Every drop restarts the upload from zero                  |
| Multipart, resumable upload           | Retries only failed parts; parts move in parallel  | Part bookkeeping, more requests, cleanup of stale uploads |

</TradeoffTable>

## Honest simplifications

This lesson's model is a toy. Here is what it leaves out:

- **Scripted bandwidth.** Real networks change in bursts, and throughput also depends on latency, TCP behavior, and
  other apps sharing the phone.
- **Exact segment sizes.** Real encoders vary the bitrate with the content. An action scene takes more bits than a
  static shot, so segments on the same rung differ in size.
- **A basic adaptive rule.** Real players use buffer-based or hybrid algorithms, smooth their estimates, and limit how
  often they switch.
- **A 90 s window.** The duel follows the first 90 s of a longer video, so a player may end the run with segments still
  in its buffer. Near the real end of a video, players stop fetching once the last segment lands.
- **Instant startup once a segment lands.** Real startup also waits on DNS, TLS, the manifest fetch, and the decoder.
- **No codecs, audio, captions, or DRM.** Real platforms encode several codecs, carry separate audio and caption
  tracks, and protect paid content with DRM.
- **Tidy upload drops.** Each drop lands halfway through every transfer in flight. Real drops land anywhere, and real
  clients also handle expired URLs and uploads that never complete.
- **Round prices.** Real egress pricing has tiers, regions, and negotiated rates.
- **Video on demand only.** Live streaming encodes in real time and keeps segments short to cut delay, which changes
  most of the trade-offs above.
