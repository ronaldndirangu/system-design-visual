## The failure: the puppy that arrived late

At 9:02 p.m., a pop star with 30 million followers announces a tour. The fanout queue jumps from a few thousand
pending feed writes to 30 million. One second later, Lena posts a photo of her new puppy for her 200 friends. They do
not see it for minutes: her small fanout job sits in the queue behind the pop star's 30 million writes. Fans who
refresh their feeds see nothing about the tour either, because the fanout fleet has reached only the first few million
of them.

Every part did its job. The post service stored both posts in milliseconds. The fanout fleet ran writes as fast as it
could, oldest job first. The feed cache answered every feed load quickly. The design turned one post into 30 million
writes, and every post behind it waited its turn.

## The idea: split the work by author

This case study has one dominant constraint: **a new post must reach every follower's feed within seconds, and one
celebrity post multiplies that work by millions.**

There are two places to do the work. **Fan out on write** (push) copies each post id into every follower's feed list
when the post goes up, so loading a feed costs one lookup. **Fan out on read** (pull) stores each post once and builds
the feed when someone loads it, by looking up every account they follow. Push makes posts expensive and reads cheap.
Pull does the opposite, and reads outnumber posts. **Hybrid fan-out** pushes posts from ordinary authors and pulls
posts from the few authors above a follower threshold, merging them in at read time.

<Analogy>

Fan out on write is a paper route: a carrier drops a copy on every doorstep each morning, so reading costs you nothing,
but a city of a million doorsteps takes the carrier a long time. Fan out on read is a notice board in the town square:
posting takes one pin, but every reader walks over and scans every board they care about. Hybrid fan-out delivers the
neighborhood newsletter to your door and pins the mayor's announcements in the square, where you glance on your way
past.

</Analogy>

## Walk the design checklist

The seven steps from the [design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/), applied to a news feed.

<Steps>

1. **Requirements.** Users post text and photos, follow and unfollow accounts, and load a home feed of recent posts
   from the accounts they follow, newest first. A new post should appear in followers' feeds within a few seconds
   (the toy model uses 2 s), and a feed load should return in well under a second (the toy model uses 1 s). Feeds may
   lag a little behind the truth, but a stored post must never be lost.

2. **Estimates (illustrative).** Assume 200 million daily users who each load the feed 10 times and post 0.5 times a
   day, follow 300 accounts on average, and peak at 3 times the daily average. That gives about 23,148 feed loads per
   second (69,444 at peak) and about 1,157 posts per second, so reads outnumber posts 20 to 1. Fan out on write turns
   each post into 300 feed writes: about 347,222 writes per second, and about 1,041,667 at peak. Fan out on read turns
   each feed load into 300 lookups: about 20,833,333 lookups per second at peak. One celebrity with 100 million
   followers adds 100 million writes, 50 seconds of work for a fleet that runs 2 million writes per second. Keeping
   the newest 800 entries per user at 16 bytes each (post id and author id) takes about 2.6 TB of feed cache. (See
   [requirements and estimates](../../thinking/requirements-and-estimates/) for the method.)

3. **API.** `POST /posts` with the text and media returns the new post id. `GET /feed?cursor=...&limit=20` returns a
   page of posts, newest first, plus a cursor for the next page. `PUT /follows/{userId}` and
   `DELETE /follows/{userId}` change the follow graph. A cursor keeps paging stable while new posts land at the top.

4. **Data model.** The posts table holds each post once, keyed by a time-sortable post id and
   [sharded](../../scaling/sharding/) by author. The follow graph stores two edge lists per user, followers and
   following, sharded by user. The feed cache holds one capped list per user of (post id, author id) pairs. A feed
   list holds ids only: post bodies come from the posts table, or a post cache in front of it, when a page renders.

5. **High-level design.** A post service stores each post and an outbox row in one transaction, and a relay publishes
   the outbox rows as fanout jobs. A fanout fleet reads followers from
   the follow graph and writes post ids into feed lists. A feed service reads the feed list, merges in celebrity
   posts, and fetches post bodies. The feed lists and celebrity posts sit in an in-memory
   [cache](../../scaling/caching/), and the follow graph serves its heavy read traffic from
   [read replicas](../../scaling/read-replicas/). The [Queues and backpressure](../../reliability/queues-and-backpressure/) lesson covers why the fanout queue
   keeps posting fast when the fleet falls behind.

6. **Deep dive: the fanout queue under a celebrity post.** This is where the design breaks first, and both simulations
   below live here. Push work grows with follower count, so one celebrity post lands millions of writes in the queue
   at once. Pull removes those writes but multiplies the work of every feed load. Hybrid picks per author.

7. **Trade-offs.** Push buys cheap reads with expensive posts. Pull buys cheap posts with expensive reads. Hybrid buys
   both cheap posts and cheap reads with two code paths and a threshold to tune. The table at the end of this page
   lays them out.

</Steps>

## Try it: a celebrity posts

Both lanes below get the same pre-generated workload: 60 ordinary posts over 24 seconds, each from an author with 20
to 80 followers, plus one celebrity post at 3 s, and 40 feed loads per second from 20,000 readers. The left lane fans
every post out on write. The right lane uses hybrid fan-out: posts from authors with more than 1,000 followers skip
the fanout queue and get merged in when a follower loads their feed. Each fanout fleet runs 500 feed writes per second,
and each read tier serves 80 lookups per second, twice what fan out on write needs. A feed load shows the celebrity
post only when the read tier returns it, so the widget also counts loads that showed the post within 1 s.
The toy model counts one lookup for each list a feed load reads (the feed list, or one account's recent posts) and
leaves out fetching post bodies, which every strategy pays equally. All numbers are illustrative, and the scale is
compressed: a real celebrity has millions of followers and a real fleet runs millions of writes per second. The shape
of the failure stays the same.

What to look for:

- **Celebrity posts.** Fan out on write misses the 2 s target for **29 of 60** ordinary posts, and hybrid fan-out
  misses **0**. At 5.9 s, post 8 has waited 2 s in the fanout queue and its 59 followers still cannot see it. At
  13.0 s, the last celebrity follower gets the post, **10 s** after it went up, and **45** feed loads came back without
  it. Hybrid runs **2,974** feed writes instead of **7,974**, and pays for that with **1,520** lookups instead of
  **1,200**. The read tier has room for both, so **292** hybrid feed loads show the celebrity post within 1 s, against
  **247** for fan out on write.
- **Twice the followers.** With 10,000 followers, fan out on write misses **53** posts, the celebrity post takes
  **20 s** to reach everyone, and **193** feed loads miss it. Hybrid still misses **0**. Its lookups rise to **1,814**,
  because more readers follow the celebrity.
- **Double the fleet.** With 1,000 writes per second, fan out on write still misses **11** posts and takes **5 s** to
  reach every celebrity follower. Hybrid misses **0**. A bigger fleet shrinks the delay, and the next celebrity can
  have ten times the followers.
- **Read tier sized for push.** Cut the read tier to 40 lookups per second, exactly what fan out on write needs. Fan
  out on write still serves every load in time. Hybrid fan-out needs 1,520 lookups, so its read queue grows: **996**
  feed loads wait more than 1 s, the average wait reaches **3 s**, and **248** loads are still waiting when the run
  ends. Only **17** hybrid loads show the celebrity post within 1 s, against **247** for fan out on write. Hybrid moves
  the celebrity's work from the fanout fleet to the read tier, so the read tier needs the headroom.
- **No celebrity.** Both lanes do the same work and meet every target. Hybrid fan-out changes nothing for ordinary
  authors.

## Why a fairer queue does not save fan out on write

A smarter fleet could interleave celebrity batches with ordinary jobs, so post 8 never waits behind all 10 batches.
Ordinary posts would then meet their target. The fleet still runs the same 500 writes per second, though, so the
celebrity's last followers would wait even longer, and every one of them who loads a feed in the meantime sees a feed
without the post everyone is talking about. Fair scheduling changes who waits. Only removing the work removes the wait,
and hybrid fan-out removes it.

Real feeds also skip fanout for users who have not opened the app in weeks. Their feed lists would expire before they
read them, so the feed service rebuilds those feeds on read when the user returns.

What to look for:

- **Evening peak.** Fan out on read runs **92,630** lookups against **1,840** for fan out on write. **959** of the
  **1,840** feed loads wait more than 1 s for the read tier, the average wait reaches **1.4 s**, and the backlog peaks

- **Double the read tier.** With 8,000 lookups per second, **0** loads wait more than 1 s. That works until the next
  peak, and every feed load still costs 30 to 70 lookups.
- **No peak.** Without the peak, fan out on read keeps up and no load waits more than 1 s. Its cost hides until the
  busiest hour of the day.

## Pick the threshold

Hybrid fan-out needs one number: the follower count above which an author's posts get pulled. A lower threshold
removes more writes and adds more read work, because every reader pays one extra lookup per followed account above
it. A reader who follows 200 celebrities would pay 201 lookups per feed load, so some designs also cap how many pulled
accounts one load merges, or push to the most active followers of a celebrity and pull for the rest.

The threshold trades between two budgets, so size both. The fanout fleet needs writes per second for every pushed
post, and the read tier needs lookups per second for every pulled author on every feed load. The "Read tier sized for
push" run shows the failure when only the first budget gets planned: hybrid fan-out clears the fanout queue and
swamps the read tier instead.

Pulled posts are cheap to serve because millions of readers ask for the same few lists. A celebrity's recent posts
make an ideal cache entry: tiny, hot, and rarely changed. The [Sharding](../../scaling/sharding/) lesson explains why
one hot key still lands on one shard, and why a cache in front absorbs the reads.

## Edges the simulation leaves out

- **Replies that beat their post.** A friend's reply can reach you, through a notification or a reply thread, before
  the original post reaches your feed. Clients fetch any referenced post by id from the posts table, the source of
  truth, so the reply never points at nothing.
- **The cap on the feed list.** Each feed list keeps the newest 800 entries. Few users scroll that far, and the feed
  service builds older pages by fan out on read when someone does.
- **Ranking.** Most real feeds rank posts instead of sorting them by time. Ranking reorders the candidates in the feed
  list and the pulled celebrity posts. It does not change who does the fanout work.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: deletes and unfollows</summary>

A pop star with 40 million followers deletes a post minutes after sharing it. Separately, a reader unfollows an
ordinary account they followed for years. With hybrid fan-out, filter at read time and clean up in the background.

- **The celebrity delete is cheap.** The post never reached any feed list. Mark it deleted in the posts table and
  evict it from the cache of the pop star's recent posts. The next feed load merges without it.
- **An ordinary delete touches many lists.** The post id sits in every follower's feed list. When the feed service
  fetches post bodies, it skips posts marked deleted, so the post vanishes on the next load. A background job removes
  the stale ids later, or the ids age out of the 800 entry cap.
- **The unfollow updates the follow graph first.** New fanout jobs then skip the reader. The feed service drops posts
  from authors the reader no longer follows, and a background job strips that author's ids from the reader's list.
- **What can still show.** A screen that already rendered the post keeps it until the reader refreshes, and any
  cached post bodies need a short expiry or an explicit eviction. Fan out on write makes deletes the expensive
  direction, and read-time filtering keeps them cheap.

</details>

## Failure walkthrough: the post service crashes before the fanout job

Lena's post commits at 9:02:00. The post service crashes at 9:02:00.1, before anything reaches the fanout queue.
Without an outbox, the post sits in the posts table and no job exists to put it in her friends' feeds. They never see
it, and nothing reports an error.

With the outbox, the same transaction that stored the post also stored a row that says "fan out post 812 to Lena's
followers". The two rows commit together or not at all, so a stored post always has its owed fanout recorded.

1. **The relay restarts.** It reads every outbox row not yet marked sent and finds post 812.
2. **It publishes again.** The relay may have published the job just before the crash, so the queue can now hold two
   copies. The relay marks the row sent only after the queue confirms, which makes delivery
   [at least once](../../async/delivery-semantics/).
3. **The fleet runs both copies.** Workers add post 812 to each follower's feed list. A feed list stores each post id
   once, so the second copy changes nothing. A worker that crashes mid-job also replays safely, for the same reason.
4. **Followers see the post late.** The delay equals the relay's downtime plus the queue wait. Nobody misses it.

Change data capture (CDC) is the common alternative: a connector tails the database's commit log and publishes each
new post as a fanout job, with the same at-least-once guarantee and no outbox table to clean up. Either way, the feed
lists must tolerate duplicates.

## News feed versus notifications

The [Notifications](../notifications/) case study also takes one event and delivers it to many people, but its
constraint sits elsewhere.

| Question | News feed | Notifications |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who owns delivery? | Your own feed cache, read when the user opens the app | External providers (push services, SMS, email) |
| What grows the work? | Follower count: one post becomes a write per follower | Send volume, capped by provider rate limits |
| What goes wrong? | One celebrity post buries every later post in the fanout queue | A provider throttles or fails, and the message expires |
| The guard | Hybrid fan-out: pull from big authors at read time | Priority lanes, deadlines, and failover between providers |
| Late is… | Fine: the post still shows up, a little stale | Often worthless: a login code that arrives late is junk |

| Choice | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fan out on write (push) | One lookup per feed load, even at peak | Writes grow with followers, and one celebrity post delays every later post |
| Fan out on read (pull) | No feed writes, and new posts appear at once | Every load costs one lookup per followed account, and the peak swamps the read tier |
| Hybrid fan-out | Cheap posts and cheap reads, with celebrity posts visible at once | Two code paths, a threshold to tune, and extra lookups for celebrity followers |
| A bigger fanout fleet | Every fanout job finishes sooner | Money, and a big enough celebrity still buries the queue |
| Cap feed lists at 800 entries | Bounded memory for every user | Deep scrolling falls back to fan out on read |
| Skip fanout for inactive users | No writes for feeds nobody reads | The first load after a long absence rebuilds the feed on read |

</TradeoffTable>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Fan out on write pays per follower when a post goes up. Fan out on read pays per followed account on every feed
  load. Reads outnumber posts, so push wins for most authors.
- One celebrity post turns into millions of queued writes and delays every post behind it. A bigger fleet only
  shrinks the delay until the next, bigger celebrity.
- Hybrid fan-out pulls posts from authors above a follower threshold. It moves their work to the read tier, so size
  both the fanout fleet and the read tier.
- Commit the post and its owed fanout job together through an outbox or CDC. Delivery is at least once, so feed lists
  must ignore duplicate post ids.

</KeyTakeaways>
