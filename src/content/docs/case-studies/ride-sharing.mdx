## The failure: measure the distance to every driver

The naive design keeps one row per driver: driver id, latitude, longitude, and status. Every 4 s each driver app
overwrites its row. When a rider asks for a ride, the server reads every free driver, computes the straight-line distance
to each one, and picks the smallest. In a small town with 20 drivers, that is instant. A city at rush hour has thousands
of drivers and a new ride request every few milliseconds, and every request pays for every driver.

The widget below runs a toy downtown: 600 drivers on a grid of 10 × 10 geohash cells, about 6 per cell. One lane scans
every free driver. The other looks only in the rider's cell and the 8 cells around it.

## The constraint: drivers never stop moving

Most systems in this course read far more than they write. Ride sharing turns that around. Every online driver reports a
position every few seconds, whether or not anyone wants a ride. With this lesson's illustrative numbers, 200,000 online
drivers send 50,000 location writes per second, against about 347 ride requests per second at peak: about 144 writes for
every search. Any index on driver location has to absorb that write stream cheaply, or it costs more to maintain than it
saves.

The way out is that most pings change almost nothing. A driver at 36 km/h moves 40 m in 4 s, and a precision 6 geohash
cell is about 610 m tall. So most pings land in the same cell as the last one: the index overwrites the stored position
and leaves its cell sets alone. Only about 1 ping in 12 crosses into a new cell and moves the driver between sets.

The rest of this lesson walks the [design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/).

## Requirements

**Functional**

- Online drivers report their position every few seconds.
- A rider requests a ride from a pickup point and gets one nearby driver of the right vehicle type.
- No driver ever holds two rides at once.
- A driver can decline an offer, and the rider still gets a ride.
- Each trip keeps its route for the fare and the receipt.

**Non-functional** (illustrative targets)

- A rider sees a matched driver within a few seconds of tapping Request.
- The index reflects a ping within a few seconds.
- A lost ping costs nothing, because the next one replaces it 4 s later.
- 50,000 location writes per second never slow down ride matching.

## Estimates

These numbers are illustrative. Assume 200,000 drivers online at once, each sending a ping every 4 s, and 10 million
rides a day, with a peak 3 times the average.

| Quantity                        | Illustrative value                                                     |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Location writes                 | 200,000 ÷ 4 s = 50,000 per second                                      |
| Geo index memory                | 100 bytes per driver × 200,000 = 20 MB                                 |
| Ride requests, average          | 10M ÷ 86,400 s, about 116 per second                                   |
| Ride requests, peak (3×)        | about 347 per second                                                   |
| Location writes per ride search | 50,000 ÷ 347, about 144                                                |
| Naive scan at peak              | 200,000 drivers × 347 requests, about 69.4M distance checks per second |
| Distance per ping               | 36 km/h for 4 s = 40 m                                                 |
| Pings that change cell          | about 1 in 12 (8.35%), about 4,175 per second                          |
| Location history                | 50,000 × 40 bytes × 86,400 s, about 173 GB per day                     |

The 1 in 12 treats a precision 6 cell as a square 0.61 km on a side. A 40 m hop in a random direction crosses an edge
with probability about (4 ÷ π) × (0.04 ÷ 0.61), which is 8.35%.

Two things stand out. First, the naive scan grows with drivers times requests: when a city doubles both, the work grows
4×. Second, the whole live index fits in 20 MB, small enough to hold in memory on one machine. The hard part is the
write rate, and only the 4,175 cell changes per second touch the cell sets.

## API

```text
POST /drivers/{driverId}/location
     body { lat, lng, heading, session, seq }
     returns 204 at once, and the app never retries a lost ping

POST /rides
     body { pickup: { lat, lng }, vehicleType }
     returns { rideId, status: "matching" }

GET  /rides/{rideId}
     returns { status, driver, etaSeconds }

POST /rides/{rideId}/offer/accept     from the driver app
     body { version }
POST /rides/{rideId}/offer/decline    from the driver app
     body { version }
```

The location call is fire and forget. A lost ping needs no retry, because the next one arrives 4 s later with fresher
data. The server issues `session` at each sign in, and the app raises `seq` by one per ping, so the index can drop a ping
that shows up after a newer one. The offer calls name the reservation `version` from the offer, so a reply to an old
offer changes nothing. `POST /rides` returns before any driver is found. The rider app learns the outcome by polling
`GET /rides/{rideId}` or over a push channel, which the [Real-time transport](../../foundations/real-time/) lesson
compares.

## Data model

| Table                        | Key                         | Holds                                                                                                         |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| drivers                      | `driver_id`                 | Vehicle type, rating, status (free, reserved, or on trip), `ride_id`, `reservation_version`, and `expires_at` |
| geo cells (in memory)        | geohash cell                | The set of driver ids whose last ping fell in this cell                                                       |
| driver positions (in memory) | `driver_id`                 | Last latitude, longitude, cell, and the `(session, seq)` of the last ping applied                             |
| rides                        | `ride_id`                   | Rider, pickup point, vehicle type, status, matched driver, timestamps                                         |
| location_history             | `(driver_id, session, seq)` | Every ping and the time the server received it, append only, on cheap bulk storage                            |

The two in-memory maps work as a pair. A ping looks up the driver's last cell in driver positions. If the new cell is the
same, the index overwrites the position and stops. If the cell changed, it removes the driver id from the old cell's set
and adds it to the new one. The index needs no disk at all: if it crashes, the next 4 s of pings rebuild it from
scratch. Location history lives elsewhere, and nothing reads it while matching.

## High-level design

Two paths share the geo index. The write path carries pings: the gateway accepts them, a log buffers them, and two
consumers feed the in-memory index and the history store. The read path carries ride requests: the matcher reads a few
cells, ranks the candidates by road ETA, and reserves one driver. The log between the gateway and the index lets a burst
of pings wait its turn instead of overwhelming the index, which is the Queues and backpressure idea at work.

## Deep dive: geohash cells and the 8 neighbors

### Geohash: one string per cell

A **geohash** turns a latitude and longitude into a short string, where each extra character names a smaller box. It
works by halving. Start with the whole world, from 180° west to 180° east. If the point lies in the east half, write a 1
and keep that half; otherwise write a 0 and keep the west half. Then do the same for latitude, from 90° south to 90°
north: 1 for the north half, 0 for the south. Keep alternating, longitude first, and every 5 bits become one character
from the base32 alphabet `0123456789bcdefghjkmnpqrstuvwxyz`, which skips a, i, l, and o so nobody misreads them.

The classic test point, latitude 57.64911 and longitude 10.40744 in Denmark, encodes to `u4pruydqqvj` at 11 characters.
Its first 5 bits are 1 1 0 1 0: east, north, west half of the east, north half of the north, west again. That is 26 in
binary, and character 26 of the alphabet is `u`.

Each character adds 5 bits, so it cuts a cell into 32 smaller ones. A cell spans a fixed number of degrees, and a degree
of longitude shrinks toward the poles, so cells grow narrower away from the equator. All sizes below are approximate.

| Characters | Approximate cell at the equator |
| ---------- | ------------------------------- |
| 1          | 5,009 × 5,009 km                |
| 4          | 39.1 × 19.6 km                  |
| 5          | 4.89 × 4.89 km                  |
| 6          | 1.22 × 0.61 km                  |
| 7          | 153 × 153 m                     |

At 57.6° N, near the test point, a precision 6 cell shrinks to about 0.66 × 0.61 km. This lesson indexes drivers at
precision 6: a cell holds a few city blocks.

Because every character refines the one before, **a shared prefix means nearby**: two drivers whose hashes both start
with `u4pruy` stand in the same precision 6 cell. The reverse fails. Two points a few meters apart on either side of a
cell boundary can share a short prefix, or none at all. A point just north east of 0°, 0° encodes to `s0000`, and a
point just south west of it encodes to `7zzzz`: about 31 m apart, with no character in common, because the very first
bit splits the world at the prime meridian. That caveat drives the whole search design below.

Step through the bits one at a time. Each bit halves the box, alternating between longitude and latitude, and every
fifth bit completes a character. Then switch to the points beside 0°, 0° and watch them split on bit 1.

### Search the cell and its 8 neighbors

The index maps each precision 6 cell to the set of drivers inside it. For a ride request, the matcher computes the
geohash of the pickup point, then reads that cell and the 8 cells that touch it along an edge or at a corner. Finding a
neighbor's hash needs no lookup table: decode the cell to its box, step one cell width or height from its center, and
encode that point at the same precision. Searching the rider's own cell alone would be cheaper, and it gives the wrong
answer whenever the closest driver stands just across an edge.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: real matchers, sparse suburbs, and a stadium</summary>

**Matching takes more than the nearest dot.** Nearest by straight line is a toy. A driver 300 m away on the far side of
a river or a one-way street can need ten minutes to arrive. Real matchers take the candidates from the cells, filter
them by vehicle type, rating, and status, ask a **routing service** for the road ETA from each one to the pickup point,
and rank by ETA. Some also collect the requests that arrive within a second or two and assign them together, which can
cut the total pickup time when riders cluster.

**Sparse suburbs.** Real systems handle sparse areas in one of two ways. They widen ring by ring, as the toy does, or
they drop one character of precision and search 9 bigger cells, each 32 times the area. Dropping a character is
simpler, and in a dense area it can pull in thousands of drivers, so systems pick the precision per area or stop
widening as soon as they hold enough candidates.

**A stadium lets out.** A concert ends, and 2,000 drivers converge on the 4 precision 6 cells around the stadium, a
2 × 2 block with about 500 drivers per cell. A rider in one of those cells requests a ride. Assume drivers spread evenly
within each cell.

- **The 9-cell search checks about 2,000 drivers.** Every one of the 4 hot cells touches the rider's cell, so the search
  reads all of them, as many checks as a scan of a small city.
- **Go one character finer in hot areas.** Each precision 6 cell splits into **32** precision 7 cells (8 columns by 4
  rows), about **153 × 153 m** at the equator. 500 ÷ 32 is about **16 drivers per cell**, so a 9-cell search checks
  about **140**.
- **Cap the candidates.** Stop at the first ring that yields, say, **20** free drivers of the right type, and send only
  those to the routing service for an ETA.
- **Expect a burst of requests too.** Everyone leaves at once, so put a queue in front of the matcher and assign nearby
  requests in batches.

</details>

### Reserve one driver, then offer the ride

Two riders a block apart can tap Request at the same moment, and both matchers can pick the same driver. Reading the
status and then writing reserved leaves a gap where both reads see free. The fix is one atomic step, a conditional
update (compare and set) that takes a **lease**, a reservation that expires on its own, and stamps it with a new
version:

```sql
UPDATE drivers
SET status = 'reserved', ride_id = :ride,
    reservation_version = reservation_version + 1,
    expires_at = now() + interval '15 seconds'
WHERE driver_id = :driver
  AND (status = 'free' OR (status = 'reserved' AND expires_at <= now()))
RETURNING reservation_version;
```

Exactly one update finds the driver available and changes a row. The other changes zero rows and moves to its next
candidate. The [Transactions and constraints](../../foundations/transactions/) lesson covers this pattern. A lock also
works, but every other matcher waits while it is held. The lease frees the driver even if the matcher that
took it crashes, and the version names this one reservation among all of the driver's past ones.

Next, the matcher offers the ride to the driver app with that version and starts a 15 s timer,
the length of the lease. The [Timeouts and cancellation](../../reliability/timeouts/) lesson covers why every wait needs
a deadline.

The trap is a stale message. Networks delay replies, and processes pause for garbage collection or a slow disk. The
driver's accept can arrive after the lease expired and another ride reserved the same driver, and a paused matcher can
wake up and fire its old timer. So every message names the ride and the version it answers, and the update matches
both. The version works as a fencing token, which the [Time, leases, and
fencing](../../distributed-data/time-leases-fencing/) lesson covers. The driver's accept also needs the lease to still
be running:

```sql
UPDATE drivers
SET status = 'on_trip'
WHERE driver_id = :driver AND ride_id = :ride
  AND reservation_version = :version
  AND status = 'reserved' AND expires_at > now();
```

A decline and the matcher's timer both release the driver with the same match, without the expiry check:

```sql
UPDATE drivers
SET status = 'free', ride_id = NULL
WHERE driver_id = :driver AND ride_id = :ride
  AND reservation_version = :version AND status = 'reserved';
```

Zero rows changed means the message was stale, and the sender drops it: the driver app shows the offer as expired, and
the matcher moves to its next candidate. A matcher that needs more time renews with the accept's conditions and pushes
`expires_at` forward. The database checks every expiry against its own clock, `now()`, so the clocks on phones and
matchers never decide who holds a driver.

This timeline comes from the lesson's reservation model. One driver, d7, meets two rides:

| Time | Message                                          | Names     | d7's version | Result                      |
| ---- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------- | ------------ | --------------------------- |
| 0 s  | Ride A reserves d7                               |           | 1            | Reserved until 15 s         |
| 15 s | Ride A's lease expires                           |           | 1            | d7 counts as free           |
| 16 s | Ride B reserves d7                               |           | 2            | Reserved until 31 s         |
| 17 s | Matcher A wakes from a pause and fires its timer | version 1 | 2            | Rejected, zero rows changed |
| 18 s | The driver's accept for ride A arrives late      | version 1 | 2            | Rejected, zero rows changed |
| 20 s | The driver accepts ride B                        | version 2 | 2            | On trip with ride B         |

A check on status alone would let the old timer free d7 in the middle of ride B's offer, and a third ride could then
claim the driver. The ride id catches a message about another ride. The version catches one from an earlier reservation
by the same ride, such as a matcher that retried after its own lease expired.

### Feeding the index

The location stream runs through a log, the structure the [Logs and pub/sub](../../async/logs-and-pubsub/) lesson builds, and the index consumer is a
small stream processor. Partition the log by driver id, so one consumer reads every ping from a driver in order and
holds that driver's last cell. Shard the geo index itself by cell prefix, for example the first 4 characters, so one
index node holds every cell in an area and answers most 9-cell searches by itself. This is
[sharding](../../scaling/sharding/) with a geographic key, and it brings three problems. Downtown at rush hour becomes a
**hot shard**, so busy areas split further on a longer prefix. A search near a shard boundary reads cells from two
nodes. A driver who crosses a boundary moves from one node to another, so the consumer sends a removal to the old node
and an addition to the new one. Keying the log by cell would break this step: the new area's partition never learns
which node still lists the driver.

Pings also arrive late or twice: mobile networks deliver them out of order, and a consumer that restarts reads some
again, the at-least-once delivery that Delivery semantics and the outbox describes. Each ping carries the
`(session, seq)` pair from the API, and so does every addition and removal it causes. Each index node remembers the
newest pair it has applied per driver, removals included, and drops anything older or equal, so a stale ping never drags
a driver back to where they were. A timestamp from the phone cannot do this job: phone clocks jump when the network or
the user corrects them, and one ping stamped an hour ahead would block every real ping for that hour. The sequence only
has to rise within one session, and the server issues a higher session number at each sign in, so the first ping of a
new session outranks the old one. As a backstop, a cell entry expires about 30 s after its last ping (illustrative), so
a driver whose removal got lost, or whose app went offline, leaves the search on its own. When consumers fall behind
during a burst, they skip to the newest ping for each driver, since an old position has no value for matching.

A second consumer writes every ping to the location history, about 173 GB a day in this estimate, on cheap bulk
storage. Fares, receipts, and disputes read a trip's route from it later.

## Failure walkthrough: the matcher crashes after reserving a driver

Ride R reserves driver d7 at 0 s and gets version 4. The matcher crashes at 0.1 s, before it sends the offer. The driver
app never rings, and the rider's screen still says matching.

1. **The lease holds d7 for 15 s.** Other matchers try d7, change zero rows, and move to their next candidate. One
   driver sits idle for one lease, and nobody can book d7 twice.
2. **The database frees d7 on its own clock.** At 15 s, `expires_at <= now()`, so the next reserve that picks d7
   succeeds and sets version 5. No process has to remember to release it.
3. **Another matcher takes over ride R.** Matchers claim rides with the same kind of lease, stored in the rides row.
   When the crashed matcher's claim expires, a live matcher claims R, searches the cells again, and reserves a driver.
   The rider waits about 15 s longer and still gets exactly one driver.
4. **The old matcher wakes up harmless.** Suppose it restarts from a checkpoint and fires its timer for version 4. If
   another reservation now holds d7, even a new one for ride R, the version differs and the release changes zero rows.
   If nobody took d7, the release frees a driver whose lease had already lapsed.

## Trade-offs

Geohash is one of several ways to bucket a map. Its strength is simplicity: a cell is a string, any key-value store can
index it, and a shared prefix names a whole area, which makes partitioning easy. Its cells are rectangles that change
shape with latitude, and finding neighbors takes a little arithmetic. **H3**, the hexagonal grid Uber published, tiles
the globe with mostly hexagonal cells, so most cells have 6 neighbors at fairly even distances and rings come out
smoother than a rectangle's 8 neighbors. The grid is not perfectly uniform: every resolution has 12 pentagons with only
5 neighbors, and cell size and shape distort a little across the globe because H3 projects an icosahedron onto the
sphere. Code should walk cells with the library's traversal calls, such as `gridDisk`, and never assume 6 neighbors at
one fixed distance. A **quadtree**
splits only crowded areas, so cells adapt to density, at the cost of a tree to rebalance as drivers move. A database
spatial index, covered in the [Indexes](../../foundations/indexes/) lesson, answers radius queries directly and pays for it on every write.

<TradeoffTable title="Ways to find nearby drivers">

| Design                                   | What it buys                                                                     | What it costs                                                                          |
| ---------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scan every driver                        | No index to maintain and trivial code                                            | Work per request grows with every online driver in the city                            |
| Database spatial index                   | Radius queries in SQL, next to the driver rows                                   | Every location write updates a disk index, 50,000 times a second                       |
| Geohash cell + 8 neighbors (this lesson) | Cells are plain strings, prefixes give partitions, most pings skip the cell sets | Rectangles that vary with latitude, and one cell size for dense and sparse areas alike |
| Hexagonal grid (H3)                      | Fairly even neighbor distances and smooth rings in most cells                    | A library to walk cells, 12 pentagons per resolution, and children only roughly fill   |
| Quadtree                                 | Small cells where drivers crowd, large cells where they are sparse               | A tree to split and merge as drivers move, and harder to partition                     |

</TradeoffTable>

## Honest simplifications

This lesson's model is a toy. Here is what it leaves out:

- **Straight-line distance.** The toy matches the nearest driver as the crow flies. Real systems rank candidates by road
  ETA from a routing service.
- **A flat toy city.** 100 cells of about 0.66 × 0.61 km, with drivers moving in straight lines at 36 km/h and no roads.
- **Bouncing drivers.** Drivers bounce off the city limits instead of driving out, so slightly fewer pings cross a cell
  edge: 7% in the dense run, against the 8.35% estimate.
- **Short runs.** 15 requests over 30 s, with trips that last 12 s so drivers come back free within the run.
- **No declines, surge pricing, or batching.** Every driver accepts at once, prices never change, and requests match one
  at a time.
- **Offers in a separate model.** The duel matches instantly and never sends an offer. The late accept timeline above
  runs on a small reservation model with one driver and two rides.
- **One index node.** The whole city fits in one in-memory index. The toy has no log, no partitions, and no lost or late
  pings. The `(session, seq)` ordering rule lives in the same small model as the offers.
- **Rectangles.** At the equator a precision 6 cell is twice as wide as it is tall. The toy city sits at 57.6° N, where
  cells come out close to square.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Search the rider's geohash cell and its 8 neighbors, and widen one ring at a time when they come up short, so the work
  tracks nearby drivers instead of the whole city.
- Location writes outnumber searches about 144 to 1. Most pings stay in their cell and only overwrite a position, so
  only about 1 ping in 12 touches the cell sets.
- Reserve a driver with one conditional update that takes a 15 s lease and bumps a version. Every accept, decline, and
  timer names the ride and the version, so a stale message changes zero rows.
- Partition the ping log by driver id to keep each driver's pings in order, and shard the index by cell prefix so one
  node answers most searches. Split hot prefixes further, and let lagging consumers skip to the newest ping.

</KeyTakeaways>
