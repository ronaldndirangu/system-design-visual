## The failure: a storm the index has never heard of

The first design builds one index each night. A batch job reads every document, attaches its ranking signals, including
the day's clicks, and writes a fresh index that goes live by morning. Searches for anything older than the build get
excellent results. Then a storm makes landfall at 00:30. Thousands of readers search for it within minutes, and the
index has never seen a single word about it.

The widget below runs one toy news day, and every number in it is illustrative. It starts with 1,800 older documents
across 30 topics. Three stories break at 00:30, 10:30, and 18:30, and 191 new documents arrive over the day, most of
them about those stories. Every 15 simulated minutes, 48 queries arrive, and once a story has broken, 70% of them chase
one. Both lanes run the same full build, which starts at 02:00 and takes 3 h. The second lane also writes each new
document into a small live segment that is searchable after a refresh, assumed to take 1 s. Recall@10 compares each
lane's top 10 with an ideal ranking that knows every document.

## The constraint: freshness runs on four clocks

Good ranking fixes that with signals from outside the document: how often readers click it, how trusted its source is,
whether a spam model flags it. New documents arrive every second, and a reader searching for breaking news wants them
ranked well now. "Fresh" means four different things here, and each runs on its own clock:

- **Index refresh.** How soon a new document becomes searchable. Lucene-style engines open a new small segment about
  once a second.
- **Signal updates.** How soon a stored signal changes. A click count is a running sum, so a stream job can add each
  click within seconds. A source's trust score and a trained spam model can score a story as it arrives.
- **Signal freshness.** How much evidence a signal holds. A brand-new story has no clicks yet, however fast the counter
  updates, so ranking leans on text match and source trust until readers find it.
- **Model training.** How often the ranking model that weighs the signals learns new weights. It trains offline on days
  of click logs and ships daily or weekly.

The nightly design ties the first two clocks to one build. It bakes the signals into the index files, so a document
becomes searchable, and its popularity changes, only when a build rewrites its shard. One option keeps the nightly
build and adds a small real-time tier for new documents: **batch plus delta**, two indexes side by side. Another keeps
signals in a separate store that a stream job updates, and search servers read the latest values at query time. Real
systems often run both. This lesson builds batch plus delta and bakes every signal in at build time to keep the duel
simple.

The rest of this lesson walks the [design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/).

## Requirements

**Functional**

- A reader searches news with a few words and gets the 10 best documents in ranked order.
- A newly published story is searchable within seconds.
- Editors can correct or take down a story, and search reflects it within seconds.
- As the reader types, the search box suggests completions (the extension at the end).

**Non-functional** (illustrative targets)

- A search answers within a few hundred milliseconds at peak.
- 20,000 searches per second at peak, with room to grow.
- Older documents keep the full-signal ranking at all times.
- Typeahead answers in under 100 ms end to end.

## Estimates

These numbers are illustrative. Assume 540M documents of about 1 KB of index each, 60 new documents per second, and 576M
searches a day with a peak 3 times the average. A build machine pool indexes 50,000 documents per second, one shard
holds 30 GB of index, and one replica serves 2,000 searches per second.

| Quantity                           | Illustrative value                                              |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Index size                         | 540M × 1 KB = 540 GB                                            |
| Shards                             | 540 GB ÷ 30 GB = 18                                             |
| New documents                      | 60 per second, 5,184,000 per day (0.96% of the corpus)          |
| Searches, average                  | 576M ÷ 86,400 s, about 6,667 per second                         |
| Searches, peak (3×)                | 20,000 per second                                               |
| Full rebuild                       | 540M ÷ 50,000 per second = 10,800 s = 3 h                       |
| New documents during one rebuild   | 60 × 10,800 s = 648,000                                         |
| Full builds per day, back to back  | at most 8                                                       |
| Rebuild every minute               | 540M ÷ 3,600 new documents = 150,000 rewrites per new document  |
| Replicas per shard                 | 20,000 ÷ 2,000 = 10, so 18 × 10 = 180 search servers            |
| Typeahead, peak (6 keystrokes)     | 20,000 × 6 = 120,000 requests per second                        |

Two things stand out. First, a full rebuild every few seconds is impossible: even one per minute would rewrite 150,000
documents for each new one, and a build takes 3 h anyway. A fresh build is already 648,000 documents behind when it goes
live. Second, new documents are small next to the corpus, under 1% a day, so a separate small index for them is cheap to
search and cheap to rewrite.

## API

```text
GET    /search?q=storm+coast&page=1
       returns { results: [{ docId, title, snippet, publishedAt }], nextPage }

POST   /documents
       body { docId, title, body, publishedAt }
       returns 202: searchable within seconds, fully ranked after the next build

DELETE /documents/{docId}
       returns 202: hidden from every result, cached or not, within about 1 s; removed from disk at the next merge

GET    /suggest?prefix=sto
       returns { completions: ["storm", "stock market today", "storm tracker"] }
```

`POST /documents` returns 202 because indexing runs after the call returns. The API promises two stages: the document
becomes searchable within seconds with text-only ranking, and it gains full ranking signals after the next full build.
`DELETE` writes a tombstone so new queries skip the document, and it pushes the id to every query server so cached pages
skip it too (see the freshness budget below). The disk space comes back at the next merge. `GET /suggest` serves
typeahead and never touches the search index.

## Data model

| Table           | Key         | Holds                                                                                  |
| --------------- | ----------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Posting lists   | `term`      | Sorted document ids with term frequency, one list per term in each segment             |
| Document store  | `docId`     | Title, body, and publish time, for snippets and for the next build                     |
| Ranking signals | `docId`     | Click popularity updated as clicks stream in, source quality, and spam score           |
| Live segments   | `segmentId` | Small inverted indexes for documents since the last build, plus a set of tombstones    |
| Typeahead trie  | `prefix`    | The top completions under that prefix, counted from the query log                      |

The document store is the source of truth. Every index here derives from it and can be rebuilt from it, which makes a
nightly rebuild possible. The posting lists answer "which documents contain this word?", and the ranking signals answer
"which of those should come first?" The [Data modeling and storage choice](../../foundations/data-modeling/) lesson
covers the stores themselves.

## High-level design

Two paths serve every query. The main index shards hold every document up to the last full build, ranked with every
signal. The live segments hold everything newer, ranked on text match only. A query searches both and merges the
results, and the next full build folds the live documents into the main index.

## Deep dive: indexing, ranking, and freshness

### The inverted index

A book's index maps a word to the pages that use it. An inverted index does the same for documents: for each term, it
keeps a **posting list** of the documents that contain it, sorted by document id, often with the term's count in each
document. Indexing splits each document into terms, lowercases them, and drops stopwords such as "the" and "on". A query
reads only its own terms' lists. To find documents with every term, it walks the sorted lists side by side and keeps the
ids that appear in all of them, so the work grows with the length of those lists and never touches the rest of the
corpus.

What to look for:

- **Two lists out of 16.** The query storm coast reads 2 of the 16 posting lists. The storm list holds d1, d2, d3
  (3 times), and d6. Only d1, d2, and d3 also appear in the coast list.
- **Keyword stuffing wins.** On text alone, d3, "Storm storm storm coast deals", scores 1.14 and beats the real story,
  d1, at 0.88.
- **A signal from outside the text fixes it.** Turn on the popularity signal and d1 leads with 1.78, then d2 at 1.48 and
  d3 at 1.19.

### Ranking: text match, then signals from outside the text

The widget scores text with a simplified BM25, a standard relevance formula. Each query term adds to the score. A rare
term adds more than a common one, because it says more about the document. A term that repeats adds more, with
diminishing returns: the third "storm" in d3 counts for less than the first. Real engines also adjust for document
length, which the toy skips.

Text relevance cannot tell a trusted story from a page that repeats the right words. So the score adds signals: click
popularity, source quality, spam scores, often combined by a learned ranking model. A stream job keeps click popularity
current by adding each click to a running count. Source quality and a trained spam model score a document the moment it
arrives, so real systems apply them at ingest. Only the ranking model's weights come from a slow offline job. The duel
skips all of that and computes every signal at build time, so a live document gets a neutral quality score until the
next build measures the real one. A brand-new story has no clicks yet anyway.

### Why one new document is expensive: immutable segments

Search engines write each index file once and never edit it. Posting lists are sorted and compressed, so inserting one
id would shift everything after it. Files that never change also let thousands of queries read them without locks and
let the operating system cache them freely. So the engine handles change around the files:

- **An add** goes into a new small segment.
- **A delete** becomes a tombstone, an entry in a deleted set that every query filters out.
- **An update** is a delete plus an add.

Every query searches every segment and merges the results, so small segments cannot pile up forever. A **merge policy**
rewrites them: in the duel, whenever 4 segments share a level, the indexer merges them into one segment a level up and
drops tombstoned documents on the way. Each document gets rewritten once per level, which keeps both the segment count
and the rewrite work small. The 19:45 merge in the breaking run, 4 level 2 segments into one level 3 segment holding 128
documents, is one of those rewrites. Log-structured storage engines use the same write-once-then-compact pattern.

### Sharding the index

At 540 GB, the index needs 18 shards. [Sharding and partitioning](../../scaling/sharding/) gives two ways to split
it:

- **By document.** Each shard holds a complete inverted index for its slice of the documents. A query goes to all 18
  shards, each returns its own top 10, and the query service merges them. Indexing one document touches one shard. The
  catch: every query waits for the slowest shard.
- **By term.** Each shard holds the full posting lists for some terms. A two-term query touches at most two shards, but
  a hot term makes a hot shard, intersecting lists means shipping them between machines, and indexing one document
  touches a shard for every distinct term in it.

Most engines shard by document. Since every query visits every shard, each shard sees the full peak of 20,000 searches
per second. At 2,000 per replica, that means 10 [read replicas](../../scaling/read-replicas/) per shard and 180 search
servers, with a [load balancer](../../scaling/load-balancing/) choosing a replica for each shard. The live segments
shard the same way, so the real-time tier adds a little fan-out inside each shard. Each shard also scores text with its
own term counts, a small skew the next block explains.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: text scores across shards</summary>

BM25 weighs a term by how rare it is, and each shard counts that over its own documents only, so the query service
merges scores computed on 18 slightly different scales. Hashing documents by docId gives every shard a similar random
sample, so the local counts land close to the global ones and the error stays small. The real-time tier breaks that
sample when it scores with statistics from its own documents. During a big story, storm appears in a large share of
today's documents, so the tier weighs storm low and ranks its fresh matches below equally good older ones. Two fixes are
common:

- **Ask first.** Elasticsearch's `dfs_query_then_fetch` collects term counts from every shard, then scores with the
  totals. Scores compare fairly, and every query pays an extra round trip.
- **Publish global statistics.** Each full build writes a table of corpus-wide term counts, and every shard and live
  segment scores with it until the next build. A word that is new today gets an estimate until then.

</details>

### Caching hot queries

Search traffic is skewed: during a big story, thousands of readers type the same few words. A
[result cache](../../scaling/caching/) keyed by the normalized query (lowercased, stopwords dropped) answers those
repeats without any fan-out. Freshness bites again here. A cached result hides any story published after it was cached,
so hot queries get a short time to live, 10 s in this design, and the cache stores only the first page. A takedown
cannot wait 10 s, so a visibility filter checks cached pages too (next section).

### The freshness budget, end to end

The 1 s refresh is only one stage between a reporter pressing publish and a reader finding the story. Add up every
stage on the path:

| Stage                                   | Illustrative time |
| --------------------------------------- | ----------------- |
| Ingest queue lag, publish to indexer    | 2 s               |
| Refresh, buffer to searchable segment   | 1 s (assumed)     |
| Copy the new segment to every replica   | 2 s               |
| Hot-query result cache, time to live    | 10 s              |
| **Worst case, new story on a hot query** | **about 15 s**    |

So the honest promise for the real-time tier is "searchable within seconds, and on a hot query within about 15 s".
Shrinking the cache's time to live buys freshness and costs fan-out: every expiry sends one more query to all 18
shards.

Takedowns run on a different budget, because a legal or safety removal cannot sit in a cache for 10 s. The `DELETE`
call does two things at once:

- **It writes a tombstone** through the normal indexing path, so new queries skip the document after the next refresh.
- **It publishes the docId** to every query server over pub/sub, where it joins an in-memory blocked set within about
  1 s.

Every response passes a final visibility filter against that blocked set, including cache hits. When a cached page
contains a blocked document, the server treats the entry as a miss and runs the query again. An entry leaves the blocked
set once the tombstone has reached every replica and every cached page holding the document has expired.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: fields that change every second</summary>

Suppose the same engine serves a shop with 540M product listings. Titles rarely change, but price and stock change 2,000
times per second across the catalog. That is 172.8M changes a day, about 32% of the catalog, and 33 times the 60 new
documents per second the real-time tier was sized for. Each change through the tier would be a delete plus an add: a
tombstone and a fresh copy of the whole listing, and merges would rewrite the same listings again and again.

Split the fast fields out instead. Keep price and stock in a column store keyed by document id and update it in place.
At 16 bytes per listing that is 8.64 GB, or 480 MB per shard across 18 shards, small enough to hold in memory. Each
shard matches text in its segments as before, then reads price and stock for the candidates, drops out-of-stock items,
and applies price filters and sorts from the fresh column.

</details>

## Failure walkthrough: the indexer crashes mid-flush

Each shard's indexer reads new documents from the ingest queue, buffers them in memory, writes a live segment every
second, and commits to disk every 30 s. A commit records the segments on disk and the queue offset they cover in one
write, so a commit point always pairs segments with the offset that produced them. Replicas copy each new segment as
soon as the indexer writes it.

At 10:31, while the storm story is breaking, shard 7's indexer crashes in the middle of writing a segment.

1. **What survives.** The queue still holds every document, because the indexer records an offset only inside a
   commit. The main index and the last commit point are untouched. The half-written segment never joined a commit, so
   the restart ignores it, along with every segment written since the last commit.
2. **Readers during recovery.** Shard 7's replicas keep serving the segments they already copied, so a reader loses
   nothing that was already searchable. Stories that reach shard 7 after the crash stay unsearchable there until the
   indexer catches up. The other 17 shards keep their 1 s refresh.
3. **The replay.** The restarted indexer reopens the last commit and reads the queue from the offset it recorded. At
   60 documents per second spread over 18 shards, that is at most 30 s of input, about 100 documents. Some of them
   already sit in segments the replicas copied, and the queue itself delivers
   [at least once](../../async/delivery-semantics/), so duplicates are certain. Every document carries its docId, and
   the indexer writes each one as an upsert: a tombstone for any older copy, then the add. The replay leaves one copy
   of each story.
4. **Back to normal.** The replicas copy the rebuilt segments and drop the ones the indexer discarded. Replaying 100
   documents takes well under a second, so the freshness budget stretches by the restart time. No story is lost, and
   none appears twice.

## Trade-offs

The nightly rebuild is simple and ranks well, and it is blind to anything newer than its last build. Rebuilding more
often narrows that window at the cost of rewriting the whole corpus each time. Batch plus delta, one design with a full
build and a real-time tier, closes the window almost completely, and every query pays for the extra segments. Signal
freshness is a separate dial: an incrementally updated signal store keeps popularity current under any index design.

<TradeoffTable title="Keeping a search index fresh">

| Design                            | What it buys                                                           | What it costs                                                                                            |
| --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Nightly rebuild only              | One index, full signals on every document, 1 segment per query         | New documents hidden for hours: 38% recall, 9.5 h unsearchable on average in the toy breaking day        |
| Rebuild every few hours           | Fresher results with the same simple serving path                      | Rewrites the whole corpus each time: 6 builds and 11,355 writes in the toy, still hours behind           |
| Batch plus delta (real-time tier) | New documents searchable after an assumed 1 s refresh, full signals for everything older | 5.5 segments per query in the toy, merge work, text-only ranking for new documents, two paths |
| Incremental signal store          | Click counts current within seconds for every document, old or new     | A second store to keep consistent, a lookup per candidate at query time, still no clicks for new stories |
| Sharding by term                  | A query touches only the shards that hold its terms                    | Hot shards for popular terms, intersections across machines, many shards per indexed document            |

</TradeoffTable>

The real-time tier wins when queries chase new documents: news, social posts, job listings, fresh products. For a
catalog of old documents, a nightly rebuild loses little and costs far less.

## Extension: typeahead

Typeahead suggests completions while the reader types. It fires on every keystroke, so at 6 keystrokes per search it
takes 120,000 requests per second at peak. Each answer must arrive in under 100 ms end to end (illustrative), and the
network round trip uses most of that, so the lookup itself has to run in memory in about a millisecond.

A **trie** stores one node per prefix: s, st, sto, and so on. Finding a prefix takes one step per typed character.
Collecting every query under a short prefix like s would still scan a large share of the log, so each node stores its
**top k completions**, computed ahead of time. An offline job counts queries in the log every hour or day, builds a new
trie, and swaps it in.

<TypeaheadTrieWidget client:visible />

What to look for:

- **One step per character.** The prefix sto visits 4 nodes, the root plus one per letter, and reads the stored answer:
  storm, stock market today, storm tracker.
- **The scan reads everything.** Without the trie, every prefix reads all 16 logged queries, and a real log holds
  millions.
- **A dead end stops early.** The prefix stx visits 3 nodes and returns nothing.
- **Memory buys speed.** The trie has more nodes than the log has queries, and each node keeps its own list.

The freshness problem returns in miniature. A query that starts trending at 10:30 stays out of the suggestions until
the next offline count. The same fix applies: a small, fast-updating list of trending queries, merged with the trie's
answer at serve time. Short prefixes are few and hot, so a cache in front of the trie, or in the browser, absorbs much
of the load.

## Honest simplifications

This lesson's model is a toy. Here is what it leaves out:

- **The ideal ranking is an oracle.** It knows every document and its full-signal score the moment it exists. Real
  teams estimate recall from human relevance judgments and click logs.
- **One shard, one machine.** The duel has no network, replicas, or tail latency. Segments per query stands in for the
  cost of fan-out.
- **A neutral score for new documents.** Live documents get a flat quality score. Real systems guess better from the
  source's reputation or a fast spam classifier.
- **Signals change only at build time.** The duel bakes popularity and quality into each build, so an older document's
  score stays fixed all day. Real systems update click counts incrementally and often read them at query time.
- **No edits or deletes in the duel.** Documents only arrive; tombstones appear in the prose only.
- **Toy scores.** Each document's text relevance and quality are seeded random draws, and spam is a share of documents
  with high text scores and low quality.
- **A fixed merge policy.** The duel merges any 4 segments on one level. Real policies weigh segment sizes and the share
  of deleted documents.
- **15-minute ticks.** The real-time tier flushes once per tick, and the lesson reports its time unsearchable as an
  assumed 1 s refresh interval. The duel models no queue lag, replica copy, or cache expiry; the freshness budget adds
  those by hand.
- **Builds snapshot at their start.** A build covers everything that arrived before it started and nothing from its 3 h
  run.
- **Simplified BM25.** The inverted index widget skips length normalization and uses one popularity signal with a fixed
  weight. Its term counts come from one small collection on one machine, so it shows none of the per-shard skew
  described in the sharding section.

<KeyTakeaways>

- An inverted index answers a query by reading only its terms' posting lists, so the work grows with those lists and
  never with the corpus.
- Freshness runs on separate clocks: index refresh, signal updates, signal freshness, and model training. Fix the slow
  one, and measure the whole path from publish to result, cache included.
- Batch plus delta keeps a full build for ranking and a small real-time tier for new documents. Every query pays for the
  extra segments, so it wins only when queries chase new documents.
- Shards and tiers score with their own term counts. Share global statistics, or accept and name the skew.
- A takedown needs its own path: a tombstone for the index and a visibility filter that checks every response,
  cached or not.

</KeyTakeaways>
