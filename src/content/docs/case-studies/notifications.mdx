## The failure: the code that came too late

Ana taps **Sign in** on a new laptop, and the site texts her a six-digit code. At the same moment, marketing launches a
spring sale to five million phones, and the sale enters the same queue as her code. The text arrives 42 minutes later,
long after the code expired. Ana tried three times, gave up, and called support.

Every part did its job. The queue kept its order, the SMS provider sent at its contracted rate, and nothing crashed. The
design treated a login code and a coupon as equals.

## The idea: send what matters first, and stop sending what expired

This case study has one dominant constraint: **each message must reach the user through someone else's provider before
it stops being useful, and only if the user wants it.** Apple's and Google's push services, SMS carriers, and email
relays carry the last mile, each with its own rate limit and its own outages. You control only what you hand them, in
what order, and where you go when one fails.

Login codes and security alerts ride a **priority lane** ahead of social updates and campaigns. A code that reaches its
**deadline** unsent leaves the queue, since a late code only wastes a send. Senders retry failures with **backoff**, and
a **circuit breaker** moves traffic to a **backup provider** when the primary keeps failing. The notifier checks
**preferences** and quiet hours before anything enters a lane.

## Walk the design checklist

The seven steps from the [design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/), applied to a notification service.

<Steps>

1. **Requirements.** Send login codes, security alerts, social updates, and campaigns through push, SMS, and email. A
   login code reaches the phone within seconds and is worthless after it expires. Respect each user's channels,
   opt-outs, and quiet hours. Send each notification once per channel, as far as the providers allow. Message templates,
   copy experiments, and analytics stay out of scope.

2. **Estimates (illustrative).** Twenty million daily users who receive 5 notifications each make **100 million a day**,
   about **1,157 per second** on average and **3,472 per second** at a 3× peak, spread across push, SMS, and email
   providers. Login codes are a thin slice: 2 million a day is about **23 per second**. Campaigns cause the bursts. A
   campaign to **5 million** phones, sent through one first-in, first-out queue and an SMS account that accepts 2,000
   sends per second, holds that account for about **42 minutes**. Paying for 20,000 sends per second shrinks that to
   about **4 minutes**, still far longer than anyone waits for a code. At 500 bytes a record, the sent log grows by about
   **50 GB a day**. (See [requirements and estimates](../../thinking/requirements-and-estimates/) for the method.)

3. **API.** Internal services call `POST /notifications` with `{idempotencyKey, userId, type, data, deadline}` and get
   back `202 Accepted {id}`. The key makes a retried request harmless. Users manage their choices with
   `GET /users/{id}/preferences` and `PUT /users/{id}/preferences`. Providers report back through webhooks such as
   `POST /receipts/{provider}`, with outcomes like delivered, bounced, or invalid token.

4. **Data model.** A `notifications` table keyed by ID, with the user, type, lane, deadline, status, attempt count,
   provider, and the provider's message ID. It doubles as the sent log, partitioned by day, with old days dropped after
   30 days. A `preferences` table holds, per user, the allowed channels for each type, opt-outs, quiet hours, and time
   zone. Every request reads it, so keep it in a cache (see [caching](../../scaling/caching/)). A `devices` table holds
   each install's push token, deleted when APNs or FCM reports it invalid. An `outbox` table holds one row per
   notification still owed to a lane, written in the same transaction as the notification itself.

5. **High-level design.** The notifier deduplicates on the idempotency key, checks preferences, and writes the
   notification row and its outbox row in one transaction. A relay publishes each ID to one of three lanes: priority for
   codes and security alerts, normal for social updates, and bulk for campaigns. It marks the outbox row sent once the
   queue confirms. Senders drain the lanes in that order, recheck preferences, take tokens from one bucket per provider
   to stay under each rate limit, and keep a circuit breaker per provider. The
   [Queues and backpressure](../../reliability/queues-and-backpressure/) lesson covers the lanes, and the
   [Rate limiting](../../reliability/rate-limiting/) lesson covers the token bucket.

6. **Deep dive: deadlines under bursts and outages.** This is where the design breaks first, and both simulations below
   live here. A campaign buries codes in a shared queue, and a provider outage turns every retry into a wasted send.
   Priority lanes fix the first. A circuit breaker and a backup provider fix the second.

7. **Trade-offs.** Lanes against fairness, dropping against sending late, and one provider against two. The table at the
   end of this page lays them out.

</Steps>

## Try it: keep codes ahead of a campaign

Both lanes receive the same 60 code requests over 24 s and the same campaign, and both send through the same SMS
provider at 20 sends per second. Only the queue differs. Time is compressed: a code lives 5 s here and minutes in real
life, and the campaign holds 300 messages here and millions in real life. The shape of the failure stays the same.

<NotificationsWidget client:visible duel="deadline" />

What to look for:

- **Campaign at 2 s.** At 2.2 s the priority lane sends code 9 past 298 marketing messages. In the shared queue, code 9
  waits until 7.2 s, its whole 5 s lifetime, and the queue still sends it later. The shared queue sends **28 of 60**
  codes late, with an average wait of **5.2 s**. The priority lane sends all 60 on time, each the moment it arrives. The
  campaign pays for that: its last message goes out at **19.1 s** instead of **17.0 s**.
- **Pay for double the rate.** At 40 sends per second the campaign drains in half the time, and the shared queue still
  misses **5** codes. The priority lane misses **0**, and its campaign finishes at **10.0 s** instead of **9.5 s**. More
  capacity shrinks the window. The lane closes it.
- **No campaign.** With nothing to jump past, the lanes match exactly. A priority lane costs nothing when the queue is
  quiet.

This demo measures **dispatch**: a code counts as on time when a sender hands it to the provider (APNs, FCM, or an SMS
gateway) within 5 s of the request. Dispatch is the part of the path you control, so give it its own objective, such
as the share of codes handed over before their deadline. **Delivery** to the phone is a separate objective, and it
adds time the demo leaves out: the provider's own queueing and latency, plus the minutes or hours a phone spends
offline or asleep while the provider holds the message. Set each code's time to live from its deadline
(`apns-expiration` for APNs, `ttl` for FCM), so the provider discards the code once it expires, and give it a collapse
key (`apns-collapse-id`, FCM's `collapse_key`), so a phone that comes back online shows only the newest code. Measure
delivery from provider receipts and the app's own acks. Both arrive late or never, so a code with no receipt by its
deadline counts as a miss, and a receipt that shows up later never turns that miss into a success.

Strict priority has a failure of its own: a long burst of codes can starve the bulk lane. Many systems give the lower
lanes a small guaranteed share of each second, so campaigns slow down without stopping.

## Failover and circuit breakers

Providers fail. A carrier's SMS gateway goes down, a push service throttles you after a burst, or an email relay starts
bouncing everything. The first response is a retry with backoff: wait 0.5 s, then 1, 2, and 4 s, so a brief blip heals
without hammering the provider. Retries alone cannot outlast an outage longer than the deadline, since every retry goes
to the same dead provider.

A **circuit breaker** counts failures in a row for each provider. After a threshold, 3 in the toy, it opens: senders stop
calling the primary and route codes to a backup. While the breaker is open, a health check probes the primary every 2 s,
and the first success closes the breaker. The backup is often slower or pricier. Here it accepts 10 sends per second,
half the primary rate. The [Circuit breakers](../../reliability/circuit-breakers/) lesson covers the states in full, and the [Retries with backoff and jitter](../../reliability/retries/)
lesson explains why real retries add randomness, which this toy leaves out so every replay matches.

## Try it: survive a provider outage

Both lanes receive the same 60 code requests, and the primary SMS provider rejects every send from 5 s to 15 s. Both
retry with backoff and drop a code at its deadline. Only one lane has a circuit breaker and a backup provider.

What to look for:

- **Primary down 5 to 15 s.** The retry lane sends each code to the dead primary again and again. At 10.1 s code 16
  reaches its deadline after 4 failed sends, and the lane drops it. By the end the retry lane drops **15** codes after
  **85** failed sends. The failover lane fails **3** sends, opens its breaker at 5.9 s, and the backup carries **27**
  codes, all on time. At 15.9 s a health check finds the primary healthy, and the breaker closes.
- **Longer outage.** With the primary down from 5 to 25 s, the retry lane drops **42** codes after **177** failed sends.
  The failover lane still fails only **3** sends and misses **0**, and the backup carries **45** codes.
- **No outage.** The breaker never opens, and the lanes match.

## Send once, and only when wanted

Retries and failover create duplicates. A provider can send a text and then time out before it answers. The sender sees
a failure, retries or fails over, and Ana gets two texts. One ID per notification guards each hop: callers pass an
idempotency key, so a retried request creates one notification. Senders pass the notification ID to providers that
accept an idempotency key, and record each send in the sent log. Some providers accept no key, and a sender can crash
between a send and its log write, so a duplicate stays possible. Retries also stop at the deadline, so a notification can
arrive twice or not at all. A second login code is harmless, so write every message to read fine twice. The [Idempotency](../../reliability/idempotency/) lesson and the [Delivery
semantics and the outbox](../../async/delivery-semantics/) lesson cover these guarantees.

Preferences decide whether a message goes at all. Check opt-outs and quiet hours before enqueueing, so a muted campaign
never takes a lane slot or a send from the rate limit. A bulk message may wait for hours, and the user may opt out or
enter quiet hours meanwhile, so the sender reads the current preferences again at send time, with quiet hours in the
user's own time zone. Security messages skip quiet hours by design: a code the user just asked for is always
wanted.

Channels differ too. APNs and FCM deliver pushes to installed apps, may hold or merge them to save battery, and report
tokens that went stale. SMS costs money per message, and carriers filter bulk traffic. Email is cheap and slow, and lands
in spam when sent carelessly. A fallback chain, such as push and then SMS, needs the same care: send the SMS only when
push had its chance and failed.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: a security alert across push, SMS, and email</summary>

A security alert, "New sign-in from Lisbon", must reach the user within 2 minutes. It goes by push first, then SMS if
push does not land, then email if SMS fails. Quiet hours never hold it back, a user whose push arrives normally gets no
SMS, and a retried step avoids sending the same channel twice. Give it one notification, one deadline, and channels
tried in order.

- **One attempt row per channel, with a state:** the alert gets one ID and a deadline 2 minutes out. Each channel's
  attempt is a row in `attempts`, keyed by `(notification_id, channel)`. The step writes `pending` before it calls the
  provider, then `sent` when the provider accepts, or `unknown` when the call times out. A retried step that finds
  `sent` stops. One that finds `pending` or `unknown` (say, after a crash between the write and the call) sends again
  with the notification ID as the provider's idempotency key. A provider that honors keys sends once; one without keys
  may send twice.
- **Confirm from the phone:** push services accept a message without proving it arrived. The app calls
  `POST /notifications/{id}/ack` when it shows the alert, and the ack cancels the next step.
- **A timer per step:** with no ack after 30 s, a scheduled job sends the SMS through the priority lane. If the SMS
  provider rejects it or no delivery receipt arrives within 60 s, the job sends email. The deadline ends the chain.
- **Skip quiet hours by type:** security alerts ignore quiet hours, and preferences may move them between channels but
  can never turn all channels off.
- **Accept a rare double:** a push that lands after its 30 s timer means the user sees both. Write the SMS to read as a
  second copy, with the same wording and sign-in time.

</details>

## Failure walkthrough: the notifier crashes between commit and publish

Ana's code request commits at 09:00:00.010. The notifier crashes at 09:00:00.020, before it publishes anything to a
lane. Without an outbox, the row sits in the `notifications` table as `pending`, and no lane holds its ID. The login
service already got `202 Accepted`, so nothing retries. Ana waits for a code that never comes, and nothing reports an
error.

With the outbox, the same transaction that stored the notification also stored a row that says "publish notification
4711 to the priority lane". The two rows commit together or not at all, so a stored notification always has its
publish recorded.

1. **The relay restarts.** It reads unsent outbox rows, priority rows first, and finds notification 4711. A relay that
   read the oldest rows first would bury codes behind a campaign's rows again, so each lane gets its own relay.
2. **It publishes again.** The relay may have published the ID just before the crash, so the priority lane can now
   hold two copies. The relay marks the row sent only after the queue confirms, which makes delivery
   [at least once](../../async/delivery-semantics/).
3. **Senders skip the duplicate.** Before it calls the provider, a sender flips the notification's row in the sent log
   from `pending` to `sending`, and only one flip can succeed. The first copy wins and goes to the SMS provider. The
   second finds the row already `sending` and stops.
4. **Ana gets her code late, or asks again.** The delay equals the relay's downtime plus the lane wait. If that pushes
   the code past its deadline, the sender drops it unsent, and the login screen offers a new code. Either way, the
   request never vanishes without a trace.

Change data capture (CDC) is the common alternative: a connector tails the database's commit log and publishes each
new notification to its lane, with the same at-least-once guarantee and no outbox table to clean up. Either way,
senders must tolerate duplicates.

## Notifications versus chat

[Chat](../chat/) is the other half of this pair. Both deliver short messages to people's devices, and they guard
different things.

| Question           | Notifications                                             | Chat                                                   |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Who owns delivery? | External providers (push services, SMS, email)            | Your gateways, over sockets you hold open              |
| What must hold?    | Arrival before a deadline, within the user's choices      | Order within a conversation, and nothing missing       |
| What goes wrong?   | A provider throttles or fails, and the code expires       | Messages overtake each other or vanish in dead sockets |
| The guard          | Priority lanes, deadlines, and failover between providers | Sequence numbers and a cursor into a replayable log    |
| Late is…           | Often worthless: a login code that arrives late is junk   | Fine, if the order is right                            |

The two meet when a chat app is closed. The chat server asks the push service to wake the phone, and the phone opens its
socket and catches up with its cursor. The push carries a nudge, and the chat log carries the messages.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Designing a notification service">

| Choice                                | What it buys                                    | What it costs                                                  |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Priority lanes by type                | Codes skip any backlog                          | Bulk waits longer, and starves without a guaranteed share      |
| Drop a code at its deadline           | Sends go to codes that can still help           | The user never gets the dropped code and must ask again        |
| Buy more provider capacity            | Every lane drains faster                        | Money, and a big enough campaign still buries codes in one queue |
| Retry with backoff                    | Brief provider blips heal on their own          | Cannot outlast an outage longer than the deadline              |
| Circuit breaker and a backup provider | Codes keep flowing through an outage            | A second contract, often slower or pricier, and more duplicates |
| Check preferences before enqueueing and at send | Muted messages cost nothing downstream, and late opt-outs hold | Two preference reads per message, so they need a fast cache |
| Outbox in the notification's transaction | A crash after the commit never loses a notification | A relay per lane to run, and duplicates the senders must skip |

</TradeoffTable>

<KeyTakeaways>

- One first-in, first-out queue treats a login code and a coupon as equals. Priority lanes put codes ahead of any
  campaign, and a deadline drops a code that can no longer help.
- Dispatch and delivery are two objectives. The demo counts the handoff to the provider, and delivery adds provider
  latency, offline phones, TTL expiry, and receipts that arrive late or never.
- Retries cannot outlast an outage longer than the deadline. A circuit breaker per provider fails over to a backup and
  probes the primary until it recovers.
- Commit each notification with its outbox row, or use CDC. Delivery is at least once, so senders skip IDs they
  already sent, and every message must read fine twice.

</KeyTakeaways>

<div slot="answer">

**The SMS account breaks first.**

- **The new numbers.** 50 million texts at 2,000 sends per second hold the account for 25,000 s, about 7 hours
  instead of about 42 minutes. Paying for 20,000 sends per second still takes about 42 minutes. Codes stay on time:
  at about 23 per second they ride the priority lane and use about 1% of the account. The campaign adds about 25 GB
  to the sent log, half of a normal day's growth.
- **The change.** Send the campaign by push to every phone with the app installed, and keep SMS for the rest. Push
  has its own rate limits, separate from the SMS account, and costs nothing per message. Spread the texts by time
  zone, which also respects quiet hours. If 80% of the phones have the app, 10 million texts at 2,000 per second
  take about 83 minutes. The cost: push reaches only users with the app and notifications on, the sale reaches
  people across a whole day, and every remaining text still costs money.
- **The crash.** For 60 s, every new notification commits with its outbox row and waits: about 1,400 codes, plus
  whatever campaign rows the notifier stored. When the relays restart, the priority relay publishes the codes first,
  and the campaign rows wait in their own lane. Codes that outlived their deadline drop unsent, and those users ask
  again. Rows published before the crash but never marked sent go out twice, and senders skip the IDs already in the
  sent log. Nothing is lost, and the campaign finishes about 60 s late.
