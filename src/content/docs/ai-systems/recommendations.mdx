## The failure: scoring the whole catalog

The support assistant works for a large online store that sells about 3,000,000 items. A customer's order includes a
50 ft green garden hose from Verdant, and it just went out of stock. The assistant should suggest five replacements in
the chat, and the recommendation step gets an illustrative 50 ms of the reply's
[latency budget](../evaluation-and-budgets/).

The first version is simple. A ranking model with rich features (price, reviews, length, brand, and how closely each
item matches the hose) scores every item in the catalog, and the assistant shows the top five. On a test with a small
catalog, the list looks perfect.

The quick fix fails the other way. A second version skips the ranker and shows the store's five best sellers. It
answers in about 2 ms and offers a phone case, a USB charger, wireless earbuds, paper towels, and a water bottle to a
customer who wanted a garden hose.

One version is accurate and far too slow. The other is fast and useless. A real system spends the expensive model only
on the items that have a chance.

## The idea: a funnel

A recommendation system narrows the catalog in stages. **Candidate generation** uses cheap sources to pull a few
hundred plausible items out of millions. **Ranking** scores only those candidates with an accurate, expensive model.
Filters and a re-rank then apply business rules and pick the handful the customer sees. Published systems use the same
shape: in YouTube deep neural network recommendations (RecSys 2016), candidate generation narrows a corpus of millions
to hundreds, ranking scores a few hundred, and the funnel goes from millions to hundreds to dozens.

<StepDiagram client:visible title="From 3,000,000 items to five" nodes={nodes} edges={edges} steps={steps} height={300} />

<Analogy>

Hiring five people for a busy store. Three thousand people apply. A quick resume screen keeps a few hundred who list
the right skills, interviews rank those carefully, reference checks remove anyone who cannot start, and the manager
picks five so that no single team gets every hire. The screen is cheap and the interviews are expensive. If the screen
rejects the best applicant, no interview can bring them back.

</Analogy>

### Where the time goes

| Stage | Items in | Items out | Dropped | Illustrative time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Candidate generation (three sources, then merge) | 3,000,000 | 489 | 2,999,511 | 9 ms |
| Ranker (rich features) | 489 | 15 | 474 | 4.89 ms |
| Filters (stock, shipping, recalls) | 15 | 12 | 3 | 0.5 ms |
| Re-rank and pick (at most two per brand) | 12 | 5 | 7 | 0.5 ms |
| Total | | | | 14.89 ms |

Every row balances: items in equals items out plus items dropped. Candidate generation removes almost everything in
9 ms because each source is a lookup, while the ranker spends 10 microseconds on every item it sees.

### Recall caps quality

- **The ranker orders what it receives.** An item that never reaches the ranker never gets a score, however good it
  is. The best the final list can be is the best set of candidates.
- **Measure recall at the candidate stage.** Keep a set of requests with known good answers, and count how many good
  items survive candidate generation. In the funnel run, 9 of 10 survive. The RV fresh water hose sits at rank 900
  among embedding neighbors, beyond the 300 that source returns.
- **Fix missed items at the source.** Add a source that sees a different signal, or give the source that finds them a
  bigger share. A heavier ranker cannot help.
- **Precompute the sources.** Embeddings, bought together lists, and category best sellers come from background jobs,
  and the request only looks them up. This is [caching](../../scaling/caching/) applied to recommendations, and the
  lists go stale between refreshes.
- **Filter after ranking, and keep spares.** The ranker keeps 15 for five slots, so stock, shipping, and diversity
  rules can remove items and still leave a full list.

## How many candidates?

The funnel sends 500 candidates to the ranker. Why 500, instead of 50 or 5,000? Two curves decide. **Recall**, the
share of the 10 known good replacements that reach the ranker, climbs fast and then flattens. **Ranker time** grows in
a straight line with every candidate. Drag the slider and watch both.

<RecBudgetWidget client:visible />

In the toy model, recall reaches 70% at 50 candidates, 90% at 500, and 100% at 2,000. Total time is 14.89 ms at 500 and
38.89 ms at 2,000, and it crosses the 50 ms budget near 2,700 candidates. Below 50 candidates the best replacement
never reaches the ranker. Past 2,000 every extra candidate costs time and finds nothing new. This is why production
funnels hand the ranker hundreds of candidates: recall has mostly flattened, and the reply keeps room under the budget.
Measure both curves on real requests before you settle on a number.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Designing a recommendation funnel">

| Choice | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| More candidates (thousands) | Higher recall, fewer good items missed | Ranker time grows with every candidate |
| Fewer candidates (dozens) | Fast, cheap ranking | Good items never reach the ranker, which caps quality |
| One candidate source | Simple to build, refresh, and debug | Misses items that only another signal finds |
| Several candidate sources | Higher recall from different signals | More pipelines to build, refresh, merge, and dedupe |
| Heavy ranker (rich features) | Better order at the top of the list | More time per item, so fewer candidates fit the budget |
| Light ranker | Scores more candidates in the same time | Weaker order, so worse items reach the top |
| Precompute (lists built in the background) | Fast lookups at request time | Stale lists: new items and stock changes lag until the next refresh |
| Compute per request | Fresh results that use the current order and customer | Spends the latency budget on every request |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

Customers often return some hoses because they leak. The store asks the assistant to stop suggesting items that are
frequently returned. Where does this go in the funnel, what data does it need, and how do you still show five good
replacements when it removes items?

<div slot="answer">

- **Placement:** a hard rule goes in the filter stage after the ranker, next to the stock and shipping checks. A softer
  rule adds the return rate as a ranker feature or a score penalty, so a slightly better item with a few returns can
  still win.
- **Data:** a return rate per item, computed by a background job from orders and returns and refreshed daily. The
  request reads a precomputed number, the same way it reads the category lists.
- **Threshold:** compare each item with similar items (for example, returned three times as often as other 50 ft
  hoses), and require a minimum number of orders so that one return cannot remove a new item.
- **Backfill:** the ranker already keeps 15 for five slots. If the new filter often leaves fewer than five, raise the
  number the ranker keeps; the candidate count stays the same.
- **Measure:** track the return rate and how often customers accept a suggested replacement, before and after the
  change. Add requests whose best match is often returned to the evaluation set, so the rule stays tested.

</div>

</Challenge>
