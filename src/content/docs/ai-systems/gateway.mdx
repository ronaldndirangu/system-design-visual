## The failure: every feature calls the model directly

In [Evaluation and budgets](../evaluation-and-budgets/), Medium was the only candidate that met every budget on the eval
set. So the team wired it in the simplest way: the chat, the refund form, and the agent tools each call Medium directly.
Three stores share the assistant, one API key, and one upstream rate limit of 15 requests per second (req/s).

Three bad days follow:

- **A noisy tenant.** Store A runs a bulk backfill that replays old tickets through the assistant. Its traffic jumps to
  25 req/s, and the shared limit starts rejecting requests from every store.
- **A malformed reply.** The refund form expects JSON with an order id, an amount, and a reason. One reply leaves out the
  amount, and the form breaks in front of a customer.
- **An outage.** Medium returns errors for eight seconds. Every feature fails at once, because nothing else can answer.

Each problem has a known fix, and every fix lives in the same place: a gateway that each model call passes through.

## The idea: one door in front of every model call

An **AI gateway** is a service between your features and your models. Every model call passes through it, so it is the
one place to enforce the rules that keep a shared model healthy: who may call how often, which answers can be reused,
which model handles a request, whether the reply is usable, and what happens when a model fails. It plays the role a
[load balancer](../../scaling/load-balancing/) plays for web servers, with checks that only AI traffic needs.

no need to page anyone. The triage nurse sends minor cases to a nurse practitioner and serious ones to a doctor, and some
hospitals let the nurse practitioner try first and call the doctor when stuck. Discharge papers get checked before the
patient leaves. When cardiology is in surgery, the desk sends patients to the on-call doctor and checks back later.

### Per-store quotas

The fix for the noisy tenant is a **quota per tenant**, enforced with a **token bucket**. Each store gets a bucket that
refills at its quota rate and holds a couple of seconds of burst. Each request takes one token (a permit, unrelated to
model tokens). When the bucket is empty, the gateway replies at once with HTTP status 429 and a Retry-After header, which
tells the client when to try again.

In the widget, Store A's quota is 6 req/s and Stores B and C get 4.5 req/s each. The quotas add up to the 15 req/s
upstream limit, so the shared limit holds even when every store spends its full quota at once. Sell more quota than the
upstream limit and the noisy tenant problem returns at the next busy hour.

- **Shed early.** A rejected request costs the gateway almost nothing. A forwarded one costs a model call that the shared
  limit may reject anyway, or worse, one that succeeds and starves another store.
- **Count what is scarce.** A long prompt costs far more model work than a short one, so production gateways often meter
  tokens per minute as well as requests per second.
- **Split batch from live.** Give backfills and other batch work their own lower quota, so they cannot crowd out a
  customer waiting in the chat.
- **Retries count.** A validator retry spends the store's quota, so a store whose requests keep failing cannot hide
  extra load.

### Fallbacks and the circuit breaker

A **fallback** is the next best answer when the main path fails: a smaller model, a cached answer, or a canned handoff
to a person. A **circuit breaker** decides when to use it. It is a small state machine in the gateway:

| State | What the gateway does | It moves on when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed | Sends requests to Medium | Three errors in a row open it |
| Open | Sends new requests to Small without trying Medium | The cool down (3 s in the widget) ends |
| Half open | Lets one probe request try Medium | The probe succeeds (closed) or fails (open again) |

Without a breaker, every request during an outage waits for Medium to fail before it falls back. With one, the gateway
stops calling a model it knows is failing, and the probe finds out when the model recovers. In the outage scenario the
breaker opens three times: two probes land inside the outage and fail, and the third one closes it. This is the
[health check](../../scaling/load-balancing/) idea from load balancing, applied to a model.

A fallback is degraded service. In the cascade toy below, Small passes hard questions only 35% of the time, against 85%
for Medium. Falling back to Small keeps the chat alive, but a refund form that needs correct numbers should hand off to
a person instead.

### Structured output validation

When a feature needs structured data, such as the refund form's order id, amount, and reason, the gateway checks every
reply before the feature sees it:

1. **Parse and check the schema.** The reply must be valid JSON with every required field, each of the right type.
2. **Check business rules.** The amount must be positive and at most the order total, and the order must belong to this
   customer. A schema cannot express these.
3. **Retry once, then fall back.** A rejected reply gets one more attempt, often with the validation error added to the
   prompt. If that attempt fails too, the customer gets the handoff.

Keep the retry bounded. If the client, the gateway, and the feature each retry three times, one bad request can become
27 model calls, just when the model is struggling. That is a **retry storm**. One retry, counted against the store's
quota, caps the extra work.

## Routing versus a cascade

Now the question the last lesson left open: can two models together beat Medium alone on cost and still meet the
budgets? Two strategies split the work between Small and Medium.

- **Routing** decides before the call. A cheap classifier guesses whether a question is easy or hard, then sends it to
  Small or Medium. Each request pays for one model call plus the classifier. A wrong guess is a **misroute**: a hard
  question lands on Small and probably fails, or an easy one pays for Medium.
- **A cascade** decides after the call. Every request tries Small first. A checker reads the answer and either accepts
  it or **escalates** the question to Medium. An escalated request pays for both calls and waits for both.

Both strategies answer the same 60 seeded questions side by side, with Medium alone on the same questions as a baseline.
The budgets come from the last lesson: at least 85% pass, a p95 latency of 2 s or less, and at most 6,000 units per
1,000 requests. Press **Play**, **Step** one question at a time, or **Skip to the end**.

<GatewayCascadeWidget client:visible />

What to look for on the mostly easy mix:

- **The cascade wins on quality and cost.** It passes 59 of 60 (98.3%) and spends about 1,709 units per 1,000 requests,
  about 57% less than Medium alone at 3,949.
- **The cascade misses the latency budget.** Ten of 60 requests escalate, about 17%. Since p95 is the time that 95% of
  answers beat, once more than 5% of requests wait for two calls, one of them sets the p95: 2.31 s against a 2 s budget.
- **Routing fits every budget.** It passes 53 of 60 (88.3%) with a p95 of 1.6 s and about 1,870 units. Its 8 misroutes
  cost some quality, and the easy mix leaves enough room to absorb them.

Pick the strategy from the traffic you actually get:

- **Routing** suits a tight latency budget and questions whose difficulty shows in the text.
- **A cascade** suits a mostly easy mix, some slack in the latency budget, and a checker you can trust.
- **One model** suits a hard mix, or budgets with no slack. It is also the simplest to run and to debug.

This is a **toy model**. Pass chances, the classifier, and the checker are toy numbers, and latency and cost are
illustrative units. It checks quality, p95, and cost, and leaves out the last lesson's must decline budget. A real
cascade needs a checker that catches every leaked address from Small, since one miss is a privacy incident. That
argues for routing sensitive question types straight to the stronger model.

Research agrees that the savings are real and depend on the mix. FrugalGPT (arXiv 2305.05176) chains models from cheap to
expensive and learns when to stop. It reports up to 98% lower cost while matching the best single model on one dataset,
and savings of 59% to 73% on others. It also needs labeled data and a trained scorer to decide when to accept an
answer. Routing has the same need: the classifier is a model too, and it learns from labeled examples of easy and hard
questions.

## Two caches

The word cache covers two different things in an AI gateway. One reuses the answer. The other reuses the work of reading
the prompt.

### Response cache: reuse the answer

A **response cache** stores the final answer and returns it for a matching request without calling a model. It works
like any [cache](../../scaling/caching/), and a hit is the cheapest answer there is. The danger is the key. Two requests
may share a cached answer only when everything that shapes the answer matches:

| Key field | Why it must be there |
| --- | --- |
| Store (tenant) | Store A allows returns for 30 days, and Store B allows 14 |
| User role (permissions) | An agent's answer can include internal notes that a customer must never see |
| Prompt version | A new prompt or policy changes the right answer |
| Model | The fallback's short answer should never stand in for Medium's |
| Settings | Temperature and output format change the reply, for example prose versus JSON |
| The question | The exact text the customer asked |

A **semantic cache** goes further and matches questions by meaning. "How long do I have to return shoes?" and "How long
do I have to return a sale item?" look alike, and they can have different answers. A fixed similarity threshold gives no
guarantee that a hit is correct. Use a semantic cache only where a near miss is harmless, and keep every other key field
exact.

### Prefix (KV) cache: reuse the prompt work

A **prefix cache** works inside the model server. Before a model writes its first token, it reads the whole prompt and
computes attention state, the keys and values (KV), for every prompt token. This step is the **prefill**. Every prompt
in the support assistant starts with the same system prompt and store policy, so the server repeats that work on every
request. A prefix cache keeps the computed state in fixed-size blocks and reuses them when a new request starts with
exactly the same tokens.

Its rules differ from a response cache:

- **It still generates a fresh answer.** It skips repeated prompt work and never returns a stored reply, so it cannot
  serve a stale or wrong answer.
- **It helps time to first token.** Time to first token is the queue wait plus the prefill, so skipping prefill cuts it.
  The time per output token comes mostly from decode, which does not change.
- **It needs an exact prefix.** Each block's hash includes the block before it, so a change near the start invalidates
  every later block. Put stable text first (system prompt, policy) and changing text last (timestamps, the question).
  Only full blocks are cached.
- **Reuse across stores is a choice.** A cache salt, such as one per store, keeps each store's blocks separate.

The widget shows both caches. In response mode, nine requests pass through the cache in order. Drop a field from the
key and watch which request gets someone else's answer. In prefix mode, one prompt is split into blocks of 16 tokens.
Move the timestamp, change the store, or add a salt, and watch reuse and time to first token change.

<GatewayCacheWidget client:visible />

What to look for:

- **Every field in the key gives one safe hit.** Only the exact repeat at step 2 hits the cache.
- **Drop the store.** At step 3, a Store B customer asks about returns and gets Store A's 30 days. Store B allows 14
  days. The widget flags it as a leak.
- **Drop any other field.** Each one causes exactly one wrong hit: an agent only note shown to a customer, an answer
  from an old prompt, the fallback's short answer, or prose where the form needs JSON.
- **Prefix reuse.** A second request from the same store reuses 22 of 25 blocks and computes only 48 tokens, so time to
  first token falls from 0.35 s to 0.13 s. Put the timestamp at the start of the prompt and reuse drops to zero.
- **Salting.** Without a salt, a request from Store B reuses only the system prompt (10 blocks), since its policy
  differs. With a salt per store it reuses nothing, which trades speed for isolation.

The nine requests are **fixtures**, and the token counts and timings are toy numbers. Real block sizes and speedups
depend on the model server. [Serving models](../llm-serving/) covers prefill, decode, and KV cache memory in depth.
[Retrieval](../retrieval/), the next lesson, adds the store's own documents to the prompt, which makes the cache key and
the tenant scoping matter even more.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Choosing gateway policies">

| Choice | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routing | One model call per request, with steady latency | A classifier that needs labeled data, and misroutes that cost quality |
| Cascade | Large savings when most questions are easy | Escalations pay twice and stretch the p95, and it needs a reliable checker |
| One model for everything | Simple, with predictable quality and latency | The bigger model's price on every easy question |
| Response cache | No model call at all on a hit | Wrong answers when the key misses a field, and stale answers after changes |
| Prefix (KV) cache | Faster time to first token on shared prompts | Needs exact, stable prefixes, and saves prefill only |
| Quota per tenant | One store cannot starve the others | The bursting store gets 429s, and quotas need tuning |
| Strict validation | Broken output never reaches the form | More retries and more handoffs |
| Lenient validation (repair near misses) | Fewer retries | Can accept a reply that parses but is wrong |
| Fallback to a smaller tier | Keeps answering during an outage | Lower quality while it runs |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

A large chain, Store E, joins the assistant. It sends up to 10 req/s at peak, and most of its questions are hard
(warranty claims across thousands of products). Its contract requires a p95 of 2 s or less, it uses the JSON refund
form, and its legal team forbids sharing any cached data with other stores. Design the gateway policy for Store E:
quota, model choice, caching, validation, and fallback.

<div slot="answer">

- **Quota:** a bucket of its own at about 10 req/s with a short burst. The quotas now add up to 25 req/s, more than the
  15 req/s upstream limit, so raise the upstream capacity before Store E goes live. Otherwise one busy hour brings the
  noisy tenant problem back. Give Store E's batch jobs a separate, lower quota.
- **Model choice:** Medium alone, or routing that sends only clearly easy questions to Small. On a hard mix the cascade
  escalates often enough to break a 2 s p95, and broad routing misses the quality floor.
- **Caching:** keep the response cache with every key field, including the store, so Store E's entries can never serve
  another store. Salt the prefix cache per store, so Store E shares no blocks with anyone and still reuses its own
  system prompt and policy.
