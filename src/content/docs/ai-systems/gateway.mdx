## The idea: one door in front of every model call

An **AI gateway** is a service that every model call passes through, so it is the one place to set quotas, reuse
answers, pick a model, check replies, and handle failures. It plays the role a
[load balancer](../../scaling/load-balancing/) plays for web servers.

<StepDiagram client:visible title="One request through the gateway" nodes={nodes} edges={edges} steps={steps} height={300} />

<Analogy>

An emergency department has one front desk. It books a busload of checkups for later so the walk-in with chest pain
gets seen, sends minor cases to a nurse, and calls the on call doctor when cardiology is busy.

Where it breaks: the desk never hands one patient another patient's chart, yet a response cache with a missing key field
does exactly that.

</Analogy>

## Quotas, breakers, and validation

**Quotas.** Each store gets a **token bucket** that refills at its quota rate. An empty bucket gets an instant HTTP 429
with a Retry-After header. The quotas (6, 4.5, and 4.5 req/s) add up to the 15 req/s limit.

**Breaker.** After three errors in a row, a **circuit breaker** sends requests to a **fallback**, here Small. After a
cool down, one probe tries Medium again, like a load balancer's [health check](../../scaling/load-balancing/).

**Validation.** The gateway checks each reply's schema and business rules. A rejected reply gets one retry, counted
against the quota, then a handoff to a person.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: quota rules, breaker states, and validation steps</summary>

**Quota rules.**

- **Shed early.** A rejected request costs the gateway almost nothing. A forwarded one costs a model call that the shared
  limit may reject anyway, or worse, one that succeeds and starves another store.
- **Allow short bursts.** Each bucket holds a couple of seconds of burst. A bucket token is a permit, unrelated to model
  tokens.
- **Count what is scarce.** A long prompt costs far more model work than a short one, so production gateways often meter
  tokens per minute as well as requests per second.
- **Split batch from live.** Give backfills and other batch work their own lower quota, so they cannot crowd out a
  customer waiting in the chat.
- **Never oversell.** Quotas that add up to more than the upstream limit bring the noisy tenant back at the next busy
  hour.
- **Retries count.** A validator retry spends the store's quota, so a store whose requests keep failing cannot hide
  extra load.

**Breaker states.** The breaker is a small state machine in the gateway:

| State | What the gateway does | It moves on when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closed | Sends requests to Medium | Three errors in a row open it |
| Open | Sends new requests to Small without trying Medium | The cool down (3 s in the widget) ends |
| Half open | Lets one probe request try Medium | The probe succeeds (closed) or fails (open again) |

Without a breaker, every request during an outage waits for Medium to fail before it falls back. With one, the gateway
stops calling a model it knows is failing, and the probe finds out when the model recovers. In the outage scenario the
breaker opens three times: two probes land inside the outage and fail, and the third one closes it.

**Fallback is degraded service.** A fallback is the next best answer: a smaller model, a cached answer, or a handoff to
a person. In the cascade toy below, Small passes hard questions only 35% of the time, against 85% for Medium. The chat
can fall back to Small, while the refund form should hand off to a person, since a wrong amount refunds the wrong money.

**Validation steps.**

1. **Parse and check the schema.** The reply must be valid JSON with every required field, each of the right type.
2. **Check business rules.** The amount must be positive and at most the order total, and the order must belong to this
   customer. A schema cannot express these.
3. **Retry once, then fall back.** A rejected reply gets one more attempt, often with the validation error added to the
   prompt. If that attempt fails too, the customer gets the handoff.

**Retry storms.** If the client, the gateway, and the feature each retry three times, one bad request can become 27
model calls, just when the model is struggling. One retry, counted against the store's quota, caps the extra work.

**The widget's last column** checks that every request ends in exactly one outcome or is still in flight.

</details>

## Routing versus a cascade

Can Small and Medium together beat Medium alone on cost and still meet the budgets?

- **Routing** decides before the call: a classifier sends easy questions to Small and hard ones to Medium. A wrong
  guess is a **misroute**.
- **A cascade** decides after: Small answers, and a checker accepts it or **escalates** to Medium, paying for both
  calls and waiting for both.

Both face 60 seeded questions and the last lesson's budgets: at least 85% pass, a p95 of 2 s or less, and at most
6,000 units per 1,000 requests.

<GatewayCascadeWidget client:visible />

What to look for on the mostly easy mix:

- **The cascade wins on quality and cost.** It passes 59 of 60 (98.3%) and spends about 1,748 units per 1,000 requests,
  about 55% less than Medium alone at 3,862.
- **The cascade misses the latency budget.** Ten of 60 requests escalate (about 17%). With more than 5% waiting for
  two calls, one of them sets the p95: 2.31 s against 2 s.
- **Routing fits every budget.** It passes 53 of 60 (88.3%) with a p95 of 1.6 s and about 1,905 units. The easy mix
  absorbs its 8 misroutes.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: picking a strategy, toy limits, and FrugalGPT</summary>

**Picking a strategy.** Pick the strategy from the traffic you actually get:

- **Routing** suits a tight latency budget and questions whose difficulty shows in the text. Each request pays for one
  model call plus the classifier.
- **A cascade** suits a mostly easy mix, some slack in the latency budget, and a checker you can trust.
- **One model** suits a hard mix, or budgets with no slack. It is also the simplest to run and to debug.

**Toy limits.** Pass chances, the classifier, and the checker are toy numbers, and latency and cost are illustrative
units. The widget checks quality, p95, and cost, and leaves out the last lesson's must decline budget. A real cascade
needs a checker that catches every leaked address from Small, since one miss is a privacy incident. That argues for
routing sensitive question types straight to the stronger model.

**FrugalGPT.** Research agrees that the savings are real and depend on the mix. FrugalGPT (arXiv 2305.05176) chains
models from cheap to expensive and learns when to stop. It reports up to 98% lower cost while matching the best single
model on one dataset, and savings of 59% to 73% on others. It also needs labeled data and a trained scorer to decide
when to accept an answer. Routing has the same need: the classifier is a model too, and it learns from labeled examples
of easy and hard questions.

</details>

## Two caches

A gateway can reuse the answer, and a model server can reuse the work of reading the prompt.

### Response cache: reuse the answer

A **response cache** returns a stored answer with no model call, like any [cache](../../scaling/caching/). Two
requests may share an answer only when every field that shapes it matches:

| Key field | Why it must be there |
| --- | --- |
| Store (tenant) | Store A allows returns for 30 days, and Store B for 14 |
| User role | Agents may see internal notes |
| Prompt version | A new prompt changes the answer |
| Index version | A reindex changes the answer |
| Model | The fallback's answer differs from Medium's |
| Settings | Temperature and format change the reply |
| Earlier turns | A follow up depends on the first question |
| The question | Exact text, with case and spacing normalized |

### Prefix (KV) cache: reuse the prompt work

Before answering, a model computes keys and values (KV) for every prompt token: the **prefill**. **Decode** then writes
one output token per step. A **prefix cache** in the model server stores prefill work in fixed-size blocks and reuses it
when a prompt starts with exactly the same tokens. It cuts only time to first token, so put stable text first.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: the cache widget, answers versus prompt work</summary>

**Key details.** The user role keeps internal notes away from customers, who must never see them. The model field
keeps the fallback's short answer from standing in for Medium's. A follow up such as "And for sale items?" depends on
the first question. Bumping the index version on every reindex or policy edit retires all older entries at once.

**Semantic cache risk.** A **semantic cache** matches questions by meaning. "How long do I have to return shoes?" and
"How long do I have to return a sale item?" look alike, and they can have different answers. A fixed similarity
threshold gives no guarantee that a hit is correct, so use one only where a near miss is harmless, and keep every other
key field exact.

**Prefix cache rules.** Its rules differ from a response cache:

- **It still generates a fresh answer.** It skips repeated prompt work and never returns a stored reply, so it cannot
  serve a stale or wrong answer.
- **It helps time to first token.** Time to first token is the queue wait plus the prefill, so skipping prefill cuts it.
  The time per output token comes mostly from decode, which does not change.
- **It needs an exact prefix.** Each block's hash includes the block before it, so a change near the start invalidates
  every later block. Put stable text first (system prompt, policy) and changing text last (timestamps, the question).
  Only full blocks are cached.
- **Reuse across stores is a choice.** A cache salt, such as one per store, keeps each store's blocks separate.

**The widget.** It shows both caches. In response mode, nine requests pass through the cache in order. Drop a field
from the key and watch which request gets someone else's answer. In prefix mode, one prompt is split into blocks of 16
tokens. Move the timestamp, change the store, or add a salt, and watch reuse and time to first token change.

<GatewayCacheWidget client:visible />

What to look for:

- **Every field in the key gives one safe hit.** Only the exact repeat at step 2 hits the cache.
- **Drop the store.** At step 3, a Store B customer asks about returns and gets Store A's 30 days. Store B allows 14
  days. The widget flags it as a leak.
- **Drop any other field.** Each one causes exactly one wrong hit: an agent only note shown to a customer, an answer
  from an old prompt, the fallback's short answer, or prose where the form needs JSON.
- **Prefix reuse.** A second request from the same store reuses 22 of 25 blocks and computes only 48 tokens, so time to
  first token falls from 0.35 s to 0.13 s. Put the timestamp at the start of the prompt and reuse drops to zero.
- **Salting.** Without a salt, a request from Store B reuses only the system prompt (10 blocks), since its policy
  differs. With a salt per store it reuses nothing, which trades speed for isolation.

The nine requests are **fixtures**, and the token counts and timings are toy numbers. Real block sizes and speedups
depend on the model server. [Serving models](../llm-serving/) covers prefill, decode, and KV cache memory in depth.
[Retrieval](../retrieval/), the next lesson, adds the store's own documents to the prompt, which makes the cache key and
the tenant scoping matter even more.

</details>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Choosing gateway policies">

| Choice | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Routing | One call per request, steady latency | A classifier to train, and misroutes |
| Cascade | Big savings on an easy mix | Escalations pay twice and stretch the p95 |
| One model | Simple, predictable quality and latency | The big model's price on easy questions |
| Response cache | No model call on a hit | Wrong answers if the key misses a field |
| Quota per tenant | One store cannot starve the others | The bursting store gets 429s |

</TradeoffTable>

Skip the gateway when one feature calls one model for one tenant and nothing parses the reply.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: more policy trade-offs</summary>

| Choice | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cascade checker | Catches weak answers from Small | It must be reliable, or bad answers slip through |
| Response cache freshness | Cheap repeat answers | Stale answers after a prompt or policy change |
| Prefix (KV) cache | Faster time to first token on shared prompts | Needs exact, stable prefixes, and saves prefill only |
| Quota tuning | Fair shares per store | Quotas need tuning as traffic grows |
| Strict validation | Broken output never reaches the form | More retries and more handoffs |
| Lenient validation (repair near misses) | Fewer retries | Can accept a reply that parses but is wrong |
| Fallback to a smaller tier | Keeps answering during an outage | Lower quality while it runs |

A gateway pays off once several features or tenants share a model, or once a reply must fit a schema, since validation
needs one place to run.

</details>

## Common mistakes

- **Quotas that add up to more than the upstream limit.** The noisy tenant returns at the next busy hour.
- **A cache key without the store.** A Store B customer gets Store A's return window.
- **Retries at every layer.** Three layers retrying three times turn one bad request into 27 calls.
- **A timestamp at the start of the prompt.** Prefix reuse drops to zero.

<KeyTakeaways>

- A gateway is the one place for quotas, caching, routing, validation, and fallback.
- Quotas per tenant, summed to the upstream limit, keep one store's burst from failing the others.
- Routing keeps latency steady, a cascade saves most on an easy mix, and a hard mix may need one strong model.
- A response cache key needs every field that shapes the answer. A prefix cache reuses only prompt work.
- Validate output, retry once, and guard every fallback with a circuit breaker.
