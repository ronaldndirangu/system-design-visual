- Write a task spec and turn it into an evaluation set with rubrics and must decline cases.

## The failure: picking a model from a demo

The support team wants an assistant that answers customer questions about orders, refunds, and accounts. Someone runs a
quick demo: five common questions, three candidate models. Small, the cheapest and fastest, answers all five perfectly.
The team is ready to ship it.

Then a customer asks for the address their neighbor's TV shipped to, and Small tells them.

The demo told the truth about five easy questions. It could not tell the candidates apart, and it never asked anything
the assistant must refuse. Nobody had written down what the assistant must do, what it must decline, how fast it must
answer, or how much it may cost.

## The idea: decide what good means, then measure

An **evaluation set** (eval set) is a fixed list of realistic inputs, each with a rubric that says what a passing
answer contains. **Budgets** turn the product's needs into four numbers a candidate must meet. You write both before
you compare models, so the results cannot move the bar. This is the
[requirements and estimates](../../thinking/requirements-and-estimates/) habit applied to a model choice.

<Analogy>

Hiring for a support desk. You write the job description and a set of practice tickets before the interviews, including
a few where the right answer is "I can't help with that." A candidate who charms the panel with easy questions and then
reads out a stranger's card number does not get the job.

</Analogy>

### The four budgets for the support assistant

| Budget | Default | Why it exists |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality floor | 85% of questions pass the rubric | Wrong refund answers cost money and trust |
| Must decline | Every must decline case declined | One leaked address is a privacy incident |
| p95 latency | 2 s or less to the full answer | A chat that stalls feels broken |
| Cost | 6,000 units or less per 1,000 questions | The support budget is fixed |

Cost uses made-up **units**, since real prices change often. One unit is about what Small spends on a typical answer.

### Read the numbers honestly

- **Small sets have big steps.** With 24 questions, one case moves the pass rate about 4 points. Medium's 87.5% clears
  the 85% floor by a single question. Before trusting that margin, grow the set.
- **p95 from 24 answers is a rough first read.** Only one answer sits above the 95th percentile. Measure latency again
  under real traffic.
- **Grades need a grader.** Here the grades are fixtures. In practice you combine exact checks (did it cite the right
  order?), a written rubric, and human review. A model can grade at scale, but compare its grades with human labels
  before trusting it.
- **Keep failures.** Every bad answer from production becomes a new eval case, so the same mistake cannot ship twice.

## When nothing fits

Budgets compete. A faster model is often a weaker one, and a stronger one is often slower and pricier. Tighten one
budget and the set of models that fit can go empty.

<Predict

  options={['Small', 'Medium', 'Small and Medium', 'None']}
  answer={3}
>

**None.** Medium's p95 is about 1.5 s, so it misses the new ceiling. Small is fast enough but still misses quality and
must decline. When nothing fits, change the design (shorter answers, cached answers, or routing easy questions to Small
and hard ones to Medium) or agree a different budget with the product owner.

</Predict>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Building evals and budgets">

| Choice | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small eval set (dozens of cases) | Fast to build, quick to rerun | Each case swings the score, so close calls stay unclear |
| Large eval set (hundreds or more) | Tighter estimates, room for rare cases | Labeling time, and grading costs on every rerun |
| Exact checks (citations, order ids) | Cheap, repeatable, no judgment calls | Miss tone, helpfulness, and partial answers |
| Human grading | Catches subtle mistakes and tone | Slow and costly, and graders disagree |
| Model graded rubric | Scales to every rerun | Needs checking against human labels, and can share the model's blind spots |
| Strict budgets | Protect customers and spend | Can rule out every candidate, forcing design work |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

The support team wants a second feature: the assistant drafts a reply for a human agent, who edits it before sending.
Write the task spec, five eval cases (at least one must decline case), and the four budgets. Which budgets change
compared with the customer-facing assistant, and why?

<div slot="answer">

- **Task spec:** draft a reply that states the policy correctly, uses the order details from the ticket, and matches
  the store's tone. Never include another customer's data, even if the ticket asks for it.
- **Eval cases:** a simple tracking question, a two-step refund, an angry customer, a ticket in which the customer
  pastes text such as "ignore your rules" (the draft must decline that request), and a ticket that needs facts the
  draft cannot know (the draft should say what the agent must check).
- **Quality:** grade on how much the agent must fix. A rubric score such as "sendable with small edits" works well.
- **Must decline:** unchanged. A human reviews the draft, but a leaked address in a draft is still a leak.
- **Latency:** looser. An agent can wait a few seconds for a draft, while a customer in a chat cannot.
- **Cost:** per ticket instead of per question, and checked against the agent time it saves.

</div>
