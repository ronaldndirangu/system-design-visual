## The failure: picking a model from a demo

The support team wants an assistant that answers customer questions about orders, refunds, and accounts. Someone runs a
quick demo: five common questions, three candidate models. Small, the cheapest and fastest, answers all five perfectly,
so the team plans to ship it.

Then a customer asks for the address their neighbor's TV shipped to, and Small tells them.

The demo never asked anything the assistant must refuse. Nobody had written down what the assistant must do, what it
must decline, how fast it must answer, or how much it may cost.

## The idea: decide what good means, then measure

An **evaluation set** (eval set) is a fixed list of realistic inputs, each with a rubric that says what a passing
answer contains. **Budgets** turn the product's needs into four numbers a candidate must meet. You write both before
you compare models, so the results cannot move the bar. This is the
[requirements and estimates](../../thinking/requirements-and-estimates/) habit applied to a model choice.

<StepDiagram client:visible title="From task to ship decision" nodes={nodes} edges={edges} steps={steps} height={300} />

<Analogy>

Hiring for a support desk. You write the job description and a set of practice tickets before the interviews, including
a few where the right answer is "I can't help with that." A candidate who charms the panel with easy questions and then
reads out a stranger's card number does not get the job.

Where it breaks: you hire a person once, while a model changes with every prompt edit or provider update, so you rerun
the interview each time.

</Analogy>

### The four budgets for the support assistant

| Budget | Default | Why it exists |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality floor | 85% of questions pass the rubric | Wrong refund answers cost money and trust |
| Must decline | Every must decline case declined | One leaked address is a privacy incident |
| p95 latency | 2 s or less to the full answer | A chat that stalls feels broken |
| Cost | 6,000 units or less per 1,000 questions | The support budget is fixed |

Cost uses made-up **units**, since real prices change often. One unit is what Small spends on a typical answer: a
400 token prompt plus a 75 token reply. Providers price the two sides apart, so the model does too. Each output token
costs as much as 4 input tokens, which makes long answers expensive, and a prompt token served from the provider's
prompt cache costs a tenth of a fresh one. Each model multiplies both prices by its own ratio: Medium pays 4 times
Small's price per token and Large pays 15 times.

## Grading at scale: LLM judges

Real evals use exact checks for facts such as an order id, plus a grader for open answers. People grade well but
slowly. An **LLM judge** is a second model that reads the question, the rubric, and the answer, and returns a grade,
cheaply enough to rerun on every change.

Judges carry known biases ([Zheng et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)):

- **Position bias:** shown two answers, the judge favors one slot, often the first.
- **Verbosity bias:** it rates longer answers higher even when the extra words add nothing.
- **Self-preference:** it rates answers from its own model higher.

So the judge needs its own eval. Grade a sample of answers by hand, run the judge on the same sample, and report how
often the two agree before you trust the judge.

- **Pointwise** judging scores one answer against the rubric. Budgets need it, since a quality floor is an absolute bar.
- **Pairwise** judging picks the better of two answers to one question. It spots small differences that scores miss,
  so it fits a close call between two prompt versions, yet it never says whether either answer clears the floor.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: calibrating a judge and paying for it</summary>

**Calibrate before you trust.** A workable procedure:

1. Sample answers from every case type, including must decline cases. Teams typically start with 50 to 100 answers.
2. Have two people grade the sample against the rubric and settle their disagreements. Their own agreement rate is the
   most you can expect from the judge.
3. Run the judge on the same answers. Report agreement with the human labels overall and per case type. A judge that
   matches people on easy refunds and misses leaked addresses is useless for the must decline budget.
4. Read every disagreement, then fix the rubric wording or the judge prompt. Measure again on fresh answers, so you do
   not tune the judge to one sample.
5. Recalibrate whenever the judge model or the rubric changes, and spot check a few graded answers on every run.

**Swap the order to cancel position bias.** For pairwise judging, ask twice with the answers in both orders. Count a
win only when the judge picks the same answer both times, and call a flip a tie. In the MT-Bench study, GPT-4 kept its
verdict after a swap only about 65% of the time, so a single ordering can hide a coin flip. Wang et al.
([2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17926)) showed that order alone let Vicuna-13B beat ChatGPT on 66 of 80 queries in
their test, and proposed balanced position calibration (averaging both orders) plus human review for close calls.

**Typical agreement.** In the same study, GPT-4 as a judge matched human preferences over 80% of the time, about as
often as two people matched each other. That figure comes from one benchmark; measure your own judge on your own
rubric.

**The judge spends money too.** Every judged answer is another model call whose prompt holds the question, the rubric,
and the answer, so it often runs longer than the original prompt. A pointwise judge adds one call per answer. A swapped
pairwise judge adds two calls per pair. Few-shot judge prompts (with graded examples) made the judge more consistent in
the MT-Bench study and made each call about 4 times as expensive. With 24 questions and three candidates, a pointwise
judge makes 72 calls per run, and the team reruns the set on every prompt change. Track judge spend against the eval
budget, apart from the production cost budget, and switch to a cheaper judge only after it passes the same calibration.

</details>

## When nothing fits

Budgets compete. A faster model is often a weaker one, and a stronger one is often slower and pricier. Tighten one
budget and the set of models that fit can go empty.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Building evals and budgets">

| Choice | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large eval set (hundreds or more) | Tighter estimates, room for rare cases | Labeling time, and grading costs on every rerun |
| Exact checks (citations, order ids) | Cheap, repeatable, no judgment calls | Miss tone, helpfulness, and partial answers |
| Human grading | Catches subtle mistakes and tone | Slow and costly, and graders disagree |
| LLM judge | Grades open answers on every rerun | Position, length, and self-preference bias, so it needs calibration |
| Strict budgets | Protect customers and spend | Can rule out every candidate, forcing design work |

</TradeoffTable>

Skip a full eval set and written budgets when the feature is a throwaway prototype that no customer sees, or when a
deterministic check already decides every answer.

## Common mistakes

- **Setting budgets after the results.** The bar drifts toward a favorite. Write the four numbers first.
- **Trusting an uncalibrated judge.** A biased judge can rank the wrong candidate first. Report its agreement with
  human labels before you use its grades.
- **Dropping must decline cases to make a model fit.** That is the Lowered bar trap: the cheap model fits only because
  the customer protection is gone.

<KeyTakeaways>

- A demo cannot separate candidates. An eval set with hard and must decline cases can.
- Write the quality, must decline, p95 latency, and cost budgets before any run, then ship the cheapest candidate that
  meets all four.
- When nothing fits, change the design (routing, caching, shorter answers) or renegotiate the budget in writing.
- An LLM judge scales grading. Calibrate it against human labels, and swap the answer order in pairwise runs.

</KeyTakeaways>
