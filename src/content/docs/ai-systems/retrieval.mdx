## The failure: a confident answer with no source

Two stores share the support assistant: Harbor Bikes and Pine Street Books. A Harbor customer asks, "Can I return a
sale item?" The model has never seen Harbor's policies, so it invents an answer:

> **Fixture answer:** Yes! Sale items can be returned within 60 days for a full refund.

Harbor's final sale items cannot be returned. So the team adds retrieval, which puts relevant policy text in the prompt,
and new failures appear. "Can I send back cycling shoes I never wore?" shares no word with the return policy. "Can I use
code TRAIL25?" means little beyond "discount". And with one index for both stores, Pine's return rule can answer a
Harbor customer.

## The idea: two searches, one ranking

Keyword and vector search fail on different questions, so a hybrid system runs both and merges the results. Their
scores sit on different scales (a keyword score of 4.2, a cosine of 0.93), so adding them lets one drown out the other.

**Reciprocal rank fusion** (RRF) uses only ranks: a chunk at rank r earns 1/(k + r) from each list, with k usually 60.
For Harbor's sale item question, both searches rank the return rule first: 1/61 + 1/61, about 0.0328. The gift card
chunk ranks second for keywords only: 1/62, about 0.0161. Agreement beats one strong placement.

### Rerank and rewrite

Two optional stages sharpen this pipeline. A **cross-encoder reranker** runs after fusion: a model reads the question
and each passage together and rescores the top 20 to 100 candidates. It is more precise than either first search, and
it typically costs tens of milliseconds per batch on a GPU, so it only runs on a short list. A **query rewrite** runs
before search: a model rewrites the question, or drafts a hypothetical answer (HyDE) that the system embeds. That adds
one model call of latency and cost, and the rewrite can drift from what the user asked. This lesson's widgets model
neither stage.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: cross-encoder rerank and HyDE</summary>

**Why rerank only a short list.** The first-stage searches compare the question with word lists and vectors computed
ahead of time, so they scale to millions of chunks. A cross-encoder (Nogueira and Cho, 2019, in Further reading) reads
the question and the passage as one input, so nothing can be computed ahead: every candidate costs a full model pass per
question. Run it on the top 20 to 100 fused candidates and keep the 3 it scores highest. Its score is also a stronger
evidence signal than RRF, which encodes rank only.

**Bi-encoder and cross-encoder.** The vector index uses a bi-encoder: the question and the chunk are embedded
separately, and similarity is a cheap dot product. The cross-encoder sees both texts at once, so it can notice that
"except final sale items" flips the answer. That attention across both texts buys the precision, and it is also why
the cost grows with every candidate.

**Query rewrite.** A rewrite can turn "and for sale items?" into a standalone question using the earlier turns, expand
abbreviations, or split a two-part question into two searches. HyDE (Gao et al., 2022) asks a model to draft a plausible
answer and embeds that draft, because an answer sits closer to answer-shaped chunks than a short question does. The
draft may contain invented facts. Only its embedding drives the search, and the final answer still comes from the
retrieved chunks.

**The costs.** A rewrite adds one model call before search starts, so it adds directly to time to first token and
spends tokens on every question. It can also drift: it may add a product, a store, or a constraint the user never
mentioned. Log the rewritten query next to the original, evaluate retrieval with and without it, and apply the store and
permission filter from the original request, never from anything the rewrite produced.

</details>

## Chunking: keep the rule with its exception

Retrieval can only send what a chunk holds. Harbor's 45-word return policy, split every 16 words with no overlap, breaks
after "within 30 days of", and "except final sale items" lands in the next chunk. The first chunk matches best, so the
model answers "Yes" from a true sentence cut short.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: try chunk sizes and overlap</summary>

<ChunkingWidget client:visible />

What to look for:

- **16 words, no overlap is wrong.** The sent chunk has the 30-day rule and no exception, so the fixture answer says
  yes. A true sentence, cut short, produces a wrong answer.
- **Overlap costs storage.** 16 words with overlap 8 makes 5 chunks where no overlap makes 3. Every repeated word is
  indexed and embedded twice.
- **Big chunks blur topics.** 24 words makes 2 chunks and keeps the clauses together. Every setting from 24 words up
  does. On a longer document, chunks this size mix several topics, which dilutes each embedding and fills the prompt
  with words the question did not need.

A rule and its exception usually share a sentence, so a splitter that respects sentences keeps them together without
guessing a size. Fixed windows with overlap are the fallback for text without structure. Whatever you choose, the
chunking only matters through the answers it produces.

</details>

## Tenant scoping: filter before you rank

Every chunk carries its store and who may read it. With no filter, Pine's return rule gets cited for a Harbor customer.
A post-filter drops unreadable chunks after the top 3: nothing leaks, yet the empty slots can force a needless abstain.
A **pre-filter** limits both searches to readable chunks before ranking, so every slot counts.

Enforce scope in the search, since a prompt instruction is a request the model can ignore. Treat retrieved chunks as
data, never instructions: indirect prompt injection arrives through retrieved documents, and
[Agents and tools](../agents/) builds that boundary. A large store with its own index is
[sharding](../../scaling/sharding/) by tenant.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: the three filter modes</summary>

- **No filter.** Search every chunk. For the sale item question, Pine's return rule ranks second and is cited for a
  Harbor customer. Ask Harbor "Do you match a lower price from another shop?" and the fixture answer comes from Pine's
  price match policy, a promise Harbor never made.
- **Post-filter.** Search every chunk, take the top 3, then drop the ones this customer may not read. Nothing leaks, and
  the dropped chunks leave empty slots. For the price match question both Pine chunks drop and one chunk remains. For
  the paraphrase only the back order note remains, and the assistant abstains although Harbor's return rule answers it.
- **Pre-filter.** Limit both searches to chunks this customer may read, then rank. All three slots go to readable
  chunks. The paraphrase finds the return rule, and the price match question abstains correctly because Harbor has no
  such policy.

When one store grows large, its own index turns the filter into a routing decision.

</details>

### Caches need the same scope

A cache in front of retrieval or answers must respect the same boundaries. Key the answer cache on the question text
alone, and Pine's cached answer to "Can I return a sale item?" can reach a Harbor customer. An exact cache key must
include every input that changes the answer: the store, the user's permission scope, the model, the prompt version,
the sampling settings, the index (corpus) version, and the earlier turns in the conversation. A reindex bumps the corpus
version, so every answer built on the old chunks stops matching. That bump is the invalidation step. The earlier turns
keep "and for sale items?" from reusing an answer to a different first question. The hybrid search widget shows the key
it would use, with the store and scope first.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: semantic caches</summary>

A semantic cache, which reuses an answer for a question that looks similar, adds a second risk. Two questions can look
alike and need different answers, and no fixed similarity threshold guarantees that a reused answer is right. The
general rules for keys, freshness, and invalidation are in [caching](../../scaling/caching/), and
[the AI gateway](../gateway/) shows where an answer cache sits and what its key must hold.

</details>

## Citations and abstention

Each chunk goes to the model with its id, and the answer cites the ids it used, so a reader or a check can verify every
claim. The RRF score is no evidence: a chunk topping both lists scores 2/61 whether it answers or not. The toy model
checks a separate **support** score, and below the threshold the assistant abstains: "I could not find this in the
store policy. Want me to connect you with a person?"

<RetrievalAbstainWidget client:visible />

On 12 fixture questions, 4 of them unanswerable, a 0.50 bar gets 4 wrong, the default 0.80 gets 1 wrong, and 0.85 gets
none while abstaining on one answerable question. Pick the bar with an eval set, as in
[evaluation and budgets](../evaluation-and-budgets/), and count both kinds of miss.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: the support score and the counts</summary>

The toy **support** score is the larger of the cosine similarity and the share of the question's keyword weight that the
chunk contains. Real systems often use a reranking model, such as the cross-encoder above, or a check that asks whether
the chunk supports the answer. In this lesson a sent chunk counts as a citation only when it clears the threshold.

- **At 0.50** the assistant answers everything: 8 correct and 4 wrong, one wrong answer for every question the policies
  cannot answer.
- **At 0.80**, the default, it gets 7 correct and 1 wrong, and abstains on 4, three of them correctly. "Can I pay in
  installments?" is still answered from the gift card chunk, which mentions paying for orders (support 0.85). "Is gift
  wrap free all year?" is abstained although Pine's gift wrap chunk answers it: free only in December.
- **At 0.85** the wrong answers reach 0. Correct answers drop to 6 and abstentions rise to 6, because the back ordered
  brake pads question now falls below the bar too.

A wrong refund answer costs money and trust, and a handoff costs a person's time. All of these counts come from the
fixture and are illustrative.

</details>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Building retrieval">

| Choice | What it buys | What it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small chunks | Precise matches, short prompts | Rules split from exceptions |
| Hybrid with RRF | Both kinds of match, no weights | Two indexes, two searches |
| Cross-encoder rerank | Precision on the top 20 to 100 | Tens of milliseconds per GPU batch |
| Query rewrite or HyDE | Matches for vague and follow-up questions | One more model call, and drift |
| Pre-filter by store and role | No leaks, full slots | Labels on every chunk |
| High abstain threshold | Fewer wrong answers | More handoffs |

</TradeoffTable>

Skip retrieval when the facts are few and stable enough to fit in the prompt.

## Common mistakes

- **Scoping tenants in the prompt.** Filter in the search.
- **Fixed windows with no overlap.** The exception lands in the next chunk.
- **Treating RRF as evidence.** Abstain on a separate support check.
- **Keying the cache on the question alone.** Include every input that changes the answer.
- **Reranking the whole corpus.** Give the cross-encoder a short list.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Keyword search finds exact tokens and vector search finds meaning. Fuse their ranks with RRF.
- A reranker sharpens a short list and a query rewrite helps vague questions. Each adds latency and cost.
- Chunk on structure so a rule keeps its exception.
- Filter by store and role before ranking, and key caches on the same scope.
- Cite chunk ids, and abstain below an evidence bar chosen with an eval set.

</KeyTakeaways>
