## The failure: the clock runs out on the data model

You have 45 minutes to design a news feed. Requirements take 10 minutes because every edge case feels important. The
estimates take another 10 while you chase exact storage figures. The API takes 5. Then you sink 20 minutes into the data
model, polishing table columns. The time is up, and the board has no read path, no write path, no deep dive, and no
trade-offs.

A budget per step. Working faster helps a little, yet most of the loss came from a few steps taking far more than their
share. With budgets, the requirements, the estimates, and the data model would each have stopped near 5 minutes, with
a note such as "follower list: check size later". That leaves 21 minutes for the high-level design and the deep dive,
and 5 for the trade-offs. Skipping requirements trades one failure for another: boxes with no numbers behind them.

</Predict>

## How a round works

The round uses the seven steps from [the design checklist](../../thinking/design-checklist/). Each step gets a slice
of the clock, weighted toward the high-level design and the deep dive.

<Steps>

1. **Pick a prompt and a pace.** Four prompts revisit systems this course builds, and ten are new to you. Each chip
   names the module to finish first. The 20 minute drill builds the habit of moving on; the 45 minute interview pace
   matches a common interview slot.

2. **Get paper, then press Start round.** The step clock runs while you work. The checklist rail shows the time used
   on every step, and each step lists the questions to ask yourself.

3. **Move on when the budget runs out.** The bar turns hatched and labeled **Over**. Write the open question down and
   press **Next step**. The budgets are a suggested split, so a few seconds over is fine. Minutes over is the habit to
   break.

4. **Finish, or let the clock stop you.** With **Stop when time runs out** on, the round ends at the total budget. Turn
   it off to see how far over you would run.

5. **Score yourself, then read the outline.** Rate each step Missing, Partial, or Solid against its one line rubric.
   Then open the reference outline and compare.

6. **Save the round if you like.** Saved rounds stay in this browser with your score for every step, so you can see
   whether your time and scores improve. Each saved round names its weakest steps, the ones to drill next.

</Steps>

<Analogy>

A chess clock leaves the thinking to the player. Its job is to show when one move is eating the time the endgame
needs. The step budget plays the same role: when it runs out, write the open question down and move on, so the
endgame (the deep dive and the trade-offs) still gets played.

</Analogy>

## Reading your round

- **A step marked Not reached** is the clearest signal. Look at the step before it: that is usually where the time
  went.
- **Over on the early steps** (requirements, estimates, data model) usually means chasing precision. Rough answers
  with a stated assumption are enough.
- **Low scores on the deep dive** usually mean the estimates never pointed at a risk. Ask which number grows fastest,
  then dig into the part that number breaks.
- **Low scores on trade-offs** usually mean each choice lacks its alternative. For every box, name what you passed over
  and what your choice costs.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="How to set up a practice round">

| Choice                               | What it buys                                                  | What it costs                                          |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| 20 minute drill                      | More rounds per week, and a strong habit of moving on          | A shallow deep dive                                    |
| 45 minute interview pace             | Room for real depth, close to a common interview slot          | Fewer rounds, and more time to drift on one step       |
| Stop when time runs out              | Honest practice of a hard limit                               | An unfinished step stays unfinished                    |
| Allow overtime                       | Shows how far over you ran and lets you finish the thought     | Hides the very habit the clock exists to break         |
| Score first, then read the outline   | An honest self-assessment                                     | A slower review                                        |
| Read the outline first               | A faster review                                               | Your score drifts toward the outline                   |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

Run a 20 minute drill on paper for a prompt outside the pool: **a ride-hailing app shows riders the drivers near
them.** Assume 1 million active drivers, each sending its location every 4 seconds. Use the drill budgets (2, 2, 2, 2,
5, 5, and 2 minutes) and score yourself with the same rubric before you open the answer.

<div slot="answer">

1. **Requirements:** drivers send locations; riders see drivers within about 2 km, updated every few seconds. A nearby
   lookup returns in under 200 ms. Trip history, pricing, and matching are out of scope.
2. **Estimates:** 1 million drivers every 4 seconds is 250,000 writes per second. The latest location per driver is
   about 50 bytes, so the raw payload is about 50 MB. Index entries and in-memory overhead bring that to a few hundred
   MB, still small enough for memory. Keeping every point would add about 1 TB a day.
3. **API:** `PUT /drivers/{id}/location` with latitude and longitude. `GET /drivers/nearby?lat=&lng=` returns a list of
   drivers with positions. Errors: unknown driver, and location out of range.
4. **Data model:** the latest location keyed by driver ID, plus a grid index that maps each map cell to the drivers in
   it. The hot query is "drivers in these few cells". The cells cover a rough square, so the server then computes each
   candidate's exact distance and drops any driver beyond 2 km.
5. **High-level design:** drivers send updates through a load balancer to location servers, which update an in-memory
   grid index split by city. Riders query the same servers by cell. A stream copies updates to cheap storage for
   later analysis.
6. **Deep dive:** the write rate is the risk. Keeping only the latest point in memory avoids 250,000 disk writes per
   second. Splitting the index by city spreads the load; a dense city center may need its own server.
7. **Trade-offs:** in memory locations vanish on a crash, which is acceptable because every driver resends within 4
   seconds. Updating every 4 seconds instead of every 10 costs 2.5 times the writes for fresher positions.

</div>

</Challenge>
