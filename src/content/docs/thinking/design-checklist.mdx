Every design in this course, and most design interviews, walks the same seven steps. Each step answers one question.
Under each step, the URL shortener from [requirements and estimates](../requirements-and-estimates/) shows a worked
answer.

<Objectives>

- Name the seven steps of a design and the question each one answers.
- Start a new design from requirements and numbers before naming any tool.
- Choose where to deep dive from what your estimates say breaks first.
- Explain why a new fact sends a design back to an earlier step.

</Objectives>

<Steps>

1. **Requirements: what must it do, and how well?** List the features, the quality bars (latency, uptime, durability),
   and what you will leave out.

   _URL shortener:_ create a short link and redirect anyone who opens it. Redirects under 100 ms, 99.9% uptime.
   Analytics stay out of scope.

2. **Estimates: how big is it?** Work out peak requests per second, storage over the system's life, and the read to
   write ratio.

   _URL shortener:_ 1 million new links a day is about 12 writes per second. At 100 clicks per link, reads dominate
   at about 1,160 a second. Five years of links hold about 913 GB of raw data, or about 3.56 TB of disk with 3 copies
   and indexes.

3. **API: how do callers talk to it?** Name the endpoints, their inputs and outputs, and the errors they return.

   _URL shortener:_ `POST /links` takes a long URL and returns a code. `GET /{code}` answers with a redirect.

4. **Data model: what do you store, and how do you read it?** Name the entities, their keys, and the queries that hit
   them most.

   _URL shortener:_ one table keyed by the short code. Every read is a lookup by that key.
   [Data modeling](../../foundations/data-modeling/) covers how to pick keys from the queries.

5. **High-level design: which boxes, and how does a request flow?** Draw the read path and the write path separately.

   _URL shortener:_ browser, load balancer, app servers, cache, and database. These are the boxes from the
   [anatomy of a web app](../anatomy-of-a-web-app/).

6. **Deep dive: where does it break first?** Take the riskiest part your estimates expose and design it in detail.

   _URL shortener:_ a viral link floods one key, and the table outgrows one machine. [Caching](../../scaling/caching/)
   and [sharding](../../scaling/sharding/) answer those.

7. **Trade-offs: what did you give up, and why?** Name the alternatives you rejected and the cost of the one you chose.

   _URL shortener:_ a `301` redirect lets browsers cache it, so repeat clicks never reach you and click counts go
   missing. A `302` keeps every click visible at the cost of more traffic.

</Steps>

## It is a loop

The steps run in order the first time, then loop. A deep dive often sends you back: a hot key changes your estimates, and
a new query changes the data model. Revisiting an earlier step means the design is working.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Requirements and estimates come first. They decide every later choice, including the database.
- The API and data model turn requirements into calls and keys before any boxes appear.
- Spend the deep dive where your numbers say the design breaks first.
- Every choice has a cost: a `301` saves traffic and hides repeat clicks.
- The steps loop. A new fact sends you back to the step it changes.

</KeyTakeaways>
