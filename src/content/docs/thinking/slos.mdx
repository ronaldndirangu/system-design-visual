## The failure: a green dashboard and angry users

The shortener's latency dashboard shows one line: the average redirect time, a steady 35 ms. Everyone agreed redirects
should feel fast, and 35 ms looks fast. Meanwhile support tickets pile up: links "hang for a moment" before they open.

Both are true. In the toy run below, during the first two seconds, 1 redirect in 100 takes 140 ms or longer, four times
the average. The average blends those slow redirects into thousands of quick ones, and they vanish from the chart.

The team writes a promise: **99% of redirects finish in under 100 ms.** Two engineers each have a fix ready, and both
ship at the 2 s mark.

## The idea: promise a percentile, then measure the tail

A **percentile** ranks every request by time. The **p50** (median) is the time half of the requests beat. The **p95**
is the time 95% beat, and the **p99** is the time 99% beat. The p99 describes the slowest 1 in 100, the requests the
average hides.

Three terms turn "fast enough" into something you can check:

- **SLI** (service level indicator): a measured ratio of good events. Here, redirects under 100 ms divided by all
  redirects.
- **SLO** (service level objective): a target for the SLI over a window, such as 99% over 30 days.
- **Error budget**: the bad events the SLO allows. A 99% SLO over 19,921 redirects allows 199 slow ones. A 99.9%
  availability SLO over 30 days allows 0.1% of the window's requests to fail. With steady traffic, that equals 43.2
  minutes of full downtime. Real traffic peaks, so an outage at the busiest hour burns more of the budget per minute.

**Burn rate** tells you how fast the budget goes: the share of bad events divided by the share allowed. At 1x the
budget lasts exactly the window. At 10x a 30 day budget is gone in 3 days, which is worth waking someone up for. When the
budget runs out, the team pauses risky launches and spends its time on reliability. While budget is left, the team can
ship faster.

The tail matters more than its 1% suggests. One page often makes many requests. If a page makes 20 calls and waits for
all of them, and each call turns slow independently, about 18% of page loads hit at least one call slower than its
p99. Calls that share a slow dependency tend to turn slow together, so the real share lands between 1% and 18%.

<Analogy>

A pizza shop promises delivery in under 30 minutes for 99 of every 100 orders. The average delivery takes 18 minutes,
and that number means nothing to the family who waited an hour. So the shop counts late pizzas, and 1 late order in 100
is its allowance. While the allowance holds, the shop tries new recipes. Once it runs out, the shop stops experimenting
and finds out why the late pizzas were late: the slow oven, a far suburb, or one driver.

## Measure before you fix

- **Measure where users feel it.** The load balancer misses the user's own network. Timing in the browser (real user
  monitoring) catches it, and it is noisier.
- **Never average percentiles.** Merge the raw samples or histograms from every server, then read the percentile.
- **Collect enough samples.** A p99 needs hundreds of requests to mean anything. Over 100 requests it is nearly the
  single slowest one.
- **Trace the slowest requests too.** The average redirect and the slowest 1% often wait on different things. In the
  toy run the average points at app code and the tail points at the database.
- **Split the SLI along the access pattern.** In [Access patterns and hot keys](../access-patterns/), one hot cache node
  turned clicks away while the other three sat idle. A fleet-wide number dilutes that node's failures. A per node view
  shows them at once.

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Measuring and promising latency">

| Choice                               | What it buys                                          | What it costs                                              |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Track the average                    | One cheap number that moves with total load           | Hides the slow tail users complain about                   |
| Track the p50                        | Shows what a typical user gets                        | Says nothing about the slowest half                        |
| Track the p99                        | Follows the tail that busy pages hit often            | Needs many samples, and it is noisy at low traffic         |
| Track the max                        | Catches every outlier                                 | One freak request sets the number                          |
| Strict SLO, 99.99%                   | Users almost never see a slow or failed request       | About 4 minutes of budget a month slows every launch       |
| Loose SLO, 99%                       | Room to ship fast and run experiments                 | Users notice 1 bad request in 100                          |
| Freeze launches when the budget ends | A rule product and operations teams agree on upfront  | A freeze can block an urgent feature                       |

</TradeoffTable>

<Challenge>

A photo app's upload endpoint has an SLO: 99.5% of uploads finish within 2 s, measured over 30 days. It handles 2
million uploads a day.

1. How many slow or failed uploads does the error budget allow in 30 days?
2. On day 10 a bad deploy fails 20% of uploads for one hour. What is the burn rate during that hour, and how much of the
   budget does the hour spend?
3. The dashboard shows a mean upload time of 600 ms and a p99 of 1.8 s, yet mobile users complain. What do you measure
   next?

<div slot="answer">

1. **300,000.** 30 days of 2 million uploads is 60 million, and 0.5% of 60 million is 300,000.
2. **40x, about 6% of the budget.** 20% bad against 0.5% allowed is a burn rate of 40. An hour holds about 83,000
   uploads, so the deploy fails about 16,700 of them, close to 6% of 300,000. At 40x the whole month's budget would last
   18 hours.
3. **Measure closer to the user and split the tail.** Time uploads in the app itself, since the server never sees a slow
   mobile network. Then break the p99 down by network type, region, and app version, and trace the slowest uploads to
   see which stage they wait on.

</div>

</Challenge>
