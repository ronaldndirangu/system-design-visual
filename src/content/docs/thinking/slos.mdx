## The failure: a green dashboard and angry users

Each redirect first checks a **cache** (a small, fast copy of popular links held in memory; see
[Caching](../../scaling/caching/)). 95% of lookups find the link there, a 95% **hit rate** (the share of lookups the
cache answers). The other 5% miss and wait for a database read.

The latency dashboard shows a steady average redirect time of 35 ms, while support tickets say links "hang for a
moment". Both are true. In the toy run below, 1 redirect in 100 takes 140 ms or longer during the first two seconds.
The average blends those into thousands of quick ones.

The team writes a promise: **99% of redirects finish in under 100 ms.** Two engineers each have a fix ready, and both
ship at the 2 s mark.

## The idea: promise a percentile, then count against it

A **percentile** ranks every request by time. The **p50** (median) is the time half of the requests beat. The **p95**
is the time 95% beat, and the **p99** is the time 99% beat. The p99 describes the slowest 1 in 100, the requests the
average hides.

Three terms turn "fast enough" into something you can check:

- **SLI** (service level indicator): a measured ratio of good events. Here, redirects under 100 ms divided by all
  redirects, timed at the **load balancer** (the front door that spreads requests across servers; see
  [Load balancing](../../scaling/load-balancing/)).
- **SLO** (service level objective): a target for the SLI over a window, such as 99% over 30 days.
- **Error budget**: the bad events the SLO allows. A 99% SLO over 19,921 redirects allows 199 slow ones. A 99.9%
  SLO over 30 days with steady traffic allows 43.2 minutes of full downtime.

**Burn rate** tells you how fast the budget goes: the share of bad events divided by the share allowed. At 1x the
budget lasts exactly the window. At 10x a 30 day budget is gone in 3 days, which is worth waking someone up for. When
the budget runs out, the team pauses risky launches until reliability recovers.

<Analogy>

A pizza shop promises delivery in under 30 minutes for 99 of every 100 orders. An average of 18 minutes means nothing
to the family who waited an hour. So the shop counts late pizzas: 1 in 100 is its allowance. While it holds, the shop
tries new recipes. Once it runs out, experiments stop.

Where it breaks: a shop sees a few dozen orders a day, so its allowance drains slowly. A service sees thousands of
requests a second, and a bad deploy can spend a month's budget in hours.

</Analogy>

## Measuring it well

### Never average percentiles

Ten servers that each report a p99 cannot be averaged into a fleet p99: a quiet server counts as much as a busy one.
Merge the raw samples or histograms from every server, then read the percentile.

### Collect enough samples

A p99 needs hundreds of requests to mean anything. Over 100 requests it is nearly the single slowest one, so a p99 on
a quiet service jumps around.

### Split the SLI along the access pattern

In [Access patterns and hot keys](../access-patterns/), one hot cache node turned clicks away while the other three sat
idle. A fleet-wide SLI dilutes that node's failures across every request. An SLI per node, per region, or per endpoint
shows them at once.

<details>
<summary>Revisit after observability: tracing the slow tail</summary>

Come back here after [Observability](../../reliability/observability/), which covers traces, spans, and sampling.

**Trace the slowest requests too.** A trace records each stage of one request as a timed span. Before the fixes, the
average redirect spends 16 of its 35 ms in app code, while the slowest 1% spend 141 of their 171 ms in the database. The
average points at app code and the tail points at the database, so a p99 promise points at the database. Sample the
slowest requests on purpose, since a random 1 in 100 sample keeps few of them.

**Fan-out makes the tail common.** One page often makes many calls. If a page makes 20 calls in parallel and waits for
all of them, and each call turns slow 1% of the time independently, all 20 are fast with probability 0.99 to the 20th
power, about 0.82. So about 18% of page loads wait on at least one call slower than its p99. Calls that share a slow
dependency tend to turn slow together, so the real share lands between 1% and 18%.

**Measure where users feel it.** The load balancer misses the user's own network. Timing in the browser (real user
monitoring) catches it, and it is noisier.

</details>

## Trade-offs

<TradeoffTable title="Measuring and promising latency">

| Choice                               | What it buys                                          | What it costs                                              |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Track the average                    | One cheap number that moves with total load           | Hides the slow tail users complain about                   |
| Track the p99                        | Follows the tail that busy pages hit often            | Needs many samples, and it is noisy at low traffic         |
| Strict SLO, 99.99%                   | Users almost never see a slow or failed request       | About 4 minutes of budget a month slows every launch       |
| Loose SLO, 99%                       | Room to ship fast and run experiments                 | Users notice 1 bad request in 100                          |
| Freeze launches when the budget ends | A rule product and operations teams agree on upfront  | A freeze can block an urgent feature                       |

</TradeoffTable>

**When to skip a formal SLO.** A prototype or an internal tool with a handful of users needs only a p99 on a dashboard.
Write an SLO once people depend on the service and the team must choose between shipping and fixing.

## Common mistakes

- **Promising an average.** A 35 ms mean sat next to 1 redirect in 100 taking 140 ms or longer.
- **Averaging percentiles across servers.** Merge histograms, then read the percentile.
- **Reading a p99 from too few requests.** Over 100 requests it is just the slowest one.
- **Choosing a fix by the mean.** Faster app code gave the lowest mean and still spent the whole budget.
- **Measuring only fleet-wide.** One hot node can fail 1 request in 10 while the fleet SLI stays inside the SLO.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Promise a percentile. The p99 covers the slowest 1 in 100, the requests an average hides.
- An SLI counts good events, an SLO sets a target over a window, and the error budget is the bad events it allows.
- Burn rate is the bad share divided by the allowed share. At 10x a 30 day budget lasts 3 days.
- Judge a fix by the SLI it delivers. The fix that lowers the mean most can still miss the SLO.

</KeyTakeaways>

A photo app's upload endpoint has an SLO: 99.5% of uploads finish within 2 s, measured over 30 days. It handles 2
million uploads a day.

1. How many slow or failed uploads does the error budget allow in 30 days?
2. On day 10 a bad deploy fails 20% of uploads for one hour. What is the burn rate during that hour, and how much of the
   budget does the hour spend?
3. The dashboard shows a mean upload time of 600 ms and a p99 of 1.8 s, yet mobile users complain. What do you measure
   next?

A strong answer shows the budget arithmetic, states the burn rate as a multiple of the allowed share, and splits the SLI
before picking a fix.

<div slot="answer">

1. **300,000.** 30 days of 2 million uploads is 60 million, and 0.5% of 60 million is 300,000.
2. **40x, about 6% of the budget.** 20% bad against 0.5% allowed is a burn rate of 40. An hour holds about 83,000
   uploads, so the deploy fails about 16,700 of them, close to 6% of 300,000.
3. **Measure closer to the user and split the SLI.** Time uploads in the app itself, since the server never sees a slow
   mobile network. Then break the p99 down by network type, region, and app version to find the group that misses.

</div>
